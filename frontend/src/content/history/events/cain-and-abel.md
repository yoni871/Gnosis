## Overview

Genesis 4:1–16 tells the first births. Eve bears Cain, named for her claim of acquiring a man, and then Abel. Cain works the ground; Abel keeps sheep. Each brings an offering: Abel's with firstlings and fat, Cain's unmarked. God regards Abel and his offering, not Cain's. God warns the fallen-faced Cain: sin waits at the door; he must rule. Cain rises against Abel in the field and kills him, the text recording no words of his, then lies to God, denying he is his brother's keeper. Abel's blood cries from the ground. Cursed from the ground and condemned to wander, Cain fears every finder will kill him, though the text never says who, yet God protects him with a sign and sevenfold vengeance, and Cain settles in Nod, east of Eden. No date or place is independently established for the event. [^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-westermann-genesis]

## Ancient World

### The offerings: farmer and shepherd

Mesopotamian literature staged farmer against shepherd. In the Sumerian Debate between Grain and Sheep, in Old Babylonian manuscripts, two sisters dispute which sustains the land; Grain is declared the greater, the reverse of Genesis. The poem contests resources, with no offering, no rejection, no murder: an analogy of motifs, not evidence of dependence. Grain was never rejected: Mesopotamian gods were fed daily with bread, beer, fruit, and meat. Both offerings are minḥah, a general gift, not a technical grain offering. The text gives no reason for God's regard. Four explanations are proposed, all debated: the gift's quality, Abel's firstlings and fat the only foothold; Abel's faith, as later readers put it; the giver's heart; and the material, animal against vegetable. Westermann calls them modern intrusions. ### Sin at the door

Genesis 4:7 is a difficult verse. The Hebrew ḥaṭṭāʾt means both sin and sin offering; the lexicon reads it as sin, a crouching beast at the door, though a sin-offering reading has supporters and a doorstep demon has been proposed, one commentary calling it unlikely. Its second half repeats Genesis 3:16's desire-and-rule wording exactly, the word for desire occurring only three times in the Bible, and its masculine pronouns cannot refer to feminine sin, leaving whether the desire is Abel's or sin's unresolved. Whether the verse is corrupt or a later addition is debated. ### Blood, curse, and the mark

A killing created a blood-debt, but laws differed: Hammurabi's code has no general murder law, and an unintentional killing was settled by oath and silver, with no avenger; in the northern feud world, the victim's kin chose killing or payment. Cain expects that world; God reverses it, protecting the murderer and threatening the avenger. The mark protects, but its form is unknown, and whether it stood on his body is debated. Scripture does not identify the mark's appearance or connect it with skin color. No ancient text or artefact shows a god placing a protective mark on a person. The closest analogue is a Ramesside mummy from Deir el-Medina, whose at least thirty tattoos were plausibly drawn amulets — human-applied marks for protection, not immunity a god granted against killing. Sevenfold is debated: seven of the killer's kin, a legal deterrent, or seven as completeness; no sevenfold formula is found in ancient law. Cain settles in Nod, a name occurring once in the Bible, east of Eden. ## Why This Context Matters

Genesis 4 spoke into a world where farmer and shepherd already rivalled each other, gods were fed by human hands, blood-debt was settled by kin or by silver, and tattoos could carry protective meaning. Reading it against that world sharpens what it says: God regards freely and gives no reason; the warning comes before the murder; and mercy interrupts the feud at its first occurrence, protecting the murderer rather than avenging the victim. Later writers take up Abel as righteous and Cain as the way not to walk — reception, not the narrative itself. [^source-westermann-genesis] [^source-bdb-lexicon]
