## Overview

After Cain's exile, Genesis 4:17–24 follows his line. Cain builds a city and names it for his son Enoch; five generations on from Cain, Lamech takes two wives, Adah and Zillah, and their children originate three ways of life — Jabal the tent-dwelling herdsman, Jubal the musician, and Tubal-cain the forger of bronze and iron. Lamech closes the genealogy with a song boasting vengeance seventy and sevenfold, against the sevenfold protection promised to Cain. The passage gives no date for these events and no site for the city, and the names of Cain's line echo those of the Sethite line that follows, with two Enochs and two Lamechs who must not be confused. What the passage does record is the Bible's first account of the origins of civilisation: city, craft, music, and song, set beside escalating violence. ## Ancient World

### Cities and kings

The Hebrew word for city, *ʿîr*, means an enclosed, fortified space, not necessarily a large town, and who built it is left open: as it stands, Cain builds, though some read Enoch. Its site cannot be identified. By around 3200 BCE, Uruk was the largest settlement in southern Mesopotamia, a city of monumental mud-brick buildings — the world of Genesis' first city, not an identification of it. The Sumerian King List offers a nearer parallel in form: eight kings before the flood, ruling 241,200 years. Scholarship matched its names to the patriarchs of Genesis 5, but those correspondences have collapsed; the ten-king list is a later elaboration. ### Herdsmen, musicians, and smiths

Jabal is the father, the originator, of tent-dwellers and livestock-keepers — the Bedouin life, which the text does not judge. Jubal fathers all who play the lyre and the pipe — the *kinnôr*, a plucked stringed instrument, and the *ʿûgāb*, a wind instrument rendered "harp and organ" in the King James Version. The Lyres of Ur, about 2600–2500 BCE, show such instruments existed in third-millennium Mesopotamia — context, not Jubal's instruments. Tubal-cain forges every implement of bronze and iron — "brass" in the King James Version is copper or bronze, and iron is a loanword — a metal-worker, not the inventor of weapons. The Nahal Mishmar hoard, about 4000–3300 BCE, holds 442 objects, most of them copper — early Levantine copper working was already advanced. The later Kenites are sometimes linked to this craft through the root *qyn*, "smith", but Scripture never calls them metalworkers or connects them with Cain. Naamah is named only as Tubal-cain's sister; the later traditions — Noah's wife, a demon, a patroness of music — are post-biblical, not Genesis. ### Lamech's song

Lamech's song is a poem in three distichs, an independent song fitted as the genealogy's conclusion: "I have slain a man to my wounding" is a boast, and its "seventy and sevenfold" intensifies Cain's sevenfold without limit. It is not a sword song celebrating his son's weapons; the song gives no hint of such an occasion. On whether the passage judges any of this, the commentaries split: Skinner and Westermann hold that it passes no moral judgment on the facts, while Keil and Gabriel — second-hand, via Maarten — read a negative evaluation in the context. Both sides are presented here; neither is the consensus. [^source-skinner-genesis] [^source-westermann-genesis] [^source-maarten-eisegesis] [^source-gabriel-cainite-genealogy]

## Why This Context Matters

Genesis 4:17–24 spoke into a world where cities, crafts, and songs already carried meaning, and it sets the first of each beside escalating vengeance. Reading it against that world sharpens what it actually says: the inventions arrive without an explicit judgment, the commentaries divide over what the context implies, and the passage's own frame is the contrast with Seth's line, which calls on the name of the LORD. Later writers take up the same materials in the other direction — the instruments in worship, and seventy-sevenfold vengeance answered by forgiveness without limit.
