## Overview

After Cain kills Abel, Adam and Eve have another son. Eve names him Seth, saying God has appointed her another seed instead of Abel, and Seth's own son Enosh is born into a line that begins to call on the name of the LORD. Genesis 5 then sets out the generations from Adam to Noah: ten names, each with the age at which a son was born, the years lived afterwards, and a total lifespan, closed each time by the words "and he died" — except for Enoch, of whom it is said that he walked with God and was not, for God took him. The list ends with Lamech naming his son Noah in hope of comfort from the cursed ground, and with Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth. The passage gives no independently established date. ## Ancient World

### Three texts, three chronologies

The genealogy survives in three textual traditions. The Masoretic Text gives Adam 130 years at Seth's birth and a creation-to-Flood total of 1,656 years. The Samaritan Pentateuch agrees with it for most names but reads Jared 62, Methuselah 67, and Lamech 53 when each son is born, for a total of 1,307 years, in which Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech each die in the Flood year. The Septuagint, in the Göttingen edition, reads larger begetting ages early on and a total of 2,242 years. Its Methuselah is 167 at Lamech's birth, so he dies fourteen years after the Flood; other manuscripts read 187, placing his death six years before it. Which is original is debated; his total of 969 years is the same either way, and each total is only what its own numbers add up to. ### Names, lifespans, and the pattern of death

Only two names carry a meaning the sources establish with confidence: Seth, "appointed", from the wordplay in Eve's own speech, and Mahalaleel, "praise of God". Enosh is probably "man", with frail, mortal resonance, and Noah's name is tied in the text itself to rest and comfort — a traditional wordplay, not a settled derivation. The meanings of the other names are not established from the Hebrew lexicon, and the popular reading of Methuselah's name as "when he dies, judgment" appears in no lexicon. Each life is measured and ends in death, except one: Enoch lives only 365 years and does not die in the usual way but is taken. Skinner judged one older theory, the list as coded degeneration, not probable. [^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-skinner-genesis]

### Enoch, the scrolls, and the Flood's approach

Enoch's 365 years have long been read against the solar year, and the closest parallel is Enmeduranki, a Babylonian king-priest of Sippar, the sun-god's city, intimate with the gods — but he is never said to have been taken up; the parallel is an analogy, not a source for the Genesis notice. The Dead Sea Scrolls add almost nothing here: their single witness, 4QGen-b, preserves one word from Genesis 5 — the name Cainan. The parallel once drawn between the ten patriarchs and Berossus's ten antediluvian kings has collapsed; the Sumerian King List names eight kings before the Flood. Skinner reads Lamech's hope for Noah as pointing to vine-culture, not the Flood, which brought that generation no comfort — his interpretation, not the verse's statement. ## Why This Context Matters

Genesis 5 spoke into a world that counted kings in long reigns and traced lines back to the beginning, yet its genealogy is spare: names, years, deaths, one man taken by God, one son named in hope of rest. Reading it against the Samaritan and Greek texts, the scrolls, and the king lists sharpens what it does and does not claim. It assigns no date, proves no lifespan, and explains no origin of evil — but it carries the story from the first murder to the threshold of the Flood, with the line of Seth, not Cain, reaching Noah.
