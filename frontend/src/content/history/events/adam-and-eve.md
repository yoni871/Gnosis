## Overview

Genesis 2:4–3:24 tells a second, distinct creation account. God forms the human from dust of the ground and breathes the breath of life into him, plants a garden in Eden, and places the human there to work it and keep it, a vocation the first account frames in royal image language. The human names the animals, and the woman is formed as a companion. A serpent questions the divine command about the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; the woman and the man eat, and God pronounces judgments on the serpent, the woman, and the man before sending them out of the garden, where cherubim guard the way to the tree of life. The passage gives no independently established date or place for Eden. One scholar tentatively dates the narrative to the sixth or fifth century BCE, a proposal rather than a consensus. ## Ancient World

### Dust, breath, and work

In Enki and Ninmaḫ, gods knead clay from the abzu to relieve divine toil, a polytheistic contest, a workforce, no breath of life. Genesis has one God forming one man from dust and breathing the breath of life into him; dust frames the event, returning in the serpent's punishment and the sentence of return. The word for rib occurs of a human body only here; elsewhere its dominant sense is side, though the commentaries say rib. The work-and-keep charge is farming and guarding language, and the same verbs recur at the end: the man tills the ground in exile, and cherubim guard it. One scholar argues the pairing marks the man as priestly, since elsewhere it occurs only for priestly duties — yet she warns the label is anachronistic, and the commentaries read it as farming and stewardship. ### Serpents, trees, and gardens

Genesis 2 is no cosmogony: unlike Babylon's Enūma eliš, no theogony or combat, only garden, command, and serpent. Serpents across the Near East are chaos monsters, divine emblems, guardians, yet none surveyed speaks to a human and deceives. In Gilgamesh, a serpent steals the rejuvenating plant while the hero bathes — youth restored, not life — and never speaks. Adapa loses immortality by refusing the food and water of life in obedience, the inverse of Genesis. Assyrian palace art shows a stylised sacred tree flanked by the king at Nimrud — but no ancient text calls such a tree a tree of life. The closest divine garden is Dilmun, without sickness or death, watered into a garden of gods, not humans, without prohibition or expulsion. ### Rivers, knowledge, and exile

Only two of Eden's rivers can be identified: the Tigris and the Euphrates. The Pishon and the Gihon remain unknown, and Cush is usually Nubia, though a Kassite land east of the Tigris is a genuine alternative. Knowledge of good and evil is most commonly read as moral maturity, one scholar's report, not a consensus; other readings are moral discernment, cultural knowledge, and functional mastery, while a sexual reading survives only as a debated candidate. The judgments are both sentence and explanation for present conditions, and the couple leaves eastward, the narrative's sign of exile. Some read the garden as a sanctuary, with cherubim as its guardians; winged guardians stood at palace entrances, as the lamassu at Nimrud show, though Genesis gives no description. ## Why This Context Matters

Genesis 2–3 spoke into a world where gardens, sacred trees, wise first men, and crafty serpents already carried meaning. Reading the passage against that world sharpens what it actually says: one God forms a single human and breathes life into him, gives work before any fall, and judges disobedience with exile rather than annihilation. Later biblical writers take up Eden as restoration imagery and as theological reception — Adam set against Christ, the tree of life promised again — but those readings belong to reception, not to the garden narrative itself.
