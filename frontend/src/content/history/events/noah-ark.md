## Overview

Genesis 6:9–22 introduces Noah as a righteous man, blameless in his generation, who walked with God, and then gives God's command to build the ark. The earth, the passage says, was corrupt and filled with violence, and God announces its destruction by flood. Noah is told to make an ark of gopher wood, pitched within and without, 300 cubits long, 50 broad, and 30 high, with rooms, a window, a side door, and three decks. In the same speech God promises to establish his covenant with Noah, and commands him to bring his household and two of every living creature into the ark, with food for them all. The passage closes as it began, with obedience: "Thus did Noah; according to all that God commanded him, so did he." No date, place, or building time is given. ## Ancient World

### Noah's portrait: righteous, blameless, walking with God

Three Hebrew words describe Noah. *Ṣaddîq* means just or righteous, a courtroom word for conduct standing before a judge — its first occurrence in the Bible. *Tāmîm* means complete or blameless, a word used of unblemished animals for sacrifice, transferred to character: integrity, not sinlessness. "Walked with God" is the same phrasing used of Enoch and Abraham. [^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-skinner-genesis] [^source-ryle-genesis]

### The ark: what the text describes

The ark is built of "gopher wood", a word found nowhere else, of unknown meaning — cypress is the leading guess, unproven. Its rooms are literally "nests", sealed inside and out with pitch, bitumen. It measures 300 cubits long, 50 broad, and 30 high, but the cubit's length is uncertain, so no modern measurement is given as fact. The "window" is disputed — light-opening or roof — and the text gives no hull shape, sail, or rudder. [^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis] [^source-ryle-genesis]

### A covenant announced

Genesis 6:18 first uses the word *bĕrît*, covenant: "But with thee will I establish my covenant." The verb means to establish, rather than to cut, a covenant. The promise is made before the Flood for Noah's whole household — eight persons — its terms and sign coming only in Genesis 9. Whether it is a unilateral promise, a compact conditioned on obedience, or the confirmation of an earlier covenant is debated. [^source-ryle-genesis] [^source-skinner-genesis]

### Other flood stories

Mesopotamia told flood stories sharing the biblical skeleton: warning, vessel, animals, flood, and sacrifice. In Atrahasis the gods act because mankind disturbs them, and the warning god must whisper through a reed hut, bound by oath. In Gilgamesh, Tablet XI, the vessel is a cube, 120 cubits on every side; a separate tablet describes a round coracle. Genesis differs where it matters: one God, a moral cause, an open warning, and a covenant instead of population control or the hero's immortality. ### How later readers heard Noah

Later readers kept the story and read it afresh. Jubilees retold it on a dated calendar, landing the ark on Lubar, a mountain of Ararat; Enoch told of his radiant birth. Josephus cited Berossus that ark remains were shown in Armenia — evidence for the tradition, not the remains. Christians read the ark as a figure of the Church and baptism: Justin saw the eight saved as a sign of the eighth day, Tertullian called the deluge the world's baptism, and Augustine insisted on both history and figure. ## Why This Context Matters

Knowing the ark's world sharpens what Genesis claims and what it does not. The Hebrew words behind "righteous" and "blameless" describe integrity before God, not sinlessness; the dimensions are the text's own measurements, not a modern engineering proof; and the Mesopotamian parallels show a shared flood story told very differently — one God, a moral cause, an open warning, and a covenant. The context leaves the passage's own shape clear: judgment announced, mercy promised, and obedience answering both. [^source-skinner-genesis] [^source-dalley-myths-mesopotamia]
