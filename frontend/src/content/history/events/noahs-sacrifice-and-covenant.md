## Overview

Noah builds the first altar and offers burnt offerings of clean animals and birds; the Lord smells the aroma and resolves never again to curse the ground because of man — though man's heart is evil from his youth — and never again to strike every living thing.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon] The seasons shall not cease. God blesses Noah and his sons — be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth — amid fear and dread; meat is permitted, but not flesh with its lifeblood, for the life is in the blood.[^source-bible-hub-interlinear] 9:6 keeps the traditional rendering, 'by man shall his blood be shed' — the Hebrew preposition is disputed, Mastnjak reading 'for [the] man' against Finlay's 'by (means of) man'; no textual variant exists.[^source-mastnjak-genesis-9-6][^source-finlay-genesis-9-6-reply] God establishes his everlasting covenant with Noah, his seed, and every living creature, with his bow in the cloud as sign.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

## Ancient World

### The sacrifice

The purpose of Noah's sacrifice is disputed: propitiation (Skinner; Ryle) against thanksgiving (Westermann following Cassuto; JFB; Keil). No consensus exists. All agree the "pleasing aroma" is gracious acceptance, not a crude divine appetite, and that 8:21b — "the intention of man's heart is evil from his youth" — grounds divine restraint, not a fresh indictment: judgment cannot cure the human condition, so God puts up with it.[^source-skinner-genesis][^source-westermann-genesis][^source-ryle-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary]

### The new order

The blessing of 9:1 repeats the creation triad of Genesis 1:28, but dominion is reframed as fear and dread falling on the animals. The seasons are ordained as a standing order of nature. Meat is permitted on the pattern of the green plant, with one reservation: flesh with its lifeblood, for the life is in the blood. Why the ban exists is not agreed — six distinct rationales — though the equation itself is established. The blood law of 9:6, a mirror of shedding and being shed, grounds itself in the image of God; whether the image survived the fall intact, was injured, or names man's higher nature is explained four different ways.[^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-bible-hub-interlinear][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-westermann-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-ryle-genesis][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary]

### The covenant and the bow

The covenant's verb shifts from "am establishing" to "I have established," the Genesis 6:18 form, and its parties widen: Noah and his seed, every living creature, the earth, all flesh. Whether it includes the ordinances of 9:1–7 or only the no-more-flood promise is open. Kessler holds it universal, unconditional, and God-only — though "unconditional" names the no-flood promise, since 9:4–6 has stipulations. The bow's origin is split — newly created or pre-existing and appointed — and the warrior-bow derivation disputed; Chrysostom holds it is given for human fear, not because God needs to remember.[^source-kessler-jesus-jewish-covenants][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-westermann-genesis][^source-chrysostom-homilies-genesis][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis]

### Other flood stories

The Mesopotamian stories share the pattern but not the covenant: in Atrahasis and Gilgamesh XI the gods gather like flies over the offering — George's wording, "gathered like flies around the man making sacrifice" — and the answer is population controls or immortality for one man, never a covenant or a sign. Jubilees 6 is the closest parallel: sacrifice, covenant, blood oath, bow as sign, annual renewal. In Enuma Elish, Marduk's bow is fixed as a Bow Star in heaven — not a rainbow, not a covenant, not a weapon laid aside. These are analogies, never dependence.[^source-dalley-myths-mesopotamia][^source-george-epic-gilgamesh-penguin][^source-etcsl-flood-story][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-enuma-elish-tablet-vi][^source-skinner-genesis]

## Why This Context Matters

The Flood ends not with a new law but with a resolve: God binds himself never again to destroy all flesh by water, while human nature stays what it was. The covenant widens beyond Noah to every living creature and the earth itself, and its sign is given for human fear — a bow in the cloud that God looks on in order to remember. "Never again" names the flood, and only the flood; the promise is everlasting, and the sign is still in the sky.
