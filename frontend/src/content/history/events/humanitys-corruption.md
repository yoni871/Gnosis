# Humanity's Corruption

## Overview

In eight verses the world changes. Men multiply, and their daughters are taken by the sons of God, whoever those sons are; a number is set — one hundred and twenty years; the Nephilim are there, and the mighty men of old; and God, looking down, sees that the wickedness of man is great and that every thought of his heart is only evil continually. The passage ends with a judgment announced and a single hinge of grace: Noah finds favour in the eyes of the LORD. Who the sons are, who the Nephilim are, and what the one hundred and twenty years mean — the passage does not say. Three readings of the sons have been argued for two thousand years, and this article presents all three, with no endorsement.[^source-bdb-lexicon]

## Ancient World

### The sons of God: three readings

The Hebrew phrase is bənê hāʾĕlōhîm, and the lexicon records three readings of it. The angelic reading — the sons are heavenly beings — is held by Ryle and Skinner, and by Justin and Tertullian. The Sethite reading — the godly line of Seth taking Cainite wives — is held by Keil and Delitzsch, and by Augustine, its fullest advocate. The royal reading — kings and judges — is held by the Targums and the rabbis. Westermann and Gunkel refine it: lower divine beings, expressly not angels. Against it, Keil cites Matthew 22:30.[^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-ryle-genesis] [^source-skinner-genesis] [^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis] [^source-augustine-city-of-god] [^source-targum-onkelos] [^source-rashi-genesis] [^source-westermann-genesis] [^source-gunkel-genesis]

### The Nephilim and the mighty men

The Nephilim are the passage's most famous word, and its least settled. The King James Version calls them giants; the Hebrew etymology is lost — the lexicon calls it dubious — and fallen ones is a gloss, not the lexicon's assertion. The text does not say the Nephilim are the children of the unions. Skinner and Gunkel identify them with the mighty men of old. Keil and the Jamieson-Fausset-Brown commentators hold that they pre-exist the unions and are distinct from them. The word appears once more, in Numbers 13:33.[^source-bdb-lexicon] [^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis] [^source-skinner-genesis] [^source-gunkel-genesis] [^source-jfb-commentary]

### The 120 years, and a grieving God

God's Spirit shall not always strive with man, and his days shall be one hundred and twenty years. The Hebrew verb is uncertain. The manuscript reads yādôn; yādûr, to abide, stands in a Dead Sea Scrolls commentary and the Greek version. Whether the years are a countdown or a new limit on human life is disputed: the countdown is held by Keil, Clarke, Augustine, and the rabbis; the cap by Skinner, Gunkel, and Westermann; Ryle finds both objectionable. Genesis 5's lifespans trouble the cap. The LORD repents that he has made man, and it grieves him. Every commentator calls the language anthropomorphic. Noah finds favour, for no stated reason.[^source-hendel-genesis-text] [^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis] [^source-skinner-genesis]

### A world of assemblies and floods

At Ugarit the gods are a family — the sons of El, the phrase's closest background. In Mesopotamia the divine assembly decides the flood, with no moral cause given; in Genesis it follows a stated one, and God is grieved, not frightened. No ancient Near Eastern text describes a divine being marrying a human woman, or a race of giants called Nephilim: the closest material is royal hero parentage, like Gilgamesh, two-thirds divine.[^source-baal-cycle-ktu] [^source-etcsl-eridu-genesis] [^source-thompson-gilgamesh]

## Why This Context Matters

This passage is where Genesis turns from genealogy to judgment. It shows a world in which the boundary between heaven and earth is contested, in which human wickedness is described not by deeds but by the imagination of the heart, and in which God is presented as grieved rather than indifferent. The scholarly literature agrees on the shape of the passage and disagrees on its identification: the sons, the Nephilim, the one hundred and twenty years. Holding those disagreements open is the honest reading of the passage, and the one this article keeps.[^source-westermann-genesis] [^source-bdb-lexicon]
