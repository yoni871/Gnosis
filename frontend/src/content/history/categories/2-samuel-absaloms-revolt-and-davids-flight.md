## Overview



A son steals a kingdom at the city gate, and a king flees his own capital weeping and barefoot. Absalom, David's third son, has spent years winning the people: rising early to stand beside the way of the gate, telling every man with a suit that his case is good but the king has no one to hear it, and saying, "Oh that I were made judge in the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. When a man bows to him, Absalom takes his hand and kisses him — and "so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saying he must pay a vow at Hebron, he has himself proclaimed king there at the trumpet's sound, with David's own counsellor Ahithophel at his side [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David does not stand a siege. He flees Jerusalem at once, to spare the city the sword, leaving ten concubines to keep the house, and crosses the brook Kidron with his household, his Cherethites and Pelethites, and six hundred Gittites under Ittai, a foreigner who arrived only yesterday and swears to stay with the king in death or in life [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The priests bring the ark out to him, but David sends it back: if he finds favour with the LORD, he will be brought back to see it again; if not, "behold, here am I, let him do to me as seemeth good unto him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He climbs the Mount of Olives weeping, head covered, barefoot, and prays that the LORD will turn Ahithophel's counsel into foolishness [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. On the road he meets two men who show how unsettled the kingdom is: Ziba, who brings supplies and claims his master Mephibosheth waits in Jerusalem for Saul's kingdom to be restored to him, and Shimei of Saul's house, who curses David at Bahurim and throws stones and dust, calling him a man of blood [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David will not let Abishai silence him: "let him curse, because the LORD hath said unto him, Curse David" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Meanwhile Absalom enters Jerusalem, accepts Hushai — David's friend, sent back to defeat Ahithophel's counsel — and takes Ahithophel's advice to go in to his father's concubines on the housetop, in the sight of all Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. That public act is read as the fulfilment of Nathan's sentence against David, spoken years before: his wives would be taken in the sight of the sun [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. Then comes the council. Ahithophel urges twelve thousand men that very night, to strike the weary king alone; Hushai urges delay, a great muster from Dan to Beersheba, and Absalom leading it himself [^source-biblicaltraining-hushai]. Absalom chooses Hushai — and the narrator explains why: "the LORD had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, to the intent that the LORD might bring evil upon Absalom" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The warning reaches David the same night; by morning light his whole company has crossed the Jordan, "there lacked not one" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Ahithophel goes home and hangs himself. At Mahanaim, beyond the river, three men bring David's hungry, weary people food and bedding [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom].

## Ancient World



### The gate and the chariot

Absalom's revolt begins not with a battle but with a morning routine. He rises early and stands beside the way of the city gate, where men come with suits for the king's judgment [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. The gate is where justice is done in his world: the place a petitioner goes to be heard. Absalom asks each man his town, tells him his claim is good, and adds the wound — the king has no one to hear it. Then he offers himself: if he were judge, everyone would receive justice [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. When a man bows, Absalom takes his hand and kisses him rather than accepting the bow. The chariot, the horses, and the fifty men running before him are part of the same message [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. That display belongs to royalty: his younger brother Adonijah will use it later when he makes his own bid for the throne [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Behind the appeal, the Absalom article notes, lay real complaints about how justice was administered in the kingdom [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Absalom is probably now David's oldest surviving son, and he may have considered himself the natural heir — but no law of succession has yet been fixed in Israel, and the narrative never says David promised him the throne [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom].

### Hebron: the old capital

Hebron is where the revolt is proclaimed, and the choice is deliberate. It is Absalom's birthplace and David's first capital, Judah's oldest centre of royal power [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. David ruled Judah from Hebron for seven years and six months before taking Jerusalem, and Absalom was born there during those years to Maacah, daughter of Talmai, king of Geshur (2 Sam 3:2–3) [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Geshur is a small Aramean state east of the Sea of Galilee, in the area of the modern Golan; the marriage to its king's daughter was probably a political alliance that secured David's north-eastern frontier, and it also gives Absalom royal blood on both sides of his family and a refuge outside Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. The vow Absalom claims to pay there was made in Geshur itself: if the LORD brings me back to Jerusalem, I will serve the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He returns to the city of his birth to be proclaimed king, with two hundred men from Jerusalem who came as invited guests and know nothing, and with secret messengers already sent through all the tribes to cry "Absalom reigneth in Hebron" at the trumpet's sound [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He also sends for Ahithophel the Gilonite from Giloh, a town in the hill country of Judah near Hebron — within easy reach of the rebellion's starting point, though its exact site is uncertain [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel].

### The flight: Kidron, Olivet, and the ark

David's answer to the news is immediate flight. He will not be trapped in Jerusalem, and he will not have the city smitten with the edge of the sword (15:14) [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He leaves ten concubines to keep the house — the women whose fate will carry the story forward — and goes out on foot with his household [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. With him go his bodyguard, the Cherethites and Pelethites, and six hundred Gittites, men from Gath who followed him from his exile there [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The most striking of them is their leader, Ittai: a foreigner, an exile, come only yesterday, whom David urges to go back — and who answers with an oath by the LORD and by the king's life, to stay with the king whether in death or in life [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. All the country weeps as the company passes over the brook Kidron toward the wilderness [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Kidron is the valley along the eastern side of Jerusalem, between the city and the Mount of Olives, and this flight is its first mention in the Bible [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. David climbs the Mount of Olives weeping, head covered, barefoot, and all the people with him do the same [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The priests Zadok and Abiathar bring the ark out of the city, but David sends it back: if he finds favour with the LORD, he will be brought back to see it again; if the LORD has no delight in him, "behold, here am I, let him do to me as seemeth good unto him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Once, the elders of Israel fetched the ark to save them in battle and lost it; David will not use it as a guarantee. The priests stay in Jerusalem, and their sons Ahimaaz and Jonathan will carry news to the king waiting in the plains of the wilderness [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. At the top of the mount David learns that Ahithophel has joined the conspirators, and prays that the LORD will turn his counsel into foolishness [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The prayer is answered almost at once, by the arrival of a friend.

### Two courts, two counsellors

The middle chapter sets two courts against each other. On the road, David meets Ziba, servant of Mephibosheth, with asses, bread, raisins, summer fruit, and wine; Ziba claims his master waits in Jerusalem, expecting Saul's kingdom to be restored to him, and David gives him all that belonged to Mephibosheth [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then, at Bahurim, Shimei son of Gera of Saul's house comes out cursing, throwing stones and dust, calling David a man of blood and saying the LORD has given the kingdom to Absalom [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Abishai offers to take his head off. David refuses: "let him curse, because the LORD hath said unto him, Curse David," and hopes the LORD will requite him good for the cursing this day [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. In Jerusalem, meanwhile, Absalom holds court. Hushai the Arkite greets him, "God save the king," and when Absalom mocks his loyalty to his friend, answers that he serves whom the LORD and the people and all Israel choose — and as he served the father, so will he serve the son [^source-biblicaltraining-hushai]. The Chronicler's list of David's officials sets the two men of the rival courts side by side: "Ahithophel was the king's counsellor. Hushai the Arkite was the king's friend" [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. "Friend of the king" may name a formal office, not only a personal bond: the same title is given to Zabud under Solomon, and similar titles are known from neighbouring courts [^source-biblicaltraining-hushai]. Ahithophel's first counsel is to take the concubines in public, on the housetop, in the sight of all Israel — in the ancient Near East, taking a king's harem is a claim to his throne, as Abner's affair with Rizpah and Adonijah's request for Abishag both show [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. It makes the break with David final, and it is read as the fulfilment of Nathan's sentence: the wives taken in the sight of the sun [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. The narrator adds that Ahithophel's counsel in those days was as if a man had enquired at the oracle of God [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### The council at Mahanaim

No battle is fought in these three chapters. The war is fought in counsel. Ahithophel proposes twelve thousand men that very night, to fall on David while he is weary and weak-handed, strike the king only, and bring everyone else back in peace; the plan pleases Absalom and all the elders [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. But Absalom calls Hushai too, and Hushai says the counsel is not good this time: David and his men are mighty and chafed, like a bear robbed of her whelps; David is a man of war and will not lodge with the people; a first reverse would be reported as a slaughter of Absalom's followers [^source-biblicaltraining-hushai]. Instead, gather all Israel from Dan to Beersheba, as the sand by the sea, and let Absalom lead it himself, falling on David like dew — and if David hides in a city, drag the city with ropes into the river [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Absalom chooses Hushai, and the narrator gives the reason: "the LORD had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, to the intent that the LORD might bring evil upon Absalom" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-bible-odyssey-absalom]. The plan is granted to be good; it fails because the LORD overrules it, not because it is poorly conceived [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. Hushai warns the priests at once. Jonathan and Ahimaaz wait at En-rogel; a servant girl carries the message, a lad betrays them, and they hide in a well at Bahurim while a woman spreads ground corn over its mouth and misdirects the searchers [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. By morning light David's whole company has crossed the Jordan, and "there lacked not one" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Ahithophel, seeing his counsel rejected, goes home, sets his house in order, and hangs himself [^source-biblicaltraining-ahithophel]. David reaches Mahanaim in Gilead — the town that once crowned Saul's son Ish-bosheth, now the headquarters of the exiled king [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. There Shobi of Rabbah, Machir of Lo-debar, and the aged Barzillai of Rogelim bring beds, food, and bedding for a hungry, weary, thirsty people [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Machir is the man who once housed Mephibosheth; the kindness David showed to Jonathan's house now returns to David through the same household.

## Why This Context Matters



These three chapters are the lowest point of David's reign, and the hinge of the whole Absalom story. A king who took Jerusalem and received the promise of a house for ever now loses his capital to his own son, flees it on foot, and leaves his return to the LORD's pleasure. Knowing the world of the chapters — the gate where justice is sought, the old capital where kings are made, the valley and the ridge a fugitive king must cross, the court where a counsellor's word counts as an oracle — keeps the reader from treating the revolt as a mere family quarrel. It is a political crisis fought in counsel, not on a battlefield, and the narrator insists that the decisive battle is the one the reader cannot see: a prayer on the Mount of Olives answered through a friend's persuasive speech and a proud prince's choice. The chapters also refuse to let David off the hook. The public shame on the housetop is the sentence Nathan pronounced after Bathsheba, carried out to the letter; the king who would not judge his own son now endures a curse he will not silence. That is why the flight matters: it shows a kingdom unravelling from within, and a God who still governs it through the very counsel that was meant to destroy his anointed.
