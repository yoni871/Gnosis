## Overview

David has spared Saul twice, and nothing has changed. After the second sparing at Hachilah, Saul blesses him, returns home, and David goes his way — the same unresolved parting as after the cave at En-gedi [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. David draws the conclusion in his own heart: one day Saul will kill him after all, and there is nothing better than to escape into the land of the Philistines, so that Saul will give up seeking him anywhere in Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He crosses to Gath with his six hundred men, each with his household, and his two wives, and Achish son of Maoch, king of Gath, receives him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul hears of it and seeks him no more. At David's request Achish gives him a country town, Ziklag, rather than a place in the royal city, and David lives in Philistine territory for a year and four months [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. From Ziklag he raids the Geshurites, the Girzites, and the Amalekites, peoples of the desert toward Shur and Egypt, leaving no one alive who could carry word to Gath — while telling Achish he has been raiding the south of Judah and its neighbours [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Achish believes him, and concludes that David has made his own people abhor him and will be his servant for ever. Then the Philistines gather for war against Israel, and Achish orders David to march with him. David answers with words that promise nothing — "thou shalt know what thy servant will do" — and Achish makes him keeper of his head for ever, the commander of his bodyguard [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The anointed king of Israel now stands as a Philistine king's guard, ordered to fight his own people, with no way out that the story has yet shown. The chapter is the hinge of David's fugitive years: the refuge he chose, the bargain it required, and the order that turns the bargain into a trap.

## Ancient World

### Gath, the inland Philistine city

Gath was one of the five principal cities of the Philistines, and the one most closely tied to the story of David [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. Unlike Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ashdod on the coast, Gath stood inland, on the border between the Philistine plain and the low hills of Judah, guarding a main route from the coastal plain up into the hill country [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. Its name means "winepress." Each of the five cities was governed by one of the "five Philistine rulers," a title used only of the Philistine lords [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. Most scholars now identify Gath with Tell es-Safi, a great mound on the south side of the Valley of Elah, where excavations since 1996 have uncovered one of the largest Iron Age cities in the region, Philistine from the early Iron Age [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. A potsherd from about the tenth or early ninth century BC found there bears two names resembling "Goliath" — not the biblical Goliath, but proof that names of that kind were in use at Gath in David's general period [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. Gath was the home of Goliath, and the road from the battlefield at Elah ran straight down the valley toward it [^source-biblicaltraining-gath]. It is also the city where the captured ark was brought after Ashdod, and struck, before being sent on to Ekron [^source-biblicaltraining-gath].

### A vassal in a foreign court

David's standing with Achish was that of a military retainer, in Ellicott's words "to all intents and purposes, a vassal prince of Achish" [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. He held a country town from the king and owed service in return — which is why Achish can order him into the host when the Philistines gather for war [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The arrangement suited both sides and trapped both. Achish gained six hundred trained Israelite fighters and a commander whose reputation was made by killing Philistines; David gained a refuge Saul would not enter and a base beyond the reach of Saul's spearmen. But the price was visible from the start. Ellicott reads the request for a country town as David's wish to conduct his affairs apart from the supervision of his Philistine friends, with Achish trusting that through David's help military demonstrations could be made in the southern districts of Saul's kingdom [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. Whether the Achish of this chapter is the same king David met alone on his first visit to Gath is debated among commentators, and the question is left open here [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. What the chapter establishes is the bargain itself: shelter and a town, in exchange for service that would one day be called in.

### Ziklag, a town without a site

Ziklag belonged in the old town lists to Simeon, and to Judah, and by David's time the Philistines held it [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. Achish gave it to David "that day," and the narrator adds that it therefore belonged to the kings of Judah "unto this day" — the voice of a later time looking back, not a date [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Where the town stood, nobody can now say with certainty. At least fourteen sites have been proposed [^source-wikipedia-ziklag]. The leading candidates are Tel Halif, Tel Sera on the north bank of Nahal Gerar, Tell el-Far'ah in the south, and Khirbet a-Ra'i in the Shephelah, proposed in 2019 by its excavators and contested by other archaeologists on grounds of biblical geography and the lack of name continuity [^source-wikipedia-ziklag]. No proposed site shows destruction from David's era, and no find ties any of them to David's stay [^source-wikipedia-ziklag]. What the text requires is simpler than a map pin: a Philistine-held country town south of Gath, near the Amalekite borders, from which raiding parties could reach the desert toward Shur and Egypt and return with sheep, oxen, asses, camels, and clothing [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. This account marks the site as unidentified rather than guessing at one.

### The raids and the lie

The peoples David raided were the old inhabitants of the desert fringe: Geshurites, Girzites, and Amalekites, living in the land toward Shur and Egypt since ancient times [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The first two are hard to place — the Geshurites are heard of in widely scattered places, and the Girzites may have been the remnant of a people dispossessed long before [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. The Amalekites are the exception: they are the people Saul was commanded to blot out and failed to destroy, and they are still present after him — the library's decided line is that no completed blotting-out is attested [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. David's raids against them were total: no man or woman left alive, and the livestock and clothing carried off [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The killing had a cold purpose stated in the text itself: no captive was to be left alive to tell Achish what David had really done [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. For when Achish asked where they had raided, David named Judah's own southern neighbours — the South of Judah, of the Jerahmeelites, and of the Kenites [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Jerahmeelites were a Judahite family on the southern frontier; the Kenites lived in friendship with Judah and under its protection [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-27]. Achish heard exactly what he wanted to hear: David was burning his bridge home, and would be his servant for ever [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The deception worked. It also set the trap that closes the chapter: a man believed to have turned against his own people is now ordered to march against them.

### The muster

Ellicott, following Josephus, reads the gathering of the Philistine hosts as a great national effort, not an ordinary border raid: since the defeat in the Valley of Elah, no such Philistine army had been assembled, and now the enemy was strong enough to strike at the centre of the kingdom, marching up the coast and invading through the Valley of Jezreel [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Achish's summons to David was, in Ellicott's words, perfectly natural: he had no reason to suspect the campaign would be distasteful to a wronged David, and David's trained force may even have encouraged the Philistine lords to the effort [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. David's answer promised nothing — "thou shalt know what thy servant will do" — and Achish, hearing a promise, gave him the most distinguished appointment in the army of Gath: keeper of his head for ever, the command of the bodyguard [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The chapter ends there, with the order given and the answer accepted. The reader is left with the question David himself must have been asking: what will his servant do, when the host marches against Israel?

## Why This Context Matters

This chapter is the hinge of David's fugitive years, and it only makes sense when its world is in view. Gath was a real Philistine city with a real king, and refuge there was a real bargain: a town and protection in exchange for service that could one day be called in against his own people. Knowing that Ziklag's site is unidentified keeps the reader from mistaking a guess for a fact, and knowing that the raids were aimed at desert peoples while Achish was told they fell on Judah explains how the deception worked — and why it trapped David. The muster is the price of the refuge coming due. David's ambiguous answer is the chapter's last word, and the context explains why it had to be ambiguous: he could neither refuse his Philistine lord nor promise to fight Israel. The story leaves him there, keeper of Achish's head, marching toward a battle he cannot fight in and cannot refuse — and the next chapters will show how he is delivered from it without ever answering the question.
