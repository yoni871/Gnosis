## Overview



In the twelfth year of Ahaz king of Judah, Hoshea son of Elah begins to reign in Samaria over Israel, and he reigns nine years [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He does evil, "but not as the kings of Israel that were before him" — and the chapter never says what was different about him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Shalmaneser king of Assyria comes up against him, and Hoshea becomes his servant and gives him presents [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the king of Assyria finds conspiracy in Hoshea: he has sent messengers to So king of Egypt and brought no present, as he had done year by year, and the king of Assyria shuts him up and binds him in prison [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king of Assyria comes up throughout all the land, besieges Samaria three years, and in the ninth year of Hoshea takes it, carrying Israel away into Assyria and placing them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. That is six verses. Then the book stops to explain, for seventeen verses, why it happened. "For so it was, that the children of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, which had brought them up out of the land of Egypt" — they feared other gods, built high places in all their cities, "from the tower of the watchmen to the fenced city," made two calves, worshipped all the host of heaven, and caused their sons and daughters to pass through the fire [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The LORD testified against them by all the prophets and all the seers, and they would not hear [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight: there was none left but the tribe of Judah only" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And in the middle of Israel's obituary the chapter adds, "Also Judah kept not the commandments" — Judah already under the same sentence [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the resettlement. The king of Assyria brings men from Babylon, Cuthah, Ava, Hamath, and Sepharvaim and places them in the cities of Samaria instead of the children of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Lions slay some of them, because they "know not the manner of the God of the land," so the king sends back one of the priests carried away from Samaria, who dwells in Bethel and teaches them how they should fear the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. But every nation makes gods of its own as well, and the chapter's verdict is stated twice: "They feared the LORD, and served their own gods... as did their fathers, so do they unto this day" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World



### Samaria, the city that falls

Samaria was the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel and later the name of the central hill country around it [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. King Omri founded the city in the early 9th century BC on a hill he bought from a man named Shemer, and it remained the royal seat until the Assyrians captured it and deported much of its population [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The site lies in the hill country of Ephraim, about 7 miles northwest of ancient Shechem and about 35 miles north of Jerusalem in a straight line, a hill standing on its own, surrounded by valleys, with steep sides and a broad, fairly level top [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Omri chose well. The earlier capitals, Shechem and Tirzah, lay in more exposed positions, and Tirzah had just been burned in civil war; a new city on a site with no prior tribal claims belonged wholly to the crown, much as Jerusalem had belonged to David [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Under Ahab the city became the centre of royal religion as well as government, with a temple for Baal, an altar, and an Asherah pole, and it withstood several Aramean sieges [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. It survived the famine siege of chapters 6–7, when a donkey's head sold for eighty shekels of silver and the city was delivered by an empty camp, and it saw Jehu's purge of Ahab's house and of Baal within its walls [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The long reign of Jeroboam II brought prosperity, and the inscribed potsherds found in the palace area probably date from about that time [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Then the kingdom fell into turmoil, and the rising power of Assyria under Tiglath-Pileser III forced Menahem to pay heavy tribute and stripped away much of Israel's territory in the north and east [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Hoshea is the last king of that city.

### Assyria at the gate

Hoshea, the last king of Israel, became a vassal of Shalmaneser V of Assyria, then withheld tribute and sought help from Egypt [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The Assyrian army besieged Samaria from Hoshea's seventh year until his ninth, about 725 to 722 BC, and then took the city and deported its people to Assyria, to Halah, to the Habor River, and to the towns of the Medes [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The Bible does not name the king who actually took the city: Shalmaneser V began the siege, but his successor Sargon II claimed the conquest in his inscriptions and reported deporting more than 27,000 people, and many historians think Shalmaneser captured Samaria shortly before his death and that Sargon put down a further revolt there about 720 BC [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Samaria then became the centre of the Assyrian province of Samerina [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. By Hoshea's reign the power that had swallowed Syria stood at Samaria's gate, and Egypt, the power Hoshea's messengers turn to, is the last alternative the chapter records.

### Seventeen verses of explanation

The fall takes six verses. The explanation takes seventeen, and it is the chapter's centre. "For so it was, that the children of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, which had brought them up out of the land of Egypt" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The passage begins with the exodus and works forward: the statutes of the heathen, the high places in all their cities, "from the tower of the watchmen to the fenced city," the images and groves on every high hill and under every green tree, the incense, the idols, the testimony of all the prophets and all the seers refused, the necks hardened, the statutes and the covenant and the testimonies rejected, the two calves, the host of heaven, Baal, the sons and daughters passed through the fire, the divination and enchantments [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the sentence: "Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight: there was none left but the tribe of Judah only" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The explanation ends where the kingdom began, with the rent from the house of David: "he rent Israel from the house of David; and they made Jeroboam the son of Nebat king: and Jeroboam drave Israel from following the LORD, and made them sin a great sin" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Every reign since is measured against that first act — "For the children of Israel walked in all the sins of Jeroboam which he did; they departed not from them" — until the removal, "as he had said by all his servants the prophets," with no single oracle named [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The biblical writer explains the disaster as the result of Israel's long unfaithfulness to the covenant [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The passage is carried here as the book's own account, not as a later appendix: the fall is explained before the resettlement is told, and the resettlement is told in its light.

### The new Samaria

The king of Assyria brings men from Babylon, Cuthah, Ava, Hamath, and Sepharvaim and places them in the cities of Samaria instead of the children of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. At the beginning of their dwelling there they fear not the LORD, so the LORD sends lions among them, which slay some of them [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. They report the danger in their own terms: the nations "know not the manner of the God of the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king of Assyria answers with a priest, not an army. One of the priests carried away from Samaria is carried back to dwell in Bethel and teach them "the manner of the God of the land," and he teaches them how they should fear the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Bethel is where Jeroboam set one of the two calves, and the teaching of the LORD's fear is now given there, by a deportee, on the conqueror's orders. The Assyrians brought the peoples from Babylon, Kuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim to live in the towns of Samaria, and when lions attacked the newcomers the Assyrian king sent back an Israelite priest to teach them how to worship the LORD — but they went on serving their own gods as well [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Every nation makes gods of its own and puts them in the houses of the high places the Samaritans had made: Succothbenoth of Babylon, Nergal of Cuth, Ashima of Hamath, Nibhaz and Tartak of the Avites, and Adrammelech and Anammelech of Sepharvaim, to whom the Sepharvites burn their children in fire [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "So they feared the LORD, and made unto themselves of the lowest of them priests of the high places... They feared the LORD, and served their own gods" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The religion that results is additive, not converted: the LORD is added to the gods each nation brought with it. Later settlers were brought in by Esarhaddon and by Ashurbanipal, and because of the settlers from Kuthah, rabbinic writings later called the Samaritans *Kutim* [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria].

### The covenant rehearsed, and the verdict

The chapter does not end with the resettlement. It rehearses the covenant over the new land: "Unto this day they do after the former manners: they fear not the LORD, neither do they after their statutes, or after their ordinances, or after the law and commandment which the LORD commanded the children of Jacob, whom he named Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The terms are restated in full — "Ye shall not fear other gods, nor bow yourselves to them, nor serve them, nor sacrifice to them: But the LORD, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt with great power and a stretched out arm, him shall ye fear, and him shall ye worship, and to him shall ye do sacrifice" — and the statutes, ordinances, law, and commandment are to be observed "for evermore" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. It is the same standard the chapter used to judge Israel, now spoken over a land of strangers who do not hearken: "Howbeit they did not hearken, but they did after their former manner" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The refrain is stated twice, and the second stating adds the generations: "So these nations feared the LORD, and served their graven images, both their children, and their children's children: as did their fathers, so do they unto this day" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "Unto this day" is the chapter's last word, and it closes the fall and the resettlement alike. The land has new inhabitants, and the old divided worship continues in them — and in the middle of Israel's obituary the chapter had already said of Judah, "Also Judah kept not the commandments" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The fall of Israel is told with Judah already under the same sentence, and the book's next movements will carry that sentence out.

## Why This Context Matters



This chapter is the end of the northern kingdom, and it is told as a verdict before it is told as a resettlement. Knowing its world keeps the reader from treating the fall as a merely military event — a siege lost, a city taken. The siege is six verses; the explanation is seventeen, and the explanation names the cause as Israel's own long unfaithfulness, traced from the exodus to Jeroboam's calves and through every reign since. The resettlement shows what that verdict looks like in practice: a land repopulated by strangers who learn the rites of the god of the land and keep their own gods beside him, generation after generation, "unto this day." And the chapter will not let Judah stand outside the sentence. In the middle of Israel's obituary it says Judah kept not the commandments either — the fall of one kingdom is the warning to the other, and the rest of the book will show whether Judah heeds it.
