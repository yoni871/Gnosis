## Overview



Ahaziah king of Judah is dead, slain at Megiddo, and his mother Athaliah seizes the throne by destroying all the seed royal [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. One child escapes. Jehosheba, the dead king's sister, steals her infant nephew Joash from among the king's sons who are being slain and hides him with his nurse in a bedchamber, and then in the house of the LORD, where he stays hidden for six years while Athaliah reigns over the land [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the seventh year the priest Jehoiada acts. He fetches the rulers over hundreds, the captains, and the guard into the house of the LORD, makes a covenant with them, takes their oath, and shows them the king's son [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He sets the sabbath watches at the king's house and the gates, arms the captains with king David's spears and shields that were kept in the temple, and brings the child out: the crown is put upon him, the testimony is given, he is made king and anointed, and the people clap their hands and cry, "God save the king" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Athaliah hears the noise, comes into the temple, sees the boy standing by a pillar with the princes and the trumpeters, rends her clothes, and cries, "Treason, Treason" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. She is taken outside the ranges and slain by the way the horses come into the king's house, because the priest will not have her slain in the house of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Jehoiada then makes a covenant between the LORD and the king and the people, and between the king and the people; the people break down the house of Baal, smash its altars and images, and slay Mattan its priest; the king sits on the throne, and the city is quiet [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. Joash is seven years old when he begins to reign, and he reigns forty years in Jerusalem (KJV 11:21 is the first verse of chapter 12 in the Hebrew numbering) [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He does right all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructs him — but the high places are not taken away [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The temple he repairs with a collection chest, he later empties to buy off Hazael of Syria, and in the end his own servants conspire against him and slay him in the house of Millo [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. It is a revolution led by a priest, in the temple, where the revolution in the north was led by an army, on the road.

## Ancient World



### Two revolutions: the road and the temple

The book of Kings tells two revolutions against the house of Ahab, one in each kingdom, and the contrast between them is the story. In the north, Jehu is anointed by a young prophet in an inner chamber at Ramoth-gilead, rides furiously to Jezreel, slays the two kings in the field, and has Jezebel thrown from a window; his men then destroy Baal in Samaria by a trick, killing its worshipers in their own temple [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the south, nothing moves so fast. A child is hidden for six years in the house of the LORD while a usurper reigns, and then a priest fetches the guard into the temple, makes them swear, shows them the king's son, and crowns the boy by a pillar while the trumpeters stand by [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Jehu's revolution is army-led and road-bound; Jehoiada's is priest-led and temple-bound. Both end the same way, with the house of Baal broken down — in Samaria by the king's men, in Jerusalem by all the people of the land — and both stop short: Jehu keeps the golden calves at Bethel and Dan, and Joash leaves the high places standing [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. The book keeps the two side by side so that each throws light on the other. The north is purged by the sword, the south by covenant; and the southern king, unlike the northern one, is a seven-year-old child who has to be carried to the throne by the priest who hid him.

### The house that hid the king

The temple is the whole stage of the first chapter: the hiding place, the mustering ground, the armoury, and the coronation hall in turn. It is one of the most famous buildings of the ancient world and also one of the most mysterious, because no trace of it has been unearthed in Jerusalem — excavations are not allowed on the Temple Mount — and what we know of it comes from the Bible's own description and from contemporary temples of neighbouring peoples [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. Solomon built it on the northern precinct of Jerusalem that David had purchased, in three parts on an east-west axis: a forecourt with the two great bronze pillars Jachin and Boaz, a central room with the golden incense altar, table, and lamps, and an inner sanctuary where two immense cherubs flanked the ark [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. It stood beside the royal palace for nearly four centuries, until the Babylonians burned it in 586 BC [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. Inside that house, the priest could do everything the chapter needs. The side rooms built against its walls likely served as storerooms for the temple treasuries and for offerings and equipment — a place where a child and his nurse could be kept out of sight for six years [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. The two pillars had a ceremonial role, and the young king Joash stood by the pillar at his coronation, as Josiah did when he renewed the covenant [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. And David's spears and shields were kept there, so that the guard who crowned David's descendant could be armed with David's own weapons [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The temple is not a backdrop in this story. It is the instrument of the rescue: the one place in Jerusalem where a priest could hide a king, arm a guard, and crown a child without ever stepping outside.

### Baal in Jerusalem

The god whose house is broken down on the day of the coronation is the LORD's oldest rival. Baal is the leading active god of the Canaanites, a storm god believed to send the rain and so to give fertility to fields, flocks, and families, and from the plains of Moab to the eve of the Babylonian exile he was the LORD's most persistent rival for Israel's loyalty [^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. The rivalry reached its height under Ahab and Jezebel, when Elijah confronted the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel, and it came south with Athaliah herself: her father Ahab had built Baal a temple and altar in Samaria, and her marriage into Judah carried the cult to Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. Baal had temples in Samaria and in Jerusalem, with altars, sacred standing stones, priests, prophets, and worshipers in special robes [^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. Jehu had already destroyed the northern cult, killing its prophets, priests, and worshipers and turning its house into a draught house; in the south the cult outlived him until the day of the coronation, when the people of the land broke down its house in Jerusalem, smashed its altars and idols, and killed Mattan the priest of Baal in front of the altars [^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. The breaking of the house is the second half of the covenant Jehoiada makes that day: the people return to the LORD, and the rival's house in their own city comes down in the same hour [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. But the purge is not complete. The calves remain in the north and the high places remain in the south, and Baal worship returns under later kings until Josiah's reform removes its articles from the temple [^source-biblicaltraining-baal].

### The chest, the breaches, and the gold

The second chapter is a story about money, and it is told in two movements that do not match. The king orders the priests to take the dedicated money — the census money, the money every man is set at, and the freewill money — and repair the breaches of the house wherever any breach is found [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Twenty-three years later the breaches are still unrepaired. The text gives no reason for the failure, and none is offered here. What changes is the system, not the men: Jehoiada sets a chest beside the altar with a hole bored in its lid, the doorkeepers put the money in, and when it is full the king's scribe and the high priest count it together and hand it to the overseers, who pay the carpenters, builders, masons, and hewers of stone [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. No bowls, snuffers, basons, trumpets, or vessels of gold or silver are made of it, because the money is for the workmen — and no reckoning is made with them, "for they dealt faithfully" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The renovation is one the biblical writers view in a positive light, alongside Josiah's later repair, which uses the same words for the same honesty [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. But the chapter does not end with the repair. Hazael of Syria takes Gath and sets his face toward Jerusalem, and the king sends him the hallowed things of three kings, his own hallowed things, and all the gold in the treasures of the house of the LORD and the king's house — and Hazael goes away [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Jerusalem history sums the reign's two money movements in one breath: Joash repairs the temple using a collection chest, but later pays off Hazael of Aram with its treasures [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The chest that saves the house is followed, in the same chapter, by the gold that leaves it.

### The high places left standing

The one-sentence limit on the reign is the refrain of the book's moderate kings: "the high places were not taken away: the people still sacrificed and burnt incense in the high places" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The high places are local shrines where the people sacrifice and burn incense outside the single sanctuary the law required, and Baal was worshiped at high places, with altars and sacred poles beside them [^source-biblicaltraining-baal]. Joash does right all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructs him — and the next verse states the limit, as if the praise needed its boundary drawn at once [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Amaziah, Azariah, and Jotham all carry the same notice after him; only Hezekiah removes the high places, and Manasseh rebuilds them [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The limit matters because it is the narrator's own grading: the king who repairs the temple does not reform the people's worship. The house is mended while the hills keep their altars, and the reign that begins with a covenant in the temple ends with the people still sacrificing on the heights — and with the king murdered by his own servants in the house of Millo, a terraced structure of the old royal quarter whose name the text gives without describing it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem].

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are about how a dynasty survives when its enemies hold the throne — and about the price of the rescue. Knowing the world of the story keeps the reader from treating Joash's crowning as a simple restoration. The child is saved by a priest who hides him in the temple for six years, and the kingdom is remade by covenant in the same house: the priest arms the guard with David's weapons, crowns the boy by the pillar, binds king and people to the LORD and to each other, and breaks down the house of Baal in the same hour. The temple is not scenery here; it is the hiding place, the armoury, and the throne room. And the context also sets the limit on the reign that follows. The king does right while the priest instructs him, repairs the house with an honest chest, and then empties its treasuries to buy off Hazael — while the high places stand untouched through it all. The rescue is real, the covenant is real, and the quiet of the city does not last.
