## Overview



In his days Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon comes up, and Jehoiakim king of Judah becomes his servant for three years — then he turns and rebels [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The LORD sends bands against him, Chaldees and Syrians, Moabites and Ammonites, to destroy Judah "according to the word of the LORD, which he spake by his servants the prophets" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And the reason is given at once, in the plainest words in the book: "Surely at the commandment of the LORD came this upon Judah, to remove them out of his sight, for the sins of Manasseh... And also for the innocent blood that he shed... which the LORD would not pardon" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Jehoiakim dies, and his son Jehoiachin reigns three months before Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem and he goes out to surrender, with his mother, his servants, his princes, and his officers [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The treasures of the house of the LORD and the king's house are carried out, the gold vessels Solomon made are cut in pieces, "as the LORD had said" and ten thousand captives are carried away — princes, mighty men, craftsmen and smiths — "none remained, save the poorest sort of the people of the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the count is given again: seven thousand men of might, and craftsmen and smiths a thousand [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king of Babylon makes Mattaniah king in Jehoiachin's stead and changes his name to Zedekiah, and Zedekiah reigns eleven years and rebels — "through the anger of the LORD it came to pass... that Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the ninth year of his reign Nebuchadnezzar comes with all his host against Jerusalem, builds forts round about, and besieges the city until the eleventh year [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The famine prevails, and "there was no bread for the people of the land"; the city is broken up, and the king flees by night and is taken in the plains of Jericho [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. At Riblah his sons are slain before his eyes, his eyes are put out, and he is bound in fetters of brass and carried to Babylon [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then Nebuzaradan burns the house of the LORD, the king's house, all the houses of Jerusalem, and every great man's house, and breaks down the walls [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The pillars, the bases, and the brasen sea are broken in pieces and carried to Babylon, and "the brass of all these vessels was without weight" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The priests and officers are slain at Riblah, "So Judah was carried away out of their land" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Over the remnant left behind Nebuchadnezzar sets Gedaliah as ruler, but in the seventh month Ishmael murders him, and the remnant flees to Egypt "for they were afraid of the Chaldees" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And the book ends, not with a return, but with a kindness in Babylon: in the seven and thirtieth year of his captivity, Evilmerodach lifts up Jehoiachin's head, speaks kindly to him, sets his throne above the kings with him in Babylon, and gives him a daily allowance, "a daily rate for every day, all the days of his life" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. No return is promised, no restoration announced, no prophet speaks. The last word is bread, not homecoming.

## Ancient World



### Babylon succeeds Assyria

The book of 2 Kings is the story of two empires succeeding each other, and the narrator never confuses them. Assyria takes its tribute first: Pul exacts silver, Tiglathpileser carries Naphtali captive, Shalmaneser takes Samaria, and Sennacherib takes Judah's fenced cities and is turned back at Jerusalem. Then Babylon takes the story. Nebuchadnezzar II reigned 604–562 BCE [^source-oracc-nebuchadnezzar-011], and his coming is marked in a single verse: the king of Egypt comes not again any more out of his land, "for the king of Babylon had taken from the river of Egypt unto the river Euphrates all that pertained to the king of Egypt" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Egypt, which had slain Josiah at Megiddo and set up Jehoiakim, is finished as a power in the Levant. The bands that come against Judah are not only Babylonians: the Chaldees come with Syrians, Moabites, and Ammonites [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] — the neighbouring peoples, once Judah's fellow sufferers under Assyria, now serving as Babylon's instruments against it. Outside the Bible, the first capture is dated by the Babylonian Chronicle, a clay tablet in the British Museum (BM 21946), which records that in Nebuchadnezzar's seventh year the king of Babylon laid siege to "the city of Judah," and on the second day of Adar conquered the city, took the king prisoner, and installed a king of his own choice. The captured king is Jehoiachin and the appointed king is Zedekiah, and the entry matches the biblical account in its sequence: siege, surrender, replacement, tribute. The chronicle breaks off before the final destruction, so the second siege has no record outside the Bible.

### Jerusalem: the walled city

Jerusalem was the walled capital of Judah, the city David took and Solomon built up, and the siege of chapter 25 is a siege of walls [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Nebuchadnezzar comes "he, and all his host," pitches against the city, and builds forts against it round about [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]; the city holds until the eleventh year of Zedekiah, and what breaks it is not the wall but hunger — on the ninth day of the fourth month "the famine prevailed in the city, and there was no bread for the people of the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Only then is the city broken up, and the men of war flee by night by the gate between two walls, by the king's garden [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The burning that follows is total in the telling: the house of the LORD, the king's house, all the houses of Jerusalem, and every great man's house, and then the walls themselves, broken down by the army of the Chaldees [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The city that the book has treated as the LORD's chosen dwelling since David brought the ark there is left without a house, without a wall, and without its people — the poor of the land alone remaining, to work the fields the city once ruled [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The poor are left "to be vinedressers and husbandmen" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]: the conqueror keeps the land worked and takes the men who could resist.

### The house and its vessels

The house burned in chapter 25 is the First Temple, the house Solomon built in Jerusalem [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple][^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. Its furnishings are named as they are taken: the two pillars of brass, the bases, and the brasen sea, broken in pieces and carried to Babylon; the pots, shovels, snuffers, and spoons; the firepans and the bowls, gold in gold and silver in silver [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. The pillars and the sea were Solomon's work, made for the house of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]; the gold vessels cut in pieces in chapter 24 are the same vessels Solomon made in the temple [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The temple treasure has left the house before, four times, to buy off a foreign king — to Hazael under Jehoash, to Tiglathpileser under Ahaz, to Sennacherib under Hezekiah — and a fifth time, here, as spoil to Babylon [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Each payment is stated without judgment on the payment itself; what the book marks is the direction of travel. The gold leaves the house to keep the city standing; in the end it leaves because the city has fallen. The brass, too, tells the story in a single phrase: "the brass of all these vessels was without weight" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] — unweighed going out, as it was unweighed when Solomon made it, when he left all the vessels unweighed because they were exceeding many.

### Three kings, one sentence

Three kings pass under Babylon's hand in two chapters, and none of them rules on his own terms. Jehoiakim becomes Nebuchadnezzar's servant for three years and then rebels [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]; his son Jehoiachin reigns three months and then goes out to the king of Babylon with his mother, his servants, his princes, and his officers, and is taken in the eighth year of Nebuchadnezzar's reign [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king of Babylon then makes Mattaniah king in his stead and changes his name to Zedekiah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The renaming is the conqueror's prerogative: the last king of Judah reigns under a name given by the king of Babylon, and reigns eleven years before he rebels — "through the anger of the LORD" the text says, "that Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Babylonian Chronicle confirms the pattern from the other side: the king of Babylon seized the city, captured the king, and installed a king of his own choice, receiving heavy tribute before he sent it to Babylon. The end of the line is not a death in battle but a blinding at Riblah: the sons slain before his eyes, the eyes put out, the fetters of brass, Babylon [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And for the king taken first, the end is different again: a throne set above the thrones of the kings with him in Babylon, prison garments changed, bread eaten continually before the king [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Babylonian administrative tablets found in Babylon list oil rations for "Jehoiachin, king of Judah" and his sons — the daily allowance has an outside echo, in kind if not in detail.

### The remnant leaves twice

The fall does not end with the burning. Ten thousand are carried away and the poorest are left [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]; the rest of the people are carried away and the poor are left to work the land [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]; and then a remnant remains under Gedaliah, with an oath from the governor himself: "dwell in the land, and serve the king of Babylon; and it shall be well with you" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Within months the governor is murdered at Mizpah by Ishmael, of the seed royal, with ten men, and the remnant — "all the people, both small and great, and the captains of the armies" — arises and comes to Egypt, "for they were afraid of the Chaldees" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The land is emptied a second time, not by deportation but by flight, and not by Babylon's order but by the remnant's own fear. The text states the fear and does not judge it. The book's remnant has been shrinking at every telling — "as yet" in chapter 13, "the remnant that are left" in chapter 19, "the remnant of mine inheritance" in chapter 21 — and here it shrinks to nothing: the land is left to the poor, and the poor themselves leave. What remains in the land at the book's end is no one the book names.

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are about what happens when the sentence the book has been carrying since Manasseh is finally executed — and about how the book refuses to soften any part of it. Knowing the world around the fall keeps the reader from turning it into either an accident of politics or a puzzle of dates. The bands come "according to the word of the LORD" the captives are counted twice over, the gold vessels are cut in pieces "as the LORD had said" and the rebellion that brings the siege is itself set inside the LORD's anger. The house Solomon built is burned, its brass carried away unweighed, and the last king of Judah reigns under a name given by his conqueror. And the book ends where no reader expects it to end: not with a return, not with a restoration, not with a prophet's word, but with a captive king eating bread at a foreign king's table on a daily allowance. The openness is the point. The book leaves its last king alive in Babylon, fed but not freed, and leaves the reader with the same question the text leaves open: whether bread in Babylon is the end of the story, or only the place where the story stops.
