## Overview

Samuel is dead, and Saul has nowhere left to turn. The Philistines are camped at Shunem, Israel at Gilboa, only a few miles apart across the Valley of Jezreel, and Saul is afraid [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He inquires of the Lord, and the Lord answers him not — neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. In his early zeal Saul had put away the mediums and wizards from the land; now, with every lawful channel silent, he sends his servants to find a woman who divines by a ghost, and is told there is one at En-dor [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He disguises himself, takes two men, and crosses the Philistine lines by night to reach her. She fears a trap — Saul himself cut off her kind, and the law makes her art a capital offence — but he swears by the Lord that no punishment will come to her, and she agrees to bring up whom he names [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. "Bring me up Samuel." At the sight of Samuel she cries out and knows her visitor is Saul, and Saul, hearing her description of an old man in a robe, bows down [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The figure speaks the judgement Saul has already heard in life: the kingdom is rent from him and given to David, because he did not obey the Lord against Amalek — and tomorrow he and his sons will be with Samuel, and Israel delivered into the Philistines' hand [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul collapses, having eaten nothing all day or night. The woman kills a fatted calf, bakes unleavened bread, and feeds the king who outlawed her; he eats, rises, and goes away that night, walking toward the morning he has just been told about [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The episode is one self-contained night, and its shape is the shape of a door closing three times before the one forbidden door is opened.

## Ancient World

### Two armies, a few miles apart

The Philistines had pushed into the heart of the land and taken a strong position on the south-western slope of Little Hermon, near Shunem, a little north of Jezreel; Saul gathered Israel on the slopes of Mount Gilboa opposite, parted by the deep Valley of Jezreel, from whose high ground he could see the whole Philistine army [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Gilboa is a range of lofty white-chalk hills rising fifteen hundred feet above the sea [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Shunem is known elsewhere in the Bible as the home of Abishag and of the woman who entertained Elisha [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The geography is what makes the night that follows both possible and desperate: En-dor lay beyond the Philistine camp, so the king had to pass the hostile lines in disguise, in darkness, probably creeping round the eastern shoulder of the hill, a circuit that made the journey about ten miles longer than the straight line [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Jewish tradition names his two companions as Abner and Amasa and says the woman was Abner's mother — which, if true, would explain how she had escaped Saul's earlier purge — but the text names neither the men nor the woman, and this account records the tradition without asserting it [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28].

### Silence from God

Saul's inquiry fails three ways at once: no answer by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Dreams and prophets were recognised channels for hearing God; the Urim, kept in the high priest's breastplate, were the means by which the Lord's judgement was sought before battle. But the text never explains what the Urim and Thummim were or how they functioned, and this account does not pretend to know: their form and mechanism are not established anywhere in Scripture [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. After the massacre of the priests at Nob, the priest in possession of the sacred ephod was with David, not with Saul, and one suggestion is that Saul inquired through a Zadok appointed at Gibeon with an imitation breastplate — but no response was received either way, and the suggestion is only a guess [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. What matters for the story is the silence itself. Every lawful door is closed, the prophet is dead, and the king who once enforced God's law against mediums is left with only the door he closed himself.

### The mistress of the *'ob*

The woman is called in Hebrew a mistress of an *'ob*, a word "which has never been explained with any certainty" [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Scholars have connected it with a word meaning hollow, so that the *'ob* is the hollow thing — the voice of the ventriloquist, whose belly seems to speak — and the ancient Greek translation renders the woman a "belly-talker" [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Others have suggested the *'ob* was a ritual pit for summoning the dead, with parallels in Mesopotamian and Hittite magical texts, or a familiar spirit, a talisman, or a wineskin [^source-wikipedia-witch-of-endor]. One leading older reading is that prophesying by the *'ob* meant calling up a departed spirit from Sheol and obtaining disclosures about one's fate through it — though even that reading leaves open whether the *'ob* was a special spirit in the woman's service or the soul of the dead person itself [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The law's verdict on the practice was clear and decisive: those who dealt in it were not to be suffered to live, and Saul in his early zeal had put the edict into force after it had long lain in abeyance [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The woman knows this. Her first words to the disguised stranger are a warning that he may be laying a snare for her life — and Saul answers with an oath by the Lord, the God whose law he is asking her to break.

### What happened at En-dor?

Readers have argued over this night for two thousand years, and this account does not settle the argument — it sets out the readings so the reader can see them. The first holds that Samuel himself really appeared, brought up not by the woman's power but by the special command of God, and that his voice was certainly heard by Saul. Its holders include the early Jewish writers of Sirach and Josephus, several early Church fathers, and later commentators such as Keil and Wordsworth [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The second holds that what appeared was a demon impersonating Samuel: Luther and Calvin held this, with Ephrem the Syrian before them, on the ground that God would never allow His prophet to be subject to diabolical conjuring, nor the souls of believers to be at the disposal of a sorceress [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. The third holds there was no appearance at all — that the woman played a piece of jugglery, taking revenge on the man who had killed her kind by telling him what everyone already believed, that David would succeed him and that he would fall in battle. Maimonides held this, with a number of later writers [^source-ellicott-1-samuel-28]. Modern scholarship adds further readings: that necromancy was an indigenous practice of family tomb rites which later priestly law sought to delegitimise; that the woman is portrayed sympathetically and the story never explicitly condemns her; that nothing in the narrative implies a demon, since both the woman and Saul understand the figure to be Samuel's ghost [^source-wikipedia-witch-of-endor]. The text itself says the woman saw Samuel, that Saul perceived it was Samuel, and that the figure spoke the judgement of God against Saul. What it does not say is by what power the figure came, or whether the woman's art had anything to do with it — and on that question the ancient and modern readers disagree, and this account leaves their disagreement standing.

### The meal

The chapter does not end with the verdict. It ends with a meal. Saul has eaten nothing all day or night, and he falls full length on the earth at the words he has heard [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The woman, who feared for her life minutes before, urges him to eat for strength for the way; he refuses, is urged by his servants and the woman together, and at last rises and sits on the bed [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. She kills a fatted calf — the best meat in the house — kneads flour, bakes unleavened bread in haste, and sets it before Saul and his servants [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. They eat, rise, and go away that night. It is a strange mercy at the end of a strange night: the outlawed woman feeds the king who outlawed her, and the king who sought the dead eats his last meal before the battle in which he will die. The narrator gives the meal the same flat attention as the apparition, and no more explanation.

## Why This Context Matters

En-dor only makes sense when its world is in view: a king who enforced the ban on mediums, a prophet dead and buried, an inquiry that fails three ways, and a village across enemy lines that can only be reached in disguise and by night. Knowing what an *'ob* might have been, and how fiercely the law condemned the practice, explains the woman's fear and Saul's oath — and why his visit is a fall, not just a mistake. Knowing the three readings of the apparition, and the modern ones beside them, keeps the reader honest: the text says the woman saw Samuel and Saul heard him, and it does not say by what power he came. The episode's judgement is plain either way — the kingdom is given to David, and tomorrow Saul and his sons will fall — but the question of the figure's identity is left where the text leaves it. And the meal matters too: after the verdict, the story pauses for bread, and the king who would not listen to the living prophet is fed by the woman he outlawed, on his last night alive.
