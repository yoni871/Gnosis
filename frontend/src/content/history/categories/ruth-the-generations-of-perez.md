## Overview

The Book of Ruth ends not with a wedding or a birth, but with a list. After the neighbouring women name the child Obed and add that he is the father of Jesse, the father of David, the narrative stops — a paragraph break in the Hebrew text marks the close — and five verses of genealogy follow, opening with "Now these are the generations of Perez: Perez begot Hezron" and running the line through Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, Boaz, Obed, and Jesse to David.[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text]

Ten names carry the reader from Perez, son of Judah by Tamar, to David, king of Israel. Nothing happens in these verses. No one speaks, travels, marries, or dies. The list simply sets the child born at Bethlehem into a line that reaches back to the patriarchs and forward to the throne. That is the whole event, and it is deliberate: the book that began with a famine, a move to Moab, and three deaths ends by showing that the small household of Naomi, Ruth, and Boaz stands in the ancestry of Israel's greatest king.

The list is not a complete family record. Four or five generations cannot cover the centuries the story spans, and the commentators agree that names have been left out and only the leading figures kept. What the list claims is descent and belonging: Boaz belongs to the house of Perez, Obed belongs to that house, and David belongs to it too. The break before the list is visible in the Hebrew text itself: a paragraph division closes the story of the birth before the genealogy begins, and another closes the book at its end. The list is therefore best read as a deliberate coda — an appendix in form, but the book's destination in meaning — rather than as one more episode in the narrative. Read that way, the five verses are the book's final argument — that God's work through two widows and a kinsman at Bethlehem was the making of a dynasty.

## Ancient World

### A list, not a story

Ancient readers would not have been surprised to find a genealogy at the end of a story. Lists of ancestors were a normal part of life in the ancient Near East: they established who a person was, what land and office they could claim, and which family they belonged to. The Bible Odyssey survey of biblical genealogies puts it plainly — such lists established ties between peoples and families, fixed social roles such as priesthood and kingship, and served as a literary frame for a story, a use known from Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Greece as well as Israel.[^source-callender-genealogies-bible] A genealogy was a credential and an argument, not a filing cabinet. That is the first thing to understand about Ruth's closing list: it is doing a job, and the job is to place Obed — and through him, David — in a line that can be named, counted, and trusted.

The Hebrew word that opens the list is *toledot*, "generations," the same word that heads the great genealogies of Genesis: "These are the generations of Adam," "These are the generations of Noah," "These are the generations of Shem."[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text] The lexicon defines it as an account of a man and his descendants, and the Cambridge Bible notes that Ruth uses the standing formula of those Genesis lists.[^source-cambridge-bible-ruth] By borrowing the formula, the writer sets this short list beside the longest spans of biblical history and signals that the same kind of telling is under way: not a scene, but an account of descent.

### Ten names, two halves

The list names ten men: Perez, Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, Boaz, Obed, Jesse, and David. Ten is a round number, and the old commentators noticed the shape. Keil and Delitzsch divide the chain into two halves of five — Perez to Nahshon belonging to the years in Egypt, Salmon to David belonging to the years from the exodus to David's death — and read the symmetry as deliberate, "the number ten as the seal of completeness."[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] The pattern is familiar from Genesis, where ten generations run from Adam to Noah and ten more from Shem to Abraham, each ten marking the turn from one great era to another.[^source-ron-genealogical-list-ruth] David stands in the tenth place, the position of the figure who opens a new age — the age of the monarchy.

Whether the author counted so carefully on purpose is a matter of judgement, and scholars differ. What no one disputes is the count itself, and the count does real work for the reader: it makes the line memorable, complete-feeling, and easy to carry. A list of ten is a list you can recite. The book's ending is built to be remembered.

### The names the list leaves out

Ten lifetimes cannot stretch from Perez to David, and the honest commentators say so. Keil and Delitzsch count only four or five generations for the centuries in Egypt and only two — Nahshon, then Salmon, then Boaz — for more than two hundred and fifty years after the exodus, and conclude that intermediate links "must have been left out."[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown put the gap at three hundred and eighty years between Salmon and David and observe that "whole generations are omitted; the leading personages only are named."[^source-jfb-commentary] Ellicott agrees, calling it a list in which "some unimportant names have been designedly left out."[^source-ellicott-ruth]

This is not a defect the writer tried to hide. Ancient genealogies regularly skipped generations, keeping the heads of tribes, clans, and families and passing over the rest — Matthew does the same openly in his own genealogy, announcing three sets of fourteen generations. The Hebrew word translated "begot" could cover a grandson as well as a son, so that a grandfather could be said to beget a grandchild without any deception being intended. The list's claim is not that these ten men are every father in the line. Its claim is that David descends from Perez through these named men, and that the line is unbroken in the sense that matters: each named man is a real link in a real chain, even if other links go unmentioned.

Where exactly the missing generations fall is still argued. One careful modern study proposes that the gap sits at a single point — Salmon, the figure who appears in no other passage linked to either his father or his son — so that Salmon stands in for many unrecorded generations between the wilderness and the settlement of the land.[^source-ron-genealogical-list-ruth] Others spread the omissions across the list. The dossier records the disagreement rather than settling it: the shape of the list is clear, the precise gaps are not.

### Why Perez heads the list

The list could have begun with Judah, the patriarch. It begins with Perez instead, and the reason is already in the story. At the gate, the elders blessed Boaz with the words, "let thy house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore unto Judah."[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text] The genealogy answers that blessing: here is the house of Perez, generation by generation, down to David. Perez is also the founder of the clan that bears his name, the Perezites, to which Elimelech and Boaz belonged.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth]

There is a deeper echo as well. Perez was born to Tamar, a childless widow who secured a son and a future through her father-in-law Judah. Ruth is a childless widow who secures a son and a future through her kinsman Boaz. The two stories rhyme, and the genealogy's first name quietly points the reader back to the first: the line to David begins with a widow's determination, just as it ends with one. The women themselves — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth — are not named in the list, but Matthew's later retelling names all three of them, and the parallel is part of what the list means, even where it stays silent.

### Why the book ends on David

Ruth and Naomi are the heart of the story, yet the book's last word is David. The ending discloses the story's purpose: this is the family history of David's ancestors. Keil and Delitzsch write that the genealogy shows the book was meant "to give a family picture from the life of the pious ancestors of this great and godly king of Israel."[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] The child born to Naomi at Bethlehem is not only the answer to her emptiness; he is the grandfather of the king, and the book closes by proving it in the form its first readers would have trusted most — a list of names, each one begetting the next, until the line arrives at David.

That ending also reaches beyond David. Every name in the list reappears in the genealogy of Jesus in Matthew's Gospel, which reproduces the same line with Grecised spellings and names Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv] The book of Ruth therefore ends twice over: once at David, and once, for Christian readers, at the king descended from David. But even without that later echo, the point stands on its own terms. A famine, a foreign marriage, a kinsman's redemption, and a birth in a small town turn out to be the beginnings of a dynasty — and the list is the proof, set down name by name, so that no reader could miss it.

## Why This Context Matters

Genealogies look like the dullest part of the Bible, and this one is only five verses long. But the list is the key to the whole book: it tells the reader why the story of Ruth was worth preserving at all. Without it, Ruth is a moving tale of loyalty and redemption in a small town. With it, that tale becomes the ancestry of David, and the loyalty of a Moabite widow becomes part of Israel's royal history. Knowing how ancient genealogies worked — selective, shaped, and meant to establish belonging rather than to record every birth — keeps us from misreading the list as a failed chronology and lets us hear what it actually claims. It also warns against two opposite mistakes: treating the list as a complete chronology, which its own spans forbid, and dismissing it as decoration, which its careful tens and its Davidic endpoint forbid. The list is neither a clock nor an ornament. It is a claim about belonging — that the king came from this house, through these people, by this road — and the book stakes its ending on it.
