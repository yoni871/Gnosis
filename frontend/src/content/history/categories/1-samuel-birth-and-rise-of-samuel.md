## Overview

A childless woman prays at Shiloh, a boy is born and given back to God, a corrupt priesthood hears its sentence, and a child hears God's voice in the night. The first three chapters of 1 Samuel tell how Israel got its prophet. Elkanah of Ramathaim has two wives: Peninnah, who has children, and Hannah, whom he loves but who has none, for "the LORD had shut up her womb" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Year after year the family goes up to Shiloh to worship, and year after year Peninnah provokes Hannah until she weeps and will not eat [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At one visit Hannah prays in silence, her lips moving without sound, and vows that if God gives her a son she will give him to the LORD all his life, no razor ever coming on his head [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. The priest Eli takes her for drunk, then blesses her, and she goes home changed before any answer has come [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. The LORD remembers her, and she names the boy Samuel — "Because I asked the LORD for him" — and when he is weaned she brings him to Shiloh and gives him back, with a bull, flour, and wine, for his whole life [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. She leaves him there with a song. The song turns her own reversal — the barren woman bearing a child — into a picture of how God governs the world: the mighty are broken, the hungry are fed, the poor are lifted from the dust to sit with princes, and the LORD will give strength to his king and exalt the horn of his anointed [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. Against Samuel's growth the narrative sets the corruption of Eli's sons, who seize the sacrifices by force and lie with the women at the sanctuary door, until a man of God announces the end of Eli's house: both sons will die on one day, and God will raise up a faithful priest [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then, in a time when the word of the LORD is rare and visions are few, the LORD calls a boy three times in the night, and the boy who once ran to Eli learns to answer, "Speak, LORD; for Thy servant heareth" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. By the end of the third chapter all Israel, from Dan to Beer-sheba, knows that Samuel is established as a prophet of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World

### Ramah in the hill country

Samuel's story opens in a town whose name means "the height." Elkanah comes from Ramathaim, "the two heights," in the hill country of Ephraim — the full form of the name appears only in the first verse, and everywhere else the town is simply Ramah [^source-biblicaltraining-ramathaim-zuphim]. The name ties the town to Zuph, an ancestor of Elkanah's family, and to the district of Zuph where Saul will later search for his father's donkeys [^source-biblicaltraining-ramathaim-zuphim]. Where exactly Ramathaim stood is not known. Three sites are proposed — Rentis, in the western foothills northeast of Lydda; er-Ram, five miles north of Jerusalem; and Nebi Samwil, the hilltop northwest of Jerusalem where a tomb of Samuel is still venerated — and none of them is certain [^source-biblicaltraining-ramathaim-zuphim]. The uncertainty is worth stating plainly: the town that shaped Samuel cannot be put on a map with confidence. What the text does establish is the family's standing. Elkanah is called an Ephraimite, yet the genealogies of Chronicles list his line among the Levites descended from Kohath; most interpreters take "Ephraimite" to describe where the family lived, since Levites held no tribal territory of their own [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. A family of priests without land, living in the hill country, going up year by year to worship: that is the household Samuel is born into.

### Shiloh and its sanctuary

Each year the family went up to Shiloh, about twenty miles north of Jerusalem, where the tabernacle had stood since the days of Joshua [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. The sanctuary there is described in language that has puzzled readers ever since: it is called a house and a temple, with a doorpost and doors, and Eli sits by the doorpost watching Hannah pray [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Whether a permanent building had replaced the tent of meeting, or whether the old words were simply used loosely for a settled shrine, interpreters still disagree, and the ground cannot settle it: no sanctuary remains have been identified with certainty at Khirbet Seilun, the site of ancient Shiloh [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. The ark itself resided at Shiloh — the sanctuary was the ark's home until chapter 4 [^source-biblicaltraining-ark-of-the-covenant]. What the ground does show is a town with a long history — fortified in the Middle Bronze Age, thinly settled in the Late Bronze Age but still a place of worship, a village of pillared houses and storage jars in the early Iron Age — and then destruction by a fierce fire, usually dated to the mid-eleventh century BC [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Many scholars link that burning with the Philistine victory told in the next chapter, but the archaeology cannot name the attackers, and the Bible never narrates the destruction of Shiloh directly [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. The sanctuary where Samuel grew up was already living on borrowed time.

### A vow, a song, and a corrupt priesthood

Hannah's barrenness was more than private grief. In her world a woman's standing in her husband's household depended heavily on bearing children, and Peninnah's yearly taunting made the pilgrimage itself a trial [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. Her vow reaches for the law of the Nazirite: a person set apart to God, marked by uncut hair, for a fixed time — except that Hannah pledges a lifetime [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. The pattern is familiar from the birth of Samson, whose barren mother was told before his birth that no razor would come on his head, and from the patriarchal stories of Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel, whose barrenness God reversed [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah][^source-bible-odyssey-samson]. The song Hannah sings when she gives Samuel up is the most quoted poetry in the opening of Samuel. Its heart is reversal: warriors' bows broken while the stumbling are given strength, the well-fed hiring themselves out for bread while the hungry are satisfied, the barren woman bearing seven while the mother of many pines away [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. And it ends by looking past Hannah's day altogether, to a king and an anointed one, in a land that has no king yet — the first time the word for "anointed" is used of a king [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. The same words will echo in the Psalms, where God raises the poor from the dust to sit with princes and makes the barren woman a joyful mother, and in Mary's song, which draws on Hannah's to describe the greater work beginning in her son [^source-biblicaltraining-hannah]. Against all of this the narrative sets Eli's sons. They take the sacrificial meat by force, they lie with the women at the sanctuary door, and their father, old and nearly blind, cannot stop them [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The contrast is the point: in the same sanctuary, a child in a linen ephod grows in favour with God and men, while the priests who should guard the altar desecrate it [^source-bible-odyssey-samuel].

### The call in the night

The third chapter opens on a bleak note: "the word of the LORD was precious in those days; there was no frequent vision" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Revelation is rare, and the house that should carry it is under sentence. Into that silence the LORD calls a boy by name, three times, in the night, and each time the boy runs to Eli before he understands who is calling [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The message he is given is the sentence itself: the iniquity of Eli's house will not be expiated with sacrifice or offering for ever [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. In the morning Samuel tells Eli everything, holding nothing back even when Eli lays on him the solemn oath, "God do so to thee, and more also" — the same binding oath formula Ruth spoke on the road to Bethlehem, used here to seal a prophet's first hard message [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Eli accepts it: "It is the LORD; let Him do what seemeth Him good" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The chapter closes with the verdict that matters: the LORD was with Samuel, none of his words fell to the ground, and all Israel from Dan to Beer-sheba knew that Samuel was established as a prophet of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The word that was rare has a voice again, and it belongs to the child Hannah gave back.

## Why This Context Matters

These three chapters are the hinge of Israel's story. The book of Judges ends with a people doing what is right in their own eyes; the books of Samuel end with a king on a throne. Between them stands a prophet, and this is how the prophet arrives: not by appointment or by birthright, but by a barren woman's prayer, a costly vow kept, and a boy who learns to listen in the night. Knowing the world of these chapters — a hill town that cannot be located, a sanctuary whose building cannot be proved, a priesthood rotting from within — keeps the reader from smoothing the story into a legend. It also keeps the reader from missing its argument. God does not wait for the institutions to repair themselves. He raises up a voice outside them, from the household of a woman the world counted empty, and the first thing that voice speaks is judgement on the house that failed. Hannah's song says the pattern plainly: the LORD humbles the proud and lifts the lowly. Samuel is the proof of it, and everything that follows — the demand for a king, the anointing of Saul and David, the whole history of the monarchy — follows from the prophet established in these three chapters.
