## Overview



For three years the rains fail, year after year, and David asks the LORD why. The answer reaches back a generation: the famine is bloodguilt, laid on Saul and his house because Saul tried to wipe out the Gibeonites, a people Israel had sworn to spare [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Gibeonites are not Israelites. They are the remnant of the Amorites, who won a treaty from Joshua by a trick and were put to work as woodcutters and water carriers for the sanctuary instead of being destroyed [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Israel kept that oath for centuries — and then Saul, in his zeal for Israel and Judah, tried to break it. David asks the Gibeonites what atonement will make them bless the LORD's inheritance again. They refuse silver and gold. They ask for seven men of Saul's sons, to be hanged before the LORD at Gibeah of Saul [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David agrees, but he spares one man: Mephibosheth son of Jonathan, because of the oath he swore to Jonathan [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The seven who die are the two sons of Rizpah, Saul's concubine, and five grandsons of Saul — and their bodies are left exposed on the hill from the beginning of barley harvest until the rains come [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What moves the story is a mother's vigil. Rizpah spreads sackcloth on a rock and guards the bodies from birds by day and wild animals by night, until water drops from heaven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. When David hears of it, he fetches the bones of Saul and Jonathan from Jabesh-gilead, where the men of that town had stolen them from Beth-shan, and buries them with the seven at Zelah, in the tomb of Saul's father Kish [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Only then does the chapter say God was entreated for the land. The chapter's second half turns to war: the Philistines fight again, David grows faint in battle, and his men swear he will go out with them no more, lest he quench the lamp of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Three more battles follow, and four giants of Gath fall — one to Abishai, one to Sibbechai, one to Elhanan, and one to Jonathan, David's nephew [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The warrior-king is retired from the field, the house of Saul is buried, and the land is entreated. The appendix has begun, and the book will never return to the battlefield.

## Ancient World



### The Gibeonites and the oath

The Gibeonites were the people of Gibeon, a city in the hill country about six miles northwest of Jerusalem, with three smaller towns allied to it: Kephirah, Beeroth, and Kiriath Jearim [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. The book of Joshua calls them Hivites, one of the peoples of Canaan; the narrator of 2 Samuel calls them the remnant of the Amorites, using the older name broadly for the peoples who lived in the hill country before Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Their story begins with a deception. Hearing what Israel had done to Jericho and Ai, the Gibeonites sent envoys to Joshua's camp at Gilgal dressed as men who had travelled a long way — worn-out sacks, cracked wineskins, patched sandals, dry and mouldy bread — and asked for a treaty [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Joshua and the leaders of Israel made peace and confirmed it by oath, and the text notes pointedly that they did not ask the LORD first [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Three days later Israel learned the truth: these "distant" people were neighbours, a short march away. The assembly grumbled, but the leaders refused to break an oath sworn in the LORD's name, for fear of bringing his wrath on the nation [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Instead the Gibeonites were spared and bound to permanent service as woodcutters and water carriers for the community and for the altar [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. The treaty then obliged Israel to defend them: when five kings attacked Gibeon, Joshua marched all night from Gilgal and routed them, and the sun stood still over Gibeon [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. Gibeon itself is no guess on a map. It is el-Jib, where excavations led by James Pritchard between 1956 and 1962 found jar handles stamped with the city's name, a great rock-cut water shaft with a spiral stair, a tunnel to a spring, and an extensive winery [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites][^source-pritchard-hebrew-inscriptions-gibeon-1959]. The Gibeonites lived on among the Israelites, in Benjamin's territory, never counted as Israelites themselves — a protected, subordinate people whose standing rested on nothing but a sworn word [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites].

### Famine, bloodguilt, and a mother's vigil

In the ancient world a famine was never only weather. Here the text says so directly: three years of famine, and the LORD's answer names a crime — Saul's attempt to destroy a people Israel had sworn to protect. The historical books never narrate Saul's attack; it comes to light only here, in the famine it caused [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. David's question to the Gibeonites uses the language of atonement: what shall I do, and with what shall I make covering, so that you will bless the LORD's inheritance? Their answer refuses the easy settlements. No silver or gold — the debt is not a debt of money. And no general right of vengeance in Israel — they will not become executioners at large. What they ask is specific and terrible: seven men of Saul's sons, exposed before the LORD at Gibeah, the hometown of the king who wronged them [^source-biblicaltraining-gibeonites]. The number seven is the whole of Saul's house as it stands, except the one man an oath protects. Mephibosheth son of Jonathan is spared because David swore to Jonathan, and one oath is set against another: the oath to the Gibeonites is honoured by the handing over, and the oath to Jonathan is honoured by the sparing. The seven die at the beginning of barley harvest, the first grain of the spring, and their bodies remain on the hill [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Rizpah acts. She is Saul's concubine, the mother of two of the dead, a woman who never speaks a word in the Bible. She spreads sackcloth on the rock and stays — from harvest until the rains, driving away the birds by day and the wild animals by night [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. In a world where an unburied body was a disgrace and exposure to scavengers the deepest dishonour, her vigil is both mourning and protest, and it is the only voice in the chapter raised for the dead. It works. When David hears what she has done, he goes to Jabesh-gilead and brings back the bones of Saul and Jonathan, recovered long ago from the wall of Beth-shan where the Philistines had displayed them, and buries them with the seven in the family tomb at Zelah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The burial is the settlement: only after it does the text say God was entreated for the land.

### The giants of Gath

The chapter's second half is a different kind of material: four short battles, told as a list rather than a campaign, against four champions of Gath. Gath was one of the five Philistine cities and the home of Goliath, and these four are presented as his kin — "born to the giant in Gath," in the Hebrew "sons of the Rapha" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Rapha is an old name. The Rephaim were an ancient people of Canaan and the lands east of the Jordan, remembered as a people of great size: Og king of Bashan, the last of them, slept in a bed of iron nine cubits long [^source-biblicaltraining-giants]. Joshua's campaign left some of the tall people alive only in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod, and Gath is where this chapter's four are born. Their weapons mark their scale: Ishbi-benob carries a spear weighing three hundred shekels of brass and a new sword, and Elhanan's victim carries a spear whose shaft is like a weaver's beam — the same image used for Goliath's spear [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The fourth is the strangest of all: a man of great stature at Gath with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot, twenty-four in number, who taunts Israel before Jonathan son of Shimeah kills him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. One detail in these verses is a famous puzzle. The Hebrew says Elhanan slew Goliath the Gittite; the King James Version says he slew the brother of Goliath, following the parallel account in Chronicles, which names the victim Lahmi, the brother of Goliath [^source-biblicaltraining-rapha]. The chapter does not resolve it, and neither does this telling: the difference between the two accounts is part of the record, and the four fall either way — by the hand of David and by the hand of his servants. The battles are the last of their kind. David goes down to the first of them and grows faint, and Abishai has to save him; after that his men swear he will not go out to battle again, "that thou quench not the lamp of Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The king who slew Goliath as a boy now watches the last of Goliath's house fall from behind the lines, and the book's wars are over.

## Why This Context Matters



This chapter is the book's reckoning with two debts: an oath broken and a giant's house still standing. The famine teaches what the rest of the book only implies — that a king's crime can outlive the king, and that a sworn word, once given in God's name, binds the generations after it. Saul's zeal looked like loyalty to Israel; the famine shows it as the breaking of a promise, and the land itself pays until the promise is honoured. Rizpah's vigil teaches the other side of the same truth: honour is restored not by the execution but by the burial, and it is a grieving mother, not a king or a priest, who moves David to give Saul's house its grave. And the giants teach the shape of the book's ending. David the warrior is retired from the field in a single sentence, and the last battles are fought by his men — the promise of a house that will stand for ever is carried now not by the sword but by the king's line. Knowing this world — the oath, the harvest, the exposed bodies, the lamp of Israel — keeps the reader from treating the chapter as two unrelated fragments. It is one closing: the house of Saul buried, the giants fallen, and the land entreated at last.
