## Overview


Solomon's reign begins with a marriage, a mountain of offerings, and a dream. He has just secured the throne his father David left him, and his first act is an alliance: he marries Pharaoh's daughter and brings her to the city of David, while his own house, the house of the LORD, and the wall of Jerusalem are still being built [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. The people still worship at local shrines called high places, because no temple stands yet — and Solomon, who loves the LORD and walks in David's ways, worships at them too [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. He goes to Gibeon, the great high place about six miles northwest of Jerusalem, and offers a thousand burnt offerings on its altar [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. That night the LORD appears to him in a dream and says, "Ask what I shall give thee" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Solomon does not ask for long life, or riches, or the death of his enemies. He asks for an understanding heart to judge God's people, to tell good from bad — in Hebrew, a listening heart [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. The speech pleases the Lord, and the gift is given with interest: wisdom beyond anyone before or after him, riches and honour he never asked for, and long life if he walks as David walked [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. He wakes, stands before the ark in Jerusalem, and feasts with his servants [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the gift is proved in public. Two women come before the king, each claiming the same living child, with no witness to settle it [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Solomon calls for a sword and orders the child divided — and the true mother gives the child away to save its life, revealing herself [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. All Israel hears of the judgment and fears the king, for they see that the wisdom of God is in him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The rest of the story is the fruit of that gift: a court of princes, twelve officers who feed the household month by month, borders at peace from the Euphrates to Egypt, every family safe under its own vine and fig tree, and a king whose wisdom draws visitors from all the earth [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon].

## Ancient World


### A king who asks for ears

In the ancient Near East, a new king was expected to ask the gods for the things kings need: long life, wealth, victory over enemies, sons to follow him. Solomon asks for none of these. He asks for a heart that listens — the Hebrew phrase is *lēb šōmēaʿ*, literally "a listening heart" — so that he can judge a people too many to count [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. It is a strange request, and the story knows it: God is pleased precisely because Solomon did not ask for the usual things, and so gives him the usual things as well, riches and honour beyond any king of his day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The long life, though, comes with a condition — walk in God's ways as David walked, and then the days will be lengthened [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. That condition is the thread that runs through the whole book of Kings. David's own charge to Solomon made the dynasty conditional on obedience, and the same condition will be repeated at the building of the temple and again when the kingdom is torn away [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Solomon's wisdom, then, is not a talent he owns. It is a gift for an office — the wisdom to judge — and the life that goes with it depends on how he uses it. The dream itself is the story's way of saying the gift comes from God and not from Solomon: he wakes and finds it was a dream, and the first thing he does is go to Jerusalem, stand before the ark of the covenant, and offer sacrifices [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text].

### The great high place

Gibeon matters because the temple does not exist yet. The people worship at high places — altars on hills, where men felt nearer to God — because there is no house built for the name of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-jfb-commentary]. Gibeon is the greatest of them, and for good reason: the tabernacle Moses made and its bronze altar are kept there, while the ark rests in a tent David pitched in Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. So the young king honours both holy places in a single day, sacrificing a thousand offerings at Gibeon and then standing before the ark when he wakes [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Gibeon itself is a real place, conventionally identified with the village of el-Jib, where excavators found jar handles stamped with the name Gibeon [^source-pritchard-hebrew-inscriptions-gibeon-1959]. The thousand offerings sound extravagant, but the occasion helps explain them: the king comes with his nobility, most likely at one of the great festivals lasting seven days, and the rank of the offerer and the succession of daily offerings account in part for the scale [^source-jfb-commentary]. The high places will not go away when the temple is built. Long after Solomon, kings will be praised and yet blamed in the same breath — their hearts perfect, but the high places not removed [^source-richelle-cult-centralization-kings]. The note in this chapter is where that long story starts, and the narrator does not hide it: Solomon loves the LORD, only he sacrifices in high places [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text].

### The sword that was never used

The two women who come before the king have no names, no husbands, no witnesses — only one living child between them and a dead one. One says the other took her son in the night; the other says the living child is hers. There is nothing for a judge to work with except the women themselves. Eastern kings judged hard cases in person, and when testimony failed they often appealed to human nature itself, staging an experiment on the feelings of the people before them [^source-jfb-commentary]. Solomon's experiment is the sword: divide the living child in two. It is the true mother who stops him, giving the child away to save its life, while the other agrees to the division — and the king knows his answer [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-bible-odyssey-solomon]. The order is never carried out; it is the test, not the sentence. What the story wants the reader to see is not cleverness but discernment: the wisdom to judge is the wisdom to see whose love is real when nothing else can be proved. And the whole kingdom sees it too. All Israel hears of the judgment and fears the king, because they see that the wisdom of God is in him to do judgment [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Fear is the right word. A king who can see through a lie in a room with no witnesses is a king his people will not trifle with.

### Twelve officers and a full table

Chapter 4 is not a story but a list — and the list is the point. Solomon's court is larger and more specialised than David's: a chief minister, two secretaries where David had one, a recorder who chronicles each day, a commander of the army, priests, a friend of the king, a steward of the palace, and an officer over the forced labour [^source-biblicaltraining-solomon][^source-jfb-commentary]. Beneath them, twelve officers divide the land into districts, each feeding the royal household for one month of the year [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. The revenues come not in money but in produce: thirty measures of fine flour and sixty of meal every day, ten fat oxen, twenty more from the pastures, a hundred sheep, deer and wildfowl besides [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. That is the whole court's food, not the king's table alone — his household, his officers, his bodyguards, his foreign visitors [^source-jfb-commentary]. The officers, the text says, lack nothing. But the list carries its own warning. Some districts follow old tribal lands; others cut across them, joining Canaanite cities to Israelite country [^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. And Judah, the king's own tribe, does not clearly appear among the twelve at all — many readers take this to mean it was administered separately, leaving the northern tribes to carry most of the burden, which may help explain the anger that splits the kingdom after Solomon's death [^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. The officer over the forced labour is Adoniram, the same man the northern tribes will stone when they demand a lighter yoke from Solomon's son [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The ordered kingdom works. It also costs.

### Peace, plenty, and the wisdom of the world

The chapter closes with the picture every reader of Kings remembers: Judah and Israel many as the sand by the sea, eating and drinking and making merry, every man dwelling safely under his vine and under his fig tree, from Dan in the north to Beersheba in the south, all the days of Solomon [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The vine and the fig tree are the ancient image of peace — shade to sit under, fruit of one's own — and the prophets will borrow it again for the peace the kingdom was meant to keep [^source-jfb-commentary]. Solomon's rule runs from the Euphrates to the Philistines and the border of Egypt, and subject kings bring him tribute [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The boundaries echo the land promised to Abraham, from the river to the sea [^source-biblicaltraining-solomon]. Then comes the wisdom itself, measured against the wisdoms of the world: it excels the wisdom of the east and all the wisdom of Egypt, and Solomon is wiser than Ethan, Heman, Chalcol, and Darda, the most famous wise men of his day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. He speaks three thousand proverbs and a thousand and five songs, and discourses on trees from the cedar of Lebanon to the hyssop in the wall, and on beasts and birds and creeping things and fishes [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Kings come from all the earth to hear him. The books of Proverbs, the Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, and two psalms are traditionally attributed to him, and later writings claim his name as well [^source-bible-odyssey-solomon]. The chapter does not prove any of that authorship; it counts the proverbs and maps the range, and leaves the rest to tradition. What it proves is the gift of chapter 3, now visible across a whole kingdom: the listening heart has become an ordered realm, and the whole world has heard of it.

## Why This Context Matters


This chapter is about what a king should want, and what wanting the right thing costs. Solomon asks for ears before he asks for anything else — a heart that listens, so he can judge a people he cannot count — and the story gives him everything else besides, except the one thing it makes conditional: long life, if he walks as David walked. Knowing the world behind the story keeps the reader from turning Solomon into a simple hero. His reign opens with a foreign marriage and worship at the high places, and the same administration that feeds his court month by month is the yoke his son's kingdom will break under. The wisdom is real — the sword that is never used, the mother revealed by her own mercy, the kingdom at peace from Dan to Beersheba — and so is the price. The gift and the warning arrive in the same dream, and the rest of Kings is the story of which one Solomon keeps.
