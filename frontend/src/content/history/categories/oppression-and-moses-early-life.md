## Overview

Exodus opens with Jacob's family settled in Egypt: the sons who came down with him, seventy souls in all. Joseph and his generation die, while their descendants multiply until the land is filled with them. A new king arises who does not know Joseph and fears the growing people, lest in war they join Egypt's enemies and leave. His answer is oppression: taskmasters are set over them, and they build store cities for Pharaoh, Pithom and Raamses, serving in mortar and brick. Yet the more they are afflicted, the more they multiply. The king then orders the Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, to kill the sons at birth. The midwives fear God and let the boys live, so Pharaoh commands all his people openly that every son shall be cast into the river, and every daughter saved alive.

Into this decree a child is born to a Levite man and woman. Hidden three months, he is set in an ark of bulrushes by the river, while his sister watches. Pharaoh's daughter comes down to wash, finds the ark, and has compassion on the weeping child; his sister arranges for his own mother to nurse him. When he grows, he is brought to Pharaoh's daughter, becomes her son, and is named Moses, because she drew him out of the water. Grown, Moses sees an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew and slays him. The next day he finds two Hebrews striving, and when challenged — "Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?" — he fears the thing is known. Pharaoh seeks to slay him, and Moses flees to Midian, where he marries Zipporah and a son, Gershom, is born. In time the king of Egypt dies, Israel cries by reason of the bondage, and God hears their groaning and remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Moses is keeping Jethro's flock when he comes to the mountain of God, Horeb, and sees a bush burning with fire and not consumed. God calls to him out of the bush: the place is holy ground, and he must put off his shoes. God names himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and says he has seen his people's affliction and heard their cry, and will deliver them to a land flowing with milk and honey. Moses is to go to Pharaoh and bring Israel out of Egypt. When Moses asks what name he shall give, the answer is, "I AM THAT I AM". The elders are to go with him to Pharaoh, demanding three days' journey into the wilderness. God says Pharaoh will not let them go except by a strong hand, that he will smite Egypt with wonders, and that after that Israel will not go empty.

Moses still doubts, and God gives him signs: his rod becomes a serpent and a rod again; his hand comes out leprous as snow and is restored; water poured on dry land becomes blood. Moses protests that he is slow of speech, and Aaron the Levite is appointed to speak for him. Moses returns to Jethro, takes leave, and sets out with his wife and sons and the rod of God, warned that Pharaoh's heart will be hardened. On the way, at a lodging place, Zipporah circumcises her son and the LORD lets Moses go. Aaron meets Moses at the mount of God, and together they gather the elders and do the signs before the people. The people believe that the LORD has visited them and looked upon their affliction, and they bow their heads and worship.

## Ancient World

### Egypt and the growing people (Exodus 1)

The chapter lists the sons of Israel who came into Egypt with Jacob, seventy souls in all, Joseph already in Egypt. Joseph dies, and all his brethren and all that generation, but the children of Israel are fruitful and multiply and wax exceeding mighty, and the land is filled with them. The growth of the family into a people is the starting point of everything that follows.

Then a new king arises over Egypt who does not know Joseph. He fears the children of Israel are more and mightier than his people, and proposes to deal wisely with them, lest they multiply and join Egypt's enemies in war and leave the land. Taskmasters afflict them with burdens, and they build for Pharaoh treasure cities, Pithom and Raamses, serving with rigour in mortar and brick. The oppression has the opposite of its intended effect: the more they are afflicted, the more they multiply and grow, and the Egyptians are grieved because of them.

The king's next move is aimed at the newborn sons. He speaks to the Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, and commands them to kill the sons at birth and save the daughters. The midwives fear God and do not do as the king commands; they save the boys alive. Called to account, they answer that the Hebrew women are lively and are delivered before the midwives come. God deals well with the midwives and makes them houses, and the people multiply. The decree is then made public: Pharaoh charges all his people that every son born shall be cast into the river, and every daughter saved alive.

### Moses' birth, flight, and Midian (Exodus 2)

A Levite man takes a Levite wife, and she bears a son, a goodly child, and hides him three months. When she can no longer hide him, she sets him in an ark of bulrushes, daubed with slime and pitch, among the flags by the river. Pharaoh's daughter comes down to wash at the river, sees the ark among the flags, and sends her maid to fetch it. She opens it, sees the weeping child, and has compassion on him. His sister offers to call a Hebrew nurse, and the child's own mother is sent for to nurse him. When he grows, she brings him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he becomes her son, named Moses, because she drew him out of the water.

When Moses is grown, he goes out to his brethren, looks on their burdens, and sees an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew. He looks about, sees no man, and slays the Egyptian. The next day he finds two Hebrews striving, and the one in the wrong answers, "Who made thee a prince and a judge over us? intendest thou to kill me, as thou killedst the Egyptian?" Moses fears the thing is known, and when Pharaoh hears of it he seeks to slay him. Moses flees to Midian and sits down by a well.

The priest of Midian has seven daughters who come to draw water for their father's flock, and the shepherds drive them away, but Moses stands up, helps them, and waters their flock. Their father Reuel hears of the Egyptian who delivered them and calls him to eat bread. Moses dwells with the man, marries his daughter Zipporah, and a son is born, Gershom, "for I have been a stranger in a strange land". In time the king of Egypt dies. The children of Israel sigh and cry by reason of the bondage, and God hears their groaning, remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and looks upon them.

### The burning bush (Exodus 3)

Moses keeps the flock of Jethro his father-in-law and leads it to the backside of the desert, to the mountain of God, Horeb. There the angel of the LORD appears in a flame of fire out of a bush that burns and is not consumed, and Moses turns aside to see. God calls, "Moses, Moses", and when he answers, "Here am I", he is told to put off his shoes, for the place is holy ground. God says, "I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob". Moses hides his face, afraid to look upon God.

The LORD says he has seen his people's affliction, heard their cry, and knows their sorrows, and has come down to deliver them to a good and large land, a land flowing with milk and honey, the place of the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites. The cry of the children of Israel has come to him, and he has seen the oppression with which the Egyptians oppress them. "Come now, and I will send thee unto Pharaoh, that thou mayest bring forth my people out of Egypt." Moses answers, "Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh?" God says, "Certainly I will be with thee; and this shall be a token: when thou hast brought forth the people out of Egypt, ye shall serve God upon this mountain".

Moses asks what he shall say when the children of Israel ask the name of the God of their fathers. God says, "I AM THAT I AM: thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you", and that this is his name for ever, his memorial unto all generations. Moses is to gather the elders and tell them that the God of their fathers has appeared to him and will bring them up out of Egypt to the land flowing with milk and honey. The elders are to go with him to Pharaoh, asking three days' journey into the wilderness to sacrifice. God says plainly that Pharaoh will not let them go except by a strong hand, that he will smite Egypt with wonders, and that after that Israel will not go empty, but will spoil the Egyptians.

### Signs, reluctance, and return (Exodus 4)

Moses answers, "But, behold, they will not believe me, nor hearken unto my voice: for they will say, The LORD hath not appeared unto thee". The LORD asks what is in his hand, and Moses says, "A rod". The LORD says, "Cast it on the ground", and it becomes a serpent, and Moses flees from before it. "Put forth thine hand, and take it by the tail", and it becomes a rod in his hand again, that they may believe that the LORD God of their fathers has appeared to him. Then the LORD says, "Put now thine hand into thy bosom", and his hand comes out leprous as snow; put in again, it comes out restored as his other flesh. If they will not believe the first sign, they will believe the second; and if they will not believe those two, he shall pour river water on dry land, and it shall become blood.

Moses says, "O my Lord, I am not eloquent, but I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue". The LORD says, "Who hath made man's mouth? have not I the LORD? Now therefore go, and I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt say". Moses says, "O my Lord, send, I pray thee, by the hand of him whom thou wilt send". The anger of the LORD is kindled, and he says, "Is not Aaron the Levite thy brother? I know that he can speak well, and behold, he cometh forth to meet thee. Thou shalt speak unto him and put words in his mouth, and I will be with thy mouth and with his mouth. He shall be thy spokesman unto the people, and thou shalt be to him instead of God. Take this rod in thine hand, wherewith thou shalt do signs".

Moses returns to Jethro and asks to return to his brethren in Egypt, and Jethro says, "Go in peace". The LORD says to Moses in Midian, "Go, return into Egypt: for all the men are dead which sought thy life". Moses takes his wife and sons and the rod of God and returns to Egypt. The LORD tells Moses to do all the wonders before Pharaoh, but he will harden Pharaoh's heart, and Moses shall say, "Thus saith the LORD, Israel is my son, even my firstborn: Let my son go, that he may serve me". On the way, at a lodging place, the LORD meets Moses and seeks to kill him. Zipporah circumcises her son with a sharp stone and says, "Surely a bloody husband art thou to me", and the LORD lets him go. The LORD sends Aaron to meet Moses, and he meets him in the mount of God and kisses him. Together they gather the elders, Aaron speaks the words and does the signs, and the people believe that the LORD has visited them, and they bow their heads and worship.

## Why This Context Matters

These four chapters set the whole book of Exodus in motion. They begin with a people who have grown from a family into a nation and are being crushed by the king who fears them, and they end with that same people believing, bowing their heads, and worshipping because they have heard that the LORD has visited them. Between those two points the narrative introduces the man through whom the deliverance will come, the God who sends him, and the demand that will be laid before Pharaoh. The oppression, the birth and flight of Moses, the burning bush, and the signs are not separate episodes: each one prepares the next. The midwives' refusal and the mother's hiding of her child preserve the deliverer; the years in Midian remove him from Egypt until the time is right; the bush gives him his commission and the name of the one who sends him; the signs give him what to show the people and Pharaoh.

The passage also reaches backward as it moves forward. God remembers his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob, and the promises made to the patriarchs are named again at the bush: the God of the fathers is the one who has seen the affliction and will bring the people to the land flowing with milk and honey. What is promised in these chapters is not yet done. Pharaoh has not yet been faced, the people have not yet left Egypt, and the demand to let them go is still to be spoken. The chapters close with belief and worship, and the confrontation that will test that belief is still ahead.
