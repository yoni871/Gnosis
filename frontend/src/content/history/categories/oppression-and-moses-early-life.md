## Overview

Exodus opens by closing the patriarchal age. The sons of Jacob are named once more, seventy souls in all; then, in a single verse, Joseph dies, and all his brethren, and all that generation. But Israel is fruitful and multiplies until the land is filled with them. A new king arises who does not know Joseph. Fearing a people growing mightier than his own, lest they join Egypt's enemies in war and leave, he sets taskmasters over them to build store cities, Pithom and Raamses, serving in mortar and brick and in the field. Yet the more they are afflicted, the more they multiply. He orders the Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, to kill the sons at birth; they fear God and let the boys live, and God makes them houses. His last measure is public: every son shall be cast into the river.

Into this decree a child is born to a man and woman of Levi. Hidden three months, he is laid in an ark of bulrushes among the flags by the river while his sister watches. Pharaoh's daughter comes down to wash, finds the ark, and has compassion; his sister fetches his own mother to nurse him, and when he grows he is brought to Pharaoh's daughter, becomes her son, and is named Moses, because she drew him out of the water. Grown, Moses sees an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew, slays him, and hides him in the sand. Challenged the next day — "Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?" — he flees to Midian when Pharaoh seeks to slay him. There he marries Zipporah, and a son, Gershom, is born. In time the king dies, Israel cries, and God remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Moses is keeping Jethro's flock when he comes to Horeb, the mountain of God, and sees a bush burning with fire and not consumed. God calls him out of the bush, tells him to put off his shoes, for the place is holy ground, and names himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He has seen his people's affliction and will deliver them to a land flowing with milk and honey; Moses is to go to Pharaoh and bring Israel out. Asked what name he shall give, the answer is "I AM THAT I AM", a name for ever and a memorial unto all generations. The elders are to demand three days' journey to sacrifice, and God says Pharaoh will not let them go except by a mighty hand, that he will smite Egypt with wonders, and that Israel will spoil the Egyptians.

Moses still doubts, and God gives him signs: the rod becomes a serpent and a rod again; the hand comes out leprous as snow and is restored; water poured on dry land becomes blood. Moses protests that he is slow of speech, and Aaron the Levite is appointed to speak for him. Moses takes leave of Jethro and sets out with his wife and sons, warned that Pharaoh's heart will be hardened and that Israel is God's son, even his firstborn. On the way, at a lodging place, the LORD meets Moses and seeks to kill him, until Zipporah circumcises her son and the LORD lets him go. Aaron meets Moses at the mount of God; they gather the elders, do the signs, and the people believe and worship — a faith the next chapter will test.

## Ancient World

### Egypt and the new king

The passage names no Pharaoh and gives no date. "A new king... which knew not Joseph" most naturally denotes a king ruling by different principles, though a dynasty change is the commonest explanation; whether he is an early Eighteenth-Dynasty king, a Hyksos king, or a Ramesside king is not decided by the text. Keil and Delitzsch's Hyksos discussion is now superseded: the Fifteenth Dynasty is established from archaeology at Tell el-Dab'a. The older identifications of the king are reception, never established fact.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The oppression is three kinds of labour: mortar, brick, and service in the field. The taskmasters are "princes of burdens", and Pithom and Raamses are store cities. Pithom, "House of Atum", is disputed between Tell el-Maskhuta and Tell er-Retaba, current opinion favouring Retaba; Raamses is probably Pi-Ramesses, the Delta capital of Ramesses II at Qantir and Tell el-Dab'a. The name Raamses was not in use before the thirteenth century BCE, the strongest datum for a late setting. The Septuagint adds a third city, On (Heliopolis). The Rekhmire tomb painting shows foreign workers making bricks under taskmasters — general corvée labour, not Israelites, cited as background only.[^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-rekhmire-tomb][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The Hebrew text counts seventy souls; the Septuagint counts seventy-five, reflecting the expanded list of Joseph's descendants and Acts 7:14. Augustine explains it by the counting: the seventy-five counts the whole period while Joseph lived, including great-grandchildren who could not have existed at the entry.[^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, were Hebrew women, heads of the midwifery profession; Josephus's claim that they were Egyptian spies is rejected by Calvin and Keil and Delitzsch. Their answer to Pharaoh is disputed. Augustine held that God rewarded their mercy and fear of God, not their lie; Calvin agreed they lied and sinned, and God bore with the fault in mercy. Lightfoot, noted in Calvin's editor's footnote, held their words were no lie but a glorious confession of faith. The text records only that they feared God, saved the children, and that God made them houses — families and posterity.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus]

### Moses' birth, the basket, and Midian

A Levite man takes a Levite wife — Amram and Jochebed, named only later — and the child is hidden three months, then laid in an ark of bulrushes daubed with bitumen and pitch. The word is *tēbâ*, the same noun as Noah's ark: twenty-eight occurrences, all in the Noah and Moses narratives. Keil and Delitzsch read the choice as a deliberate recall of Noah's ark. Miriam watches; Pharaoh's daughter finds the ark. The text does not name her: Josephus calls her Thermuthis, rabbinic tradition Bithiah, Jubilees Tharmuth. She names him Moses, "because I drew him out of the water". The name is Egyptian — the lexicon gives *mes*, *mesu*, "child, son", as the probable origin — while the Hebrew explanation is a theological reading.[^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Moses sees an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew, slays him, and hides him; the next day comes the rebuke, "Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?" Whether the killing was justified is disputed. Augustine held it a crime, yet showing a heart capable of virtue; Philo called it "a pious action"; Keil and Delitzsch stand between them — holy zeal, but not without sin, and premature.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-philo-life-of-moses][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Moses flees to Midian and sits by a well, where he helps the seven daughters of the priest of Midian and marries Zipporah. The priest is Reuel in Exodus 2:18 and Jethro in Exodus 3:1: Keil and Delitzsch take Reuel as the proper name and Jethro as a title; Clarke conjectured Reuel dead and Jethro his successor. Neither is established. Jethro is plausibly a Kenite. The Midianites descend from Abraham through Keturah; the exact site of Midian is uncertain. The flight parallels the Tale of Sinuhe, a courtier who flees Egypt and returns — literary, not textual. The forty-year figures come from Acts 7, not Exodus. Jubilees gives its own chronology, thirty-six years in Midian, recorded as tradition. Whether the king who dies in Exodus 2:23 is the "new king" or a successor is unknown.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-jvl-kenite][^source-sinuhe-gardiner][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### The burning bush and the divine name

Moses keeps Jethro's flock and comes to Horeb, the mountain of God. Horeb is the general name for the mountainous district of which Sinai is a part; no modern peak can be identified with it. The angel of the LORD appears in a flame of fire out of a bush; the speaker is then identified as Jehovah himself. The bush is a *sĕneh*, a thorny bush. Tradition reads it as Israel in the fire of affliction, not consumed because God is in its midst: Keil and Delitzsch take the bush as despised Israel and the fire as affliction; Philo reads the same allegory. Clarke adds a threefold emblem as reception. The reading is traditional, not established.[^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-philo-life-of-moses][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Moses asks, "What is his name?" — because a name discloses nature and deed. The answer, *'ehyeh 'ăsher 'ehyeh*, is "I AM THAT I AM" in the King James Version. Calvin reads it as future with present force, "I will be what I will be"; Keil and Delitzsch hear the absolute God acting with unfettered liberty. The Septuagint renders it "I am The One Who Is". Exodus 3:15 names YHWH, the God of the fathers. Yet Exodus 6:3 says, "by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them". The standard resolution — the patriarchs knew the name, not yet its full redemptive meaning — is probable, not established.[^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-bible-hub-interlinear][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The elders are to ask Pharaoh for three days' journey to sacrifice. Keil and Delitzsch read the request as a genuine first step, not a deception. The King James Version's "no, not by a mighty hand" mistranslates the Hebrew, which reads "except by a mighty hand". The spoiling turns on a word: *šā'al* means "ask, demand", not "borrow" — settled. The moral framing is not: Clarke reads the spoiling as recompense for unpaid labour, Keil and Delitzsch as a gift of divine favour. Both are presented; neither is adjudicated.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### The signs, the return, and the lodging-place

Moses doubts Israel will believe him, and God gives three signs: the rod becomes a serpent and a rod again; the hand comes out leprous as snow and is restored; Nile water poured on dry land becomes blood. The signs convince Israel, strengthen Moses' faith, and later confront Pharaoh; a voice is ascribed to them. The rod-serpent sign recurs before Pharaoh in Exodus 7:10, but by Aaron, not Moses; that difference is recorded, not suppressed. Moses' plea that he is slow of speech denotes a defect of utterance, or difficulty in fluent Egyptian after long disuse — not stammering, which the text does not specify. God answers, "Who hath made man's mouth?" and appoints Aaron as spokesman, Moses to be to him instead of God.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-bible-odyssey-moses][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Moses takes leave of Jethro and sets out, warned that Pharaoh's heart will be hardened and that Israel is God's son, even his firstborn. The hardening is ascribed to God ten times and to Pharaoh himself ten times, Pharaoh's preceding God's; the pattern is developed across Exodus 7–14. On the way, at a lodging place, the LORD meets Moses and seeks to kill him, until Zipporah circumcises her son with a stone. The probable reading, Keil and Delitzsch's and Calvin's, is that Moses had neglected the circumcision of his younger son, probably Eliezer, and that "a bloody husband" is addressed to Moses. The Septuagint renders it wholly differently: an angel meets him, and Zipporah says the blood of the circumcision is staunched. Jubilees names the agent Mastema — a tradition absent from the Hebrew text. The cause is inferred, not stated, and the passage is flagged as disputed. Aaron meets Moses at the mount of God; they gather the elders, do the signs, and the people believe. Their faith did not stand the test of the next chapter.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### The date and the Egyptian record

No date can be assigned to the oppression, the birth of Moses, or the Exodus. The early date, about 1446 BCE, rests on 1 Kings 6:1 and a literal reading of the 430 and 400 years; it must explain Raamses as anachronistic. The late date, in the thirteenth century, rests on the name Raamses itself; it must explain the 480 years as schematic. The 430 and 400 years are not reconcilable into one literal chronology. Augustine's 144 years is his own computation, recorded as tradition.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

One fixed point exists. The Merneptah Stele, from about 1208 BCE, says "Israel is laid waste; his seed is not", with the determinative of a people: Israel was a people-group in Canaan by then. It is the earliest extra-biblical mention of Israel and a terminus ante quem for any Exodus date; it does not date the Exodus or mention Moses. The material is background, from secondary summaries only. No Egyptian text names Moses, the enslavement, or the Exodus: the archaeology makes the world intelligible without corroborating the events.[^source-merneptah-stele][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

## Why This Context Matters

These four chapters set the whole book in motion, and they do it by joining a people's suffering to a God's self-disclosure. The oppression explains why a deliverance was needed at all: a family has become a nation, and the nation has become enslaved under a king who fears its growth. The birth and flight of Moses explain how the deliverer was prepared outside Egypt, in exile, until the time was right. The burning bush is the hinge: there the God of the fathers discloses his name and his purpose together, so that the deliverance is not only an escape from Pharaoh but the keeping of a covenant made with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The signs, the reluctance, and Aaron's appointment show that the commission is carried by a reluctant man with a spokesman, not by a hero confident in his own speech.

The context also guards the reading. Knowing that no Pharaoh is named and no date is given keeps the reader from fastening the story to a king the text never identifies. Knowing that "borrow" should read "ask", that the midwives' answer and Moses' killing are genuinely disputed, and that the lodging-place passage is the most obscure in these chapters keeps the reader from treating every episode as morally plain. And knowing that no Egyptian record names Moses or the Exodus keeps the archaeology in its place: background that makes the world of the story intelligible, not proof of the story itself. The chapters close with belief and worship, and the confrontation that will test that belief is still ahead.
