## Overview



The war with Syria begins with a borrowed axe. The sons of the prophets, Elisha's community, have outgrown their dwelling, and at the Jordan a borrowed axe head slips off its handle into the water; Elisha casts in a stick, and the iron swims [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-sons-of-the-prophets]. Then the war turns strange. The king of Syria camps against Israel, but every camp is betrayed before it is pitched: Elisha tells the king of Israel the words the Syrian king speaks in his bedchamber, and the king saves himself "not once nor twice" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Syrian king sends a great host by night to seize the prophet at Dothan, and Elisha's servant wakes to find the city compassed with horses and chariots — until his eyes are opened and he sees the mountain full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The army is struck blind, led into Samaria, and then — instead of being slain — feasted and sent home, so that the bands of Syria come no more into the land of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. But the mercy does not hold. Benhadad gathers all his host and besieges Samaria, and famine does what no army could: an ass's head sells for fourscore pieces of silver, and a woman tells the king on the wall that she and another woman agreed to eat their sons [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-ben-hadad]. The king rends his clothes and swears Elisha's head shall not stand on him this day [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elisha answers with a price: "To morrow about this time shall a measure of fine flour be sold for a shekel, and two measures of barley for a shekel, in the gate of Samaria" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. A lord scoffs, and is told he shall see it with his eyes but not eat of it. The deliverance comes at night, by a noise, to four leprous men at the gate, who ask, "Why sit we here until we die?" and find the Syrian camp empty [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The people spoil the tents, the prices are as Elisha said, and the scoffing lord is trodden to death in the gate [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The ministry closes at Damascus, where Elisha tells Hazael he shall be king over Syria — and weeps at the evil he will do — and in Judah, where Jehoram and Ahaziah reign under Ahab's shadow and end together at Jezreel, where the next chapter strikes [^source-biblicaltraining-hazael][^source-biblicaltraining-damascus].

## Ancient World



### The war you cannot see

The king of Syria's war against Israel is fought first in secret. He takes counsel with his servants and camps "in such and such a place," but the man of God in Israel warns the king, and the ambush finds no one there [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Syrian king suspects a traitor; his servant tells him there is none — Elisha, the prophet in Israel, tells the king of Israel the words the Syrian king speaks in his bedchamber [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The scene shows what a prophet meant in this world: not only a preacher, but the king's intelligence, the man who knows the enemy's plans before the enemy moves. The response is to seize the prophet himself. A great host comes by night to Dothan, horses and chariots compassing a small town, and Elisha's servant despairs at the sight [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elisha's answer is the chapter's centre: "Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The servant's eyes are opened, and he sees the mountain full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha — the same sight that parted Elijah and Elisha at the Jordan, now standing as an army round the successor [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Syrian host is then struck blind and led, unsuspecting, into the midst of Samaria, the capital itself [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. It is a victory without a battle, and it ends without blood: the king of Israel asks twice whether he should smite them, and is told to set bread and water before them instead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### A feast instead of a sword

The feast at Samaria is the strangest victory in the book. The blinded army, led into the enemy's capital, is not slaughtered or ransomed but fed — great provision is prepared for them, they eat and drink, and they are sent away to their master [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The text says the result plainly: the bands of Syria came no more into the land of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The mercy works. And then, in the next verse, Benhadad gathers all his host and besieges Samaria anyway [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-ben-hadad]. The two outcomes sit side by side, and the book offers no explanation for the turn. The feast shows what Elisha's war is: the prophet does not defeat the enemy by force but by feeding him, and the enemy's raids stop — until a full siege, a different kind of war, begins. The contrast is the point. The hidden war is won by sight and mercy; the open war is fought by hunger, and no warning saves the city from it. The reader is left with both facts, and with the question the text will not answer: why the mercy held for the raiding bands and not for the besieging host.

### Hunger at the gate

A siege in the ancient world was a war against a city's food, and Samaria's famine is told in prices. An ass's head — meat no one would choose — sells for fourscore pieces of silver, and a fourth part of a cab of dove's dung for five [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The gate, where grain was normally sold, becomes the place where the famine is measured and where the deliverance will be priced. On the wall the king meets the famine's worst face: a woman tells him she and another woman agreed to eat their sons, and the second has hidden hers [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The text states it without comment, and the king's answer is to rend his clothes — the people see sackcloth within upon his flesh — and to swear the prophet's head shall not stand on him this day [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The oath is sworn in the old self-imprecatory formula, the binding idiom of the Old Testament's great oaths. Elisha, sitting with the elders, calls the king "this son of a murderer" and orders the door held against the messenger [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The chapter ends with the king's patience exhausted and the prophet's life demanded — and the answer comes in the next chapter, spoken before the siege lifts.

### The lepers' good tidings

The deliverance of Samaria is discovered by four leprous men at the entering in of the gate. Under the law the leper dwelt alone, outside the camp; here they sit outside the besieged city, with death on every side — the famine in the city, death if they sit still, and the Syrian camp as their only hope [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Their reasoning is plain: "Why sit we here until we die? ... if they save us alive, we shall live; and if they kill us, we shall but die" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the twilight they find the camp empty. The Lord had made the Syrians hear a noise of chariots and horses, and they fled, believing Israel had hired the kings of the Hittites and of the Egyptians against them [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The lepers eat and drink, carry off silver and gold and raiment, and hide it — then stop themselves: "We do not well: this day is a day of good tidings, and we hold our peace" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The good tidings reach the city through the lowest men in the siege, carried by the porters to the king's house, while the king himself, risen in the night, suspects an ambush [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Only when scouts follow the fleeing host to Jordan, down a road full of garments and vessels cast away in haste, does the city go out and spoil the tents — and the prices are as Elisha said, "according to the word of the LORD" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The lord who scoffed is set over the gate, and the people tread upon him there and he dies, as the man of God had said [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### Damascus and the usurper

Elisha's last journey takes him to Damascus, the capital of Aram, Israel's most persistent northern rival [^source-biblicaltraining-damascus]. Benhadad is sick, and sends his servant Hazael with forty camels' burden of every good thing of Damascus to ask whether he shall recover [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-hazael]. Elisha's answer holds two words together: "Thou mayest certainly recover: howbeit the LORD hath shewed me that he shall surely die" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He then fixes his gaze on Hazael until the man is ashamed, and weeps — because he knows the evil Hazael will do to Israel, the strong holds burned, the young men slain, the children dashed, the women with child ripped up [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-hazael]. Hazael protests that he is only a dog, a servant, too small for such deeds; Elisha answers that the LORD has shown him Hazael as king over Syria [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The next day Benhadad is dead, smothered with a wet cloth, and Hazael reigns in his stead [^source-biblicaltraining-hazael]. The scene is the execution of a commission given a generation earlier, when the LORD told Elijah at Horeb to anoint Hazael king over Aram [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Elijah never anointed him; Elisha announces his kingship by word alone. Outside the Bible, Assyrian records agree that Hazael was a usurper: Shalmaneser III's inscriptions call him "son of a nobody" and record the campaign against him in 841 BC, and the Aramaic stele found at Tel Dan in 1993 and 1994, usually attributed to Hazael, claims the deaths of a king of Israel and a king of the house of David [^source-biblicaltraining-hazael]. He ruled Damascus for about four decades, the most formidable king the city produced, and held Israel under heavy pressure through the reigns of Jehu and Jehoahaz [^source-biblicaltraining-hazael].

### Two kingdoms, one house

The chapter closes by turning south, to a Judah that has married into the enemy. Jehoram of Judah walks in the way of the kings of Israel, as did the house of Ahab, for the daughter of Ahab is his wife — Athaliah, daughter of Omri, mother of the next king [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Yet the LORD would not destroy Judah for David his servant's sake, "as he promised him to give him alway a light, and to his children" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The lamp promised to David (1 Kings 11:36) is the reason the kingdom survives a king who has joined Ahab's house. Edom revolts in Jehoram's days and makes its own king, and Libnah revolts at the same time; Joram's night attack fails, and Edom is free "unto this day" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Ahaziah, two and twenty years old, reigns one year and walks in the way of the house of Ahab, "for he was the son in law of the house of Ahab" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He goes with Joram to war against Hazael at Ramoth Gilead — the fortress east of the Jordan where Ahab himself died — and Joram is wounded and returns to Jezreel to be healed, and Ahaziah goes down to see him there, because he is sick [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-ben-hadad]. The chapter ends with both kings at Jezreel, one wounded and one visiting, and the next chapter will strike them there together.

## Why This Context Matters



These three chapters are about a war that is won twice in opposite ways — once by mercy, once by hunger — and about a ministry that ends in tears at a foreign court. Knowing the world of the Syrian wars keeps the reader from treating the siege as a simple miracle story. The hidden war is won by a prophet who feeds his enemies; the open war is won by a noise in the night that empties a camp no army could take; and the news of it comes through four lepers at the gate, the last men anyone would send. The chapter also sets the terms for everything that follows: the kingship announced weeping at Damascus will oppress Israel for a generation, and the two kingdoms, joined by marriage into Ahab's house, will fall together at Jezreel. The mercy, the famine, and the tears belong to one story, and the story does not resolve them — it leaves the feast and the siege standing side by side, and asks the reader to hold both.
