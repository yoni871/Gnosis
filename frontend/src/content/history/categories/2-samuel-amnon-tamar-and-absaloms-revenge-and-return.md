## Overview



David's house, promised to stand for ever, begins to tear itself apart from within. After Nathan's sentence — "the sword shall never depart from thine house" — the first blow falls not on a battlefield but at home. Amnon, David's firstborn, loves his half-sister Tamar, Absalom's full sister, and falls sick with wanting her [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. His cousin Jonadab, "a very subtil man," gives him the plan: feign sickness, ask the king to send Tamar to cook for him, and David, suspecting nothing, sends her [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Tamar comes, kneads and bakes in his sight, and Amnon sends every man out and seizes her in the chamber. She pleads with him — "do not force me; for no such thing ought to be done in Israel" — and he will not listen [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then his love turns at once to hatred greater than the love, and he has his servant put her out and bolt the door, though she tells him the casting out is a greater evil still [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. She tears the robe of divers colours that marked her as a virgin daughter of the king, puts ashes on her head, and goes away crying, and her brother Absalom takes her into his house, where she remains desolate [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-tamar]. David hears it all and is very wroth — and does nothing [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The king who sentenced the rich man in Nathan's parable does not judge his own son. Absalom says nothing to Amnon, neither good nor bad, for two full years, and then kills him at a sheepshearing feast at Baal-hazor and flees to his grandfather's kingdom of Geshur for three years [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. David's heart turns back to his exiled son, and Joab, seeing it, sends to Tekoa for a wise woman who tells the king a parable of two sons and turns his own verdict back on him [^source-biblicaltraining-tekoa]. Absalom is fetched home — but may not see the king's face, and does not for two full years [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. At last he forces an audience by burning Joab's barley field, and the king kisses him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The kiss closes the chapter. The next opens on chariots and fifty runners.

## Ancient World



### The king's house at Hebron

Amnon and Absalom were both born at Hebron, where David ruled Judah for seven and a half years before he took Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Amnon was the eldest, son of Ahinoam of Jezreel; Absalom was the third son, son of Maacah, daughter of Talmai king of Geshur [^source-biblicaltraining-tamar][^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. The second son, Chileab, never appears in the story again. Tamar was Absalom's full sister, daughter of the same Maacah, and Amnon's half-sister [^source-biblicaltraining-tamar]. Everything in these chapters turns on that family tree: the assault is a half-brother's act against a half-sister, the revenge is a full brother's act for a full sister, and the exile is a flight to the mother's father's court. As the eldest son, Amnon stood first in the line of succession, and with Chileab gone from the record, Absalom was probably now David's oldest surviving son after Amnon's death [^source-biblicaltraining-absalom]. Nobody in these chapters speaks of the throne. But the order of the sons is the background against which the killing of the firstborn and the return of the third son must be read, and it is the background the revolt of chapter 15 will bring into the open.

### Geshur, the kingdom beyond the Jordan

When Absalom fled, he went to a kingdom most readers have never heard of. Geshur was a small Aramean kingdom east of the upper Jordan, in the country now called the Golan Heights, beside Maacah and the territory of Bashan [^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. Israel never drove its people out: Joshua counts their land within the allotment, then adds plainly that the Israelites did not drive out the people of Geshur and Maacah, so they lived on as a separate people with their own kings into the time of the monarchy [^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. David's marriage to Maacah, daughter of Talmai king of Geshur, was made while he reigned at Hebron, and it is commonly understood as a diplomatic marriage of the kind ancient kings regularly made — it tied David to a neighbouring ruler on the northeastern edge of Israelite territory [^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. The tie became Absalom's refuge. His grandfather was the king there, and Geshur lay outside David's reach, so it was a natural place to run to [^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. Three years is a long exile, and the text lets the reader feel it: David mourns every day, and at last his heart turns back to the son he cannot fetch home. Geshur is not mentioned again in the biblical story after the time of David [^source-biblicaltraining-geshur]. It enters the narrative as a marriage alliance and leaves it as an exile, and both tell the same truth about the ancient world: a king's family was also his foreign policy, and a grandson could always go home to his mother's people.

### Law, honour, and the avenger

Tamar's plea is the most quoted speech in these chapters, and the hardest to read. "Do not force me," she says, "for no such thing ought to be done in Israel" — and then, "speak unto the king; for he will not withhold me from thee" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The first half is plain: the act is a forcing and an outrage in Israel, in the same words Genesis uses for Dinah. The second half is difficult, because the law forbade marriage between half-siblings [^source-biblicaltraining-tamar]. Whether Tamar hopes the king can dispense with the law, or speaks to delay Amnon, or appeals to a father she knows to be indulgent, the text does not say. What the law of Deuteronomy required of a man who forced a virgin was marriage without divorce; if that law covers a case like this, which is debated, Amnon does the opposite, casting her out, and Tamar names the casting out as the greater evil [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The second chapter turns on a different institution, the avenger of blood: the family demands the surviving son be handed over for the brother he killed, which would quench the widow's last coal and leave her husband neither name nor remainder [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The avenger was the kinsman whose duty was blood for blood, and the woman's case is that the family's claim, pressed to the letter, would destroy the very house it claims to defend. Sheepshearing, the occasion Absalom chooses for the revenge, was a festive season — Judah goes up to his sheepshearers in Genesis — and a feast with the king's sons gathered and wine flowing is the setting a killing "when Amnon's heart is merry with wine" requires [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### Mourning, parables, and the wise woman

Tamar's answer to the casting out is public and legible: ashes on her head, the rent garment, her hand on her head, and she goes on crying [^source-biblicaltraining-tamar]. The robe she tears is explained by the narrator as the dress of the king's virgin daughters, and the same rare Hebrew expression describes Joseph's robe — in both cases a father's marked favour worn by a child, and a brother's violence ending with the garment torn or taken away [^source-biblicaltraining-tamar]. The woman of Tekoa uses the same language of mourning as a disguise: Joab tells her to feign a mourner, wear mourning apparel, and not anoint herself with oil, as a woman long mourning for the dead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Tekoa was a town in the hill country of Judah, about ten miles south of Jerusalem, where farmland gives way to the wilderness [^source-biblicaltraining-tekoa]. The text does not say why Joab looked to Tekoa in particular [^source-biblicaltraining-tekoa]. Her parable mirrors Nathan's: both draw a verdict from the king before he knows the case is his own, and both turn the verdict back on him [^source-biblicaltraining-tekoa]. The difference is the sender. Nathan was sent by the LORD; Joab put the words in the woman's mouth, and David detects his hand at once [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What she asks for is not justice in the abstract but a way back for the banished: God, she says, devises means so that his banished are not expelled from him for ever [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David grants half of it. Absalom comes home and does not see the king's face for two years — until he burns a field to force the audience, and the king answers with a kiss [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are where Nathan's sentence stops being a prophecy and starts being a history. The sword does not enter David's house from outside, through a Philistine or an Ammonite; it is drawn inside the family, by a son against a sister and a brother against a brother, while the king who could judge a rich man over a lamb cannot judge his own firstborn. Knowing the world of the chapters — a marriage alliance that becomes an exile, a law that forbids what Tamar proposes and commands what Amnon refuses, a parable that once brought a king to confession and now brings him only to a compromise — keeps the reader from treating the story as a private tragedy. It is a public one: the house promised for ever is the house where the sword does its work. And it keeps the reader from missing the chapter's question, which is still open at the kiss. A father can fetch a son home without facing what the son did, and a son can be kissed without being forgiven. The next chapter will show what that kind of peace is worth.
