## Overview

Deuteronomy 26–28 closes the law corpus and stages the covenant's public acceptance at the threshold of the land. The three chapters form a single arc: confession, ceremony, and sanctions. Chapter 26 gives two set speeches for life in the land — the firstfruits presentation with its historical creed, and the third-year tithe declaration — and ends with a mutual avowal between the LORD and Israel. Chapter 27 instructs Israel, on the day of crossing the Jordan, to set up plastered stones inscribed with the law, build an altar of whole stones, and divide the tribes between Mount Gerizim for blessing and Mount Ebal for curse, while the Levites pronounce twelve curses answered by the people's "Amen." Chapter 28 states the covenant's sanctions in full: blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience, the curses occupying nearly four-fifths of the chapter.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The firstfruits rite presupposes settlement. The offerer takes the first of all the fruit of the earth in a basket, goes to the place the LORD chooses to place his name there, and the priest sets the basket before the altar. The spoken history begins, "A Syrian ready to perish was my father, and he went down into Egypt, and sojourned there with a few, and became there a nation, great, mighty, and populous," and moves from affliction and crying out to deliverance "with a mighty hand, and with an outstretched arm" and the gift of "a land that floweth with milk and honey." The father is probably Jacob, whose years in Aram lie behind the word "Syrian"; Abraham is a live alternative, not settled here. The offerer then rejoices "in every good thing," together with the Levite and the stranger.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The third-year tithe goes to the landless — the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow — "that they may eat within thy gates, and be filled." The giver declares negatively what he has not done: he has not eaten of the tithe in mourning, taken it for unclean use, or given it for the dead. The chapter closes with the avowal: "Thou hast avouched the LORD this day to be thy God," answered by "the LORD hath avouched thee this day to be his peculiar people," the same *sĕgullâ* language as Exodus 19:5, now applied to the settled people.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The blessings of chapter 28 are concrete and this-worldly — city and field, body and land and cattle, basket and store, coming in and going out, victory, rain, lending without borrowing, "the head, and not the tail." The curses mirror the blessings and then outrun them, moving from disease and defeat through the futility series, in which every effort is frustrated, to siege, scattering, and a return to Egypt by ships — the exodus reversed. The chapter is conditional both ways: "if thou shalt hearken diligently … all these blessings shall come on thee," and "if thou wilt not hearken … all these curses shall come upon thee."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Two questions about these chapters remain debated among scholars, and this page presents both sides rather than settling either. Deuteronomy 27:4 reads "Ebal" in the Masoretic Text and "Gerizim" in the Samaritan Pentateuch, and which is earlier is disputed. The dependence of Deuteronomy 28 on Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty is likewise debated: direct borrowing on one side, mediation through Northwest Semitic tradition or a variety of texts on the other. No year is given in Deuteronomy 26–28, so the category is undated; the 672–622 BCE window for Deuteronomy 28:20–44 follows only if the direct-borrowing argument is accepted, which it is not here.[^source-anderson-giles-samaritan-pentateuch][^source-steymans-deuteronomy-28-tell-tayinat][^source-quick-curses-deuteronomy-aramaic]

## Ancient World

### The confessions of the settled life

Deuteronomy 26 is in three movements. The firstfruits rite (verses 1–11) centres on the basket, chosen place, priest, and spoken history. The text does not name the chosen place — Jerusalem, the later traditional identification, is not stated here — and the offerer rejoices with the Levite and the stranger.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The third-year tithe (verses 12–15) is the local, charitable tithe, eaten "within thy gates" by the landless, unlike the annual tithe brought to the central place (Deuteronomy 14:22–29). The giver declares his ritual cleanness, not a boast: the hallowed things have been brought away, the commandments have not been transgressed or forgotten, and nothing has been eaten in mourning, used for unclean purposes, or given for the dead. The prayer asks the LORD to "look down from thy holy habitation, from heaven, and bless thy people Israel, and the land which thou hast given us."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The avowal (verses 16–19) closes the law corpus of Deuteronomy 12–26 and opens the covenant ceremony: Israel avouches the LORD as God, and the LORD avouches Israel as his peculiar people and "an holy people unto the LORD thy God." The confessions look backward to the creed and forward to settled life, and the avowal transitions to the Shechem ceremony of chapter 27.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### The ceremony on the two mountains

Deuteronomy 27 is instruction for a single day: "on the day when ye shall pass over Jordan." The stones are set up, plastered, and inscribed "very plainly". The altar is of "whole stones" with "no iron tool" upon them, the same rule as Exodus 20:25, and the offerings are burnt and peace offerings, eaten with rejoicing. Verses 9–10 mark the people's new status. Six tribes stand on Gerizim to bless and six on Ebal to curse; the Levites pronounce twelve curses, each answered "Amen" by all the people. The curses cover hidden sins — secret idolatry, dishonouring parents, moving a landmark, misleading the blind, perverting justice for the vulnerable, sexual offences, secret murder, murder for reward — and close with the whole law. The chapter pronounces curses only; the blessings are implied by the stations and stated in full in Deuteronomy 28:1–14.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The two mountains were already named for blessing and curse before the crossing (Deuteronomy 11:29–30). The Shechem landscape — two mountains flanking the valley, matching the tribal stations — is geographically secure, while the installations, where the stones and altar stood, are disputed. No excavation report was read directly for this dossier.

Verse 4's mountain is the chapter's central textual problem. The Masoretic Text reads "in mount Ebal"; the Samaritan Pentateuch reads "Gerizim"; the Old Latin agrees with the Samaritan Pentateuch; and a Qumran fragment reported by Charlesworth reads Gerizim. The other Qumran witness, 4QDeut-f, has a lacuna at the place-name and attests neither reading. The prior claim that the Samaritan Pentateuch and all the Dead Sea Scrolls read "Ebal" is false. Anderson and Giles report Kartveit's conclusion that Gerizim is the earlier reading and Ebal a polemical change, while stating the opposite — a Samaritan change to Gerizim — as the live alternative; Nodet's thesis has not won broad support. Pummer warns against using the Gerizim reading as the criterion for a uniquely Samaritan reading. Segal's 2025 article treats the "Gerizim Composition" as unrelated to sectarian concerns, but only its abstract was read. No archetype is chosen here.[^source-anderson-giles-samaritan-pentateuch][^source-segal-jordan-ebal-vetus-testamentum-2025]

Joshua 8:30–35 narrates the fulfilment: Joshua builds the altar "in mount Ebal," of whole stones with no iron lifted upon them, writes the law on the stones, stations half the people facing Gerizim and half facing Ebal, and reads the blessings and cursings. The place-name follows the Masoretic Text, so the variant affects the narrative's geography as well as the law's wording.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### The blessings and the curses

Deuteronomy 28 gives the covenant's sanctions in two movements, unbalanced: fourteen verses of blessing and fifty-four of curses. The curses of verses 16–19 invert the blessings of verses 3–6 almost word for word. The first curse section (verses 20–44) runs through disease, cosmic reversal — heaven brass, earth iron, rain as powder and dust — defeat, exposure of the dead, madness and blindness, and the futility series. The second section (verses 45–68) raises the stakes: pursuit "till thou be destroyed," the yoke of iron, a distant nation "whose tongue thou shalt not understand," siege, the eating of children, scattering among all peoples, and the return to Egypt by ships — "Thou shalt see it no more again."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The curses of Deuteronomy 28 are the most severe sanctions in the Pentateuch, and this page does not soften them: the eating of children in siege (verses 53–57), the LORD rejoicing over destruction (verse 63), and the return to Egypt (verse 68) are stated as written. How the published page presents them is an editorial decision for Yonatan; the dossier does not present the curses as hyperbole.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Leviticus 26 is the other great blessings-and-curses chapter, presented here as a parallel, not as source or borrower; no direction of dependence is claimed. It gives blessings for obedience (verses 3–13), then escalating curses in four waves (verses 14–39) — heaven as iron and earth as brass, the eating of sons and daughters, and scattering — and ends in confession and covenant remembrance (verses 40–45) that Deuteronomy 28 lacks; Deuteronomy 28 ends instead with the return to Egypt.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### The treaty comparison: Deuteronomy 28 and Esarhaddon

The setting is seventh-century Judah under Assyrian domination: Manasseh of Judah was a tribute-paying vassal and military ally of Esarhaddon and swore loyalty oaths including the Esarhaddon Succession Treaty (EST). The treaty, SAA 2 no. 6, dates to 672 BCE: preamble, divine witnesses, stipulations of loyalty to Assurbanipal, the oath, the great-gods curse in §56, and a ceremonial curse section. At least eight tablets from Nimrud (Calhu), sealed with three divine seals of Assur, were found in the throne room of the temple of Nabû; a further exemplar, found in 2009 at Tell Tayinat, manuscript T-1808, a display tablet 40 × 26 cm, pierced for fixing, in the inner sanctum of Building XVI. The Tayinat exemplar proves the treaty was imposed on western vassals, not only Median bodyguards at court.[^source-oracc-saa2-6-esarhaddon-treaty][^source-steymans-deuteronomy-28-tell-tayinat]

Steymans argues the topic sequence of EST §56 and Deuteronomy 28:20–44 is "almost identical" and unique, with parallel syntax — optative divine curses alternating with natural-force curses — and the shared predicate "to strike" (EST *mahasu*; Deuteronomy 28 *n.k.h*-Hiphil in verses 22, 27, 28, 35). He concludes the similarities are "due to direct borrowing from the EST," dating Deuteronomy 28:20–44 to 672–622 BCE. From the Tayinat exemplar's §§54–55, invoking Levantine deities, he infers a copy stood in the Jerusalem temple, where Judean scribes could read cuneiform — an inference, not an excavated fact: no Jerusalem copy is known. His article was read in part; its later tables and conclusion were not reached.[^source-steymans-deuteronomy-28-tell-tayinat]

Quick argues the other way, documenting the same EST §§39–42 // Deuteronomy 28:26–35 parallels but showing the futility-curse form — maximum effort, minimal gain — is standard Northwest Semitic literary form, attested in 43 biblical instances and 19 Old Aramaic instances (Sefire KAI 222, Tell Fakhariyah, Bukān, 9th–8th centuries BCE), closer to the biblical world than the Mesopotamian texts. Deuteronomy "builds upon and amplifies the Akkadian," she writes, but "mediates between the traditions of the East and West." Hutton and Crouch, read only in abstract, find the passages most likely to represent direct translation lack the procedural similarities of the Tell Fekheriyeh bilingual inscription, and argue reliance "was unlikely to have been limited to a single exemplar."[^source-quick-curses-deuteronomy-aramaic][^source-hutton-crouch-deuteronomy-translation-assyrian-treaties]

The dossier's position: the parallels are established and the mechanism is debated — direct borrowing, mediation between traditions, and reliance on a variety of texts are all live, and the 672–622 BCE dating follows only from Steymans' argument. The futility-curse form is background to Deuteronomy 28:30–33 and 38–42, not evidence of a date.[^source-quick-curses-deuteronomy-aramaic]

### What archaeology does and does not show

The key archaeological fact here is a negative one: there is no direct archaeological evidence for the Deuteronomy 27 Shechem ceremony — no inscription, no plastered stones, no altar at Shechem tied to it. The Samaritan temple on Gerizim is a later, Hellenistic-period structure, not evidence for the Deuteronomy 27 altar. Absence of direct evidence does not settle the historical question either way.

What archaeology does supply is the treaty context: the Tell Tayinat and Nimrud tablets establish that Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty was a real, displayed instrument imposed on western vassals in the seventh century BCE. That frames the dependence question without deciding it: a displayed treaty in the Levant makes scribal contact plausible, but contact is not borrowing, and futility-curse parallels in ninth- and eighth-century Aramaic inscriptions show the same forms circulating in the Northwest Semitic world. The Shechem landscape is secure while the installations remain disputed, and any map pin must say traditional or proposed, never verified event location.[^source-steymans-deuteronomy-28-tell-tayinat][^source-quick-curses-deuteronomy-aramaic]

## Why This Context Matters

Deuteronomy 26–28 is where the covenant stops being only law and becomes a public act with stated consequences. The firstfruits confession turns a harvest offering into a retelling of the exodus, so that the settled life in the land is remembered as gift rather than achievement; the tithe declaration makes care for the landless part of the same worship. The Shechem ceremony then stages the covenant where the whole people can see and hear it — stones inscribed "very plainly," tribes divided across a valley, curses answered "Amen" — and the blessings and curses of chapter 28 spell out what obedience and disobedience will bring, in this world, in concrete terms of rain, harvest, health, and exile.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The context also matters because the two hardest questions about these chapters are still open, and a reader should know that. Whether the mountain of Deuteronomy 27:4 was Gerizim or Ebal in the earliest text is disputed between witnesses, and whether Deuteronomy 28 borrowed directly from Esarhaddon's treaty or mediated a shared curse tradition is debated between scholars who have read the same evidence. Presenting both sides honestly is not indecision: it is what the evidence supports. The curses themselves are severe, and the page reports them as written rather than softening them. What endures from these chapters is the shape of the covenant they present — blessing and curse set before the people as a choice, with the whole community as witness.[^source-anderson-giles-samaritan-pentateuch][^source-steymans-deuteronomy-28-tell-tayinat][^source-quick-curses-deuteronomy-aramaic]
