## Overview

Exodus 35–40 narrates the building of the tabernacle: the execution, after the covenant's renewal in chapter 34, of the instructions given on the mountain in chapters 25–31. Moses gathers the congregation and re-issues the Sabbath law before any work begins, adding the ban on kindling fire on the Sabbath day — even the sacred work of building is bounded by the Sabbath. Then comes the call for offerings, freewill only: "whosoever is of a willing heart" is to bring gold, silver, and bronze; blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and fine linen; goats' hair; rams' skins dyed red and the skins called *tahash*; acacia wood; oil and spices; onyx stones and gems. The list repeats the one given on the mountain. The people answer with their own jewellery — bracelets, earrings, rings — with yarn the women spin with their hands, and with goats' hair; the rulers bring the stones and the spices.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

Over the work stand two named men: Bezalel son of Uri, son of Hur, of Judah, and Aholiab son of Ahisamach, of Dan. Bezalel is filled with the Spirit of God in wisdom, understanding, and knowledge — wisdom here meaning technical skill in craftsmanship — and both men are given the ability to teach others. The wise-hearted are summoned to make all that the LORD commanded. Moses hands the offerings to the craftsmen, and the people keep bringing them every morning until the workers report that the people bring much more than enough, and Moses proclaims a restraint through the camp: no one is to bring any more. What they have is sufficient for all the work, and too much.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-bdb-lexicon]

Chapters 36–39 then repeat the instructions of chapters 25–31 nearly verbatim — the curtains and the boards, the veil and the screen, the ark and its cover, the table and the lampstand, the altars, the basin, the courtyard, and the priestly garments — under the recurring refrain "as the LORD commanded Moses". The metals are counted under Ithamar son of Aaron: gold 29 talents 730 shekels; silver 100 talents 1,775 shekels, from 603,550 men at a half-shekel each; bronze 70 talents 2,400 shekels. The finished work is brought to Moses, who inspects it, finds it done as commanded, and blesses the people.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

On the first day of the first month of the second year, Moses erects the tabernacle in the commanded order. The anointing commanded in chapter 40 does not take place then, but at Aaron's consecration. Then the cloud covers the tent and the glory of the LORD fills the tabernacle, so that Moses cannot enter, and the cloud and the fire guide every journey thereafter. "So Moses finished the work" — words that echo the completion of creation. The book ends not with a law or a march but with God's presence dwelling among Israel. When exactly this happened is debated: the text gives only the relative date, and the early and late datings of the exodus are both on record.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-childs-exodus][^source-sarna-exodus]

## Ancient World

### The Sabbath first

The building account opens with the Sabbath, and the placement is deliberate. The Sabbath law is re-issued after the covenant's restoration, strengthened by the ban on kindling fire, a rule to be observed even in connection with the work for the tabernacle. Sabbath work carries the death penalty, and the day is "a sabbath of rest". The re-issue is the hinge between the covenant's renewal and the building: the work is bounded by the Sabbath, the sign of the covenant it serves. The offerings that follow are freewill only. The gift is a *terumah*, a heave-offering, and the gold a *tenuphah*, a wave-offering, the two terms overlapping here. The gold and silver, on the commentators' reading, came from Egyptian and Amalekite spoil and from trade with neighbouring peoples — a probable account, not a stated one.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-jfb-commentary]

### The craftsmen

Bezalel's name means "in the shadow (protection) of El", and Aholiab's means "Father's tent". The pairing joins Judah, the leading tribe, with Dan — a traditional observation drawn from the tribal pairing itself, not a statement of the text. What the text states is the endowment: Bezalel is filled with the Spirit of God in wisdom, understanding, and knowledge, and in all workmanship, and given the ability to teach, that is, to instruct labourers under his supervision. The account names them again at the end as the makers of all that the LORD commanded Moses. The people keep bringing offerings every morning until the craftsmen report that the people bring much more than enough, and Moses proclaims a restraint through the camp. The surplus is read as the people's covenant fidelity made visible.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law]

### The work, in order

The construction repeats the instructions nearly verbatim — same order, measurements, materials, and phrasing — and the repetition is the point: in it the commentators trace the exact conformity of the execution to the command. The refrain "as the LORD commanded Moses" recurs through chapters 39 and 40. The work runs from the outside in: ten linen curtains of 28 by 4 cubits with cherubim and fifty gold clasps, eleven goats'-hair curtains of 30 by 4 cubits with fifty bronze clasps; coverings of rams' skins dyed red and of *tahash* skins; forty-eight boards of acacia wood, ten by one and a half cubits, with silver sockets; the veil on four pillars and the door hanging on five. Bezalel personally makes the ark — two and a half by one and a half by one and a half cubits, gold within and without, mercy seat and two cherubim — the most holy thing, expressly his work. Then the table, the lampstand of one talent of pure gold with seven lamps, the incense altar, the bronze altar, and the court, a hundred by fifty cubits.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

The laver is bronze from the mirrors of the women at the door — polished metal — women of pious character, on the commentators' reading, not priestesses. The garments are made in full, the gold plate "HOLINESS TO THE LORD" following the Egyptian seal-ring practice. The Urim and Thummim are not mentioned in chapter 39.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

### The counting of the metals

The accounting is made by the Levites under Ithamar son of Aaron — a reckoning of weight and worth. The totals are gold 29 talents 730 shekels; silver 100 talents 1,775 shekels from 603,550 men at a half-shekel, a bekah, each; bronze 70 talents 2,400 shekels. The silver total implies the census of 603,550 men aged twenty and upward, and proves a talent contained 3,000 shekels. The hundred talents were cast into a hundred sockets — ninety-six for the boards and four for the veil pillars — and the 1,775 shekels made the hooks, the silvering of the capitals, and the connecting rods. The quantities are modest beside the treasures of the ancient East; the materials came from Egyptian spoil and Arab caravan trade. The building took about six months.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

### Egypt and the wilderness

The Israelites came out of Egypt with the materials and, on the commentators' reading, the skills for the work. Egypt cultivated every description of art and handicraft from the earliest times, and the Israelites could have acquired the ability required under their two inspired master-builders. Egyptian women carried mirrors to the temples and are shown at the spindle on the monuments; cloth of golden tissue is found rolled about mummies. One division of labour needs care. The text attributes the spinning to the women — the women spun the yarn and the goats' hair, the cloth dyed in the yarn as in Egypt — but it does not say who wove. Keil and Delitzsch hold the woven cloths were prepared by men, because artistic weaving was learned in Egypt. That is his inference, not the text's statement. The spices came from Arab caravan trade through the desert.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary]

### Portable sanctuaries

The tabernacle's defining feature against every fixed temple of the ancient Near East is its portability. Philo calls it a movable temple; Josephus, a movable and ambulatory temple. The text makes every part carriable: staves in rings, the altar hollow with boards, the court on pillars with pins and cords. Portable sanctuaries of this kind existed in the Late Bronze Age world; the war tent of Ramesses II at Kadesh is the most striking parallel reported — second-hand, and structural only.[^source-philo-life-of-moses][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The closest material parallel is the tent-shrine at Timna, in the Arabah: a small Egyptian shrine to Hathor at a copper mine, built under Seti I, taken over by Midianites in the twelfth century, who broke Hathor's images, raised standing stones, and hung it with dyed linen and wool, with a gilt-headed copper serpent in its innermost chamber. This rests on secondary accounts; the primary report was not read. Moses' in-laws were Midianites, but whether Israel's tent-shrine tradition drew on Midianite practice has no direct evidence. Timna shows a portable, tented desert sanctuary existed in the right world and the right centuries — not that the Israelites built such a shrine.[^source-timna-tent-shrine][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### The Septuagint's different order

The Greek version orders the construction account differently: the craftsmen and the surplus first, then the garments, then the curtains and boards, then the furniture, then the altar, washbasin, and court, then the metals account with the delivery and blessing, and finally the erection and the glory. Whether the rearrangement reflects a different Vorlage, a translator's reordering, or a different translator is unresolved. The renderings differ too: *tahash* becomes "blue skins" and Oholiab "Eliab"; the bekah is "one drachma"; the women at the door are "the women who fasted". Two additions stand out: the bronze altar is made from the fire-pans of Korah's rebels, and the remaining gold into utensils for ministry. The identity of *tahash* is genuinely uncertain: the lexicon suggests probably the dugong, with sheep-skin and Egyptian leather as alternatives; one commentary suggests the dugong or dolphin of the Red Sea; the King James Version says "badgers' skins".[^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### The date of the exodus

The text gives a relative date: the first day of the first month of the second year. The absolute date is debated. The early date — traditionally 1446 BCE — places the tabernacle in the Late Bronze Age I; the late date, under Ramesses II, in the Late Bronze Age II, contemporary with the Timna shrine's Egyptian period. The Merneptah Stele, dated to Merneptah's fifth year, about 1208 BCE, sets a firm limit: it reads "Israel is laid waste and his seed is not" — and where Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yanoam take the determinative for a city, Israel takes the determinative for a people, the earliest reference to Israel outside the Bible. It does not settle the exodus date: both dates precede 1208. The late date leaves little room for the forty years of wandering, the conquest, and the settlement before 1208 — an argument, not a proof.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-merneptah-stele]

### How the ancients read the tabernacle

Philo reads the tabernacle as a copy of the world: the four materials are the four elements, the seven lamps the seven planets, the twelve breastplate stones the zodiac. Josephus repeats the scheme, dividing the tabernacle into land, sea, and heaven — his reading, on Whiston's note, deriving from Philo. These are traditional reception readings, not the text's own meaning. Jubilees dates the lawgiving to the first year of the exodus, the third month, the sixteenth day. Augustine places the dedication after the law given on the fiftieth day after Passover, reading Passover as a type of Christ and Moses' not entering the land as prefiguring Joshua. The New Testament reads the tabernacle as a copy and shadow of the heavenly, fulfilled in the Word who "tabernacled" among us — the texts are the claim; the typological reading is traditional. One influential modern reading, reported second-hand, sees the tabernacle as a portable Sinai, extending the Sinai experience by a permanent cultic institution, so that the presence which once abode on the mountain now dwells in the sanctuary and accompanies Israel — a probable reading, not an established one.[^source-philo-life-of-moses][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-sarna-exodus][^source-childs-exodus]

## Why This Context Matters

This category is the answer to the one before it. There the instructions were given on the mountain and the people below broke faith; here the people give freely, morning after morning, until they are told to stop, and skilled hands carry out the work exactly as commanded. The same materials return, the same measurements, the same recipes — but now the telling is of making rather than of commanding, and the refrain running through the building is that it was done just as the LORD commanded Moses. The pattern shown on the mountain is the standard, Moses' inspection is the test, his blessing is the acceptance, and the glory is the divine seal.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary]

The context also keeps the reader honest about what is known and what is not. The Egyptian background — the mirrors, the spinning, the golden tissue, the seal-rings — makes the craftsmanship plausible as the work of people who had lived in Egypt; the Timna shrine shows that tented desert sanctuaries existed in the same world and the same centuries; the Septuagint shows that even the order of the account was not fixed in antiquity; and the date of the exodus remains debated, bounded only by the Merneptah Stele. The book of Exodus, which began with a people in bondage, ends with a dwelling among them and a cloud that leads their journeys — and the journey from Egypt to Sinai is now a journey that can continue with that presence going along.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-timna-tent-shrine][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus][^source-merneptah-stele]
