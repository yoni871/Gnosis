## Overview

The Philistines have won. On Mount Gilboa, at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley, the men of Israel flee and fall, and the Philistines kill Saul's three sons — Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua — and wound the king himself with arrows [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Saul asks his armourbearer to run him through, so that the uncircumcised will not abuse him, and when the man refuses, Saul falls on his own sword [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. The next day the Philistines strip the dead, take Saul's head and his armour, and send the news through their land to their idols and their people; the armour goes into the house of their gods, and the head is fastened in the temple of Dagon [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. The men of Jabesh Gilead, the town Saul rescued at the start of his reign, march through the night, take the bodies down, and bury the bones under the oak at Jabesh, fasting seven days [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. Then the Chronicler gives his verdict, and it is the whole of Saul's story in this book: Saul died for his transgression against the LORD, for the word he did not keep, and for asking counsel of one that had a familiar spirit, and he enquired not of the LORD — therefore the LORD slew him, and turned the kingdom to David the son of Jesse [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. All Israel gathers to David at Hebron, saying, "Behold, we are thy bone and thy flesh", and remembering that even when Saul was king, David was the one who led them out and brought them in [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. The elders make a covenant with him before the LORD, and they anoint him king over Israel, according to the word of the LORD by Samuel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. David takes Jerusalem from the Jebusites, and Joab, who goes up first, wins the chief command [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. The mighty men are named — Jashobeam and his spear, Eleazar standing in the barley field, the three who break through to the well of Bethlehem, Benaiah and his lion in the pit on a snowy day — and the roll closes with Uriah the Hittite [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Then the tribes come, band by band, to Hebron: the Benjamites who could use both hands, the Gadites with faces like lions, the men of Issachar who understood the times, Zebulun with fifty thousand men not of double heart [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. They eat and drink with David for three days, and the neighbours bring bread and meat and wine in abundance, for there was joy in Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World

### The ridge where a kingdom fell

Mount Gilboa is a ridge of hills at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley in northern Israel, overlooking the descent toward Beth Shan and the Jordan Valley [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. It is remembered as the place where King Saul and three of his sons died in battle against the Philistines, and David's lament for the fallen made the name a byword for that defeat [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The ridge forms a northeastern spur of the hill country of Samaria, curving from the town of Jezreel southeast toward the Jordan Valley, its summits rising to somewhat more than 1,600 feet above sea level [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. Across the valley to the north stands the Hill of Moreh, with Shunem on its southern slope and Endor beyond it, and to the east lies Beth Shan, which guarded the route down to the Jordan crossings [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. Gilboa thus overlooked one of the main passages through the land [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The Philistines had gathered at Shunem while Saul assembled Israel on Gilboa [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The battle went against Israel, the men fled and fell on the ridge, and the Israelites living on the far side of the valley and across the Jordan abandoned their towns, which the Philistines then occupied [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa].

### The god who received the king's head

Dagon was an ancient Semitic god worshipped from the middle Euphrates to the Mediterranean coast, and in the Bible he is the chief god of the Philistines [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. Scripture mentions three of his temples: the temple at Gaza that Samson pulled down, the temple at Ashdod where the image of Dagon fell before the ark of the covenant, and the temple where the Philistines hung Saul's head [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. Dagan was worshipped for well over a thousand years before the Philistines arrived in Canaan: texts from Ebla in Syria call him lord of Tuttul, and letters from Mari show Dagan as a leading god of the region [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. At Ugarit the storm god Baal is repeatedly called son of Dagan, and the eastern temple on the city's acropolis is usually assigned to Dagan [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. The Philistines evidently adopted him as their principal god when they settled on the southern coastal plain in the 12th century BC [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon]. The Philistines were a people of Aegean origin who settled on the southern coastal plain early in the 12th century BC, organised around five cities — Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron — each with its own ruler [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. Egyptian records place their arrival within the upheavals that ended the Late Bronze Age, naming the Peleset among the Sea Peoples [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. A cemetery found at Ashkelon in 2016 held early Iron Age remains with European ancestry [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. To hang a defeated king's head in Dagon's temple was to announce that the god had won the war — and neither account says where the temple stood, so the dossier does not say either [^source-biblicaltraining-temples-of-dagon].

### The town that remembered

Jabesh Gilead was an Israelite town in the hill country of Gilead, east of the Jordan, whose history is closely bound up with the tribe of Benjamin and with Saul [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. At the start of his reign Saul rescued the town from Nahash the Ammonite, who had besieged it [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. When Saul died on Mount Gilboa, the fighting men of Jabesh recovered his body from the wall of Beth Shan and buried it [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The journey meant crossing the Jordan valley and taking the bodies down from a fortified city held by the enemy, travelling through the night [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. At Jabesh they burned the bodies, buried the bones under a tamarisk tree, and fasted seven days in Samuel's account, while the Chronicler says only that they buried the bones under the oak and fasted seven days [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The burning was loyalty to the king who had saved the town, not the normal Israelite practice [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The ancient name survives in the Wadi Yabis, a stream that flows west from the hills of Gilead and enters the Jordan from the east, but the exact site is still debated, and no inscription has confirmed the town's name [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead].

### The anointing at Hebron

Anointing was the central act in making a king in Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Samuel anointed Saul privately, then David in Bethlehem, and the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. David was anointed again by the men of Judah at Hebron and then by the elders of all Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Because the king had been set apart by God, his person was sacred [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Anointing was also used to install rulers elsewhere in the ancient Near East, but in Israel the rite was understood in terms of the LORD's choice and the gift of his Spirit [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. At Hebron the rite is also a covenant: the elders come, David makes a covenant with them before the LORD, and they anoint him king over Israel, according to the word of the LORD by Samuel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. The Hebrew verb is *māšaḥ*, "to smear" or "to spread on", and from it comes *māšîaḥ*, "anointed one", which reaches English as "Messiah" [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The Chronicler tells the anointing without the seven-and-a-half-year reign over Judah alone that precedes it in Samuel: in his telling the kingdom turns whole, from Saul's house to David's, in a single gathering [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. Jonathan, Saul's son and heir, had already yielded the throne to David by covenant [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan].

### The city that would not open

The Jebusites were one of the peoples living in Canaan before and during Israel's settlement of the land, best known as the inhabitants of Jerusalem, which the Bible sometimes calls Jebus after them [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. David finally captured it, made it his capital, and later bought from Araunah the Jebusite the threshing floor that became the site of the temple [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. The Jebusite city occupied the narrow southeastern ridge of later Jerusalem, above the Kidron Valley, and its water came from the Gihon Spring at the foot of the eastern slope [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. David promised the chief command to whoever led the attack, and Joab son of Zeruiah went up first and won the post [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites][^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. Joab, David's nephew and the commander of his army, had already led David's troops in Judah [^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. How Joab got in is not stated in either account: Samuel's account mentions a water channel as the route, but excavations from the 1990s onward have found that the vertical shaft discovered in 1867 was probably not accessible in the Canaanite period, so exactly how the attack worked remains uncertain [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites].

### The men who made the king

The mighty men are presented as the men who made David king [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Jashobeam lifts his spear against three hundred slain at one time; Eleazar stands in a parcel of ground full of barley while the people flee [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. The three break through to the well of Bethlehem, and David pours the water out to the LORD rather than drink it [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Benaiah slays two lionlike men of Moab, a lion in a pit on a snowy day, and an Egyptian five cubits high [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. The roll closes with Uriah the Hittite, and names men from outside Israel without comment [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. The men who came to David at Ziklag were Saul's own brethren of Benjamin, armed with bows, able to use both the right hand and the left [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-ziklag]. Ziklag, in the far south, was the town Achish king of Gath gave David while he was a fugitive from Saul [^source-biblicaltraining-ziklag]. The Gadites had faces like the faces of lions and were as swift as the roes upon the mountains, and they crossed the Jordan in the first month, when it had overflown all his banks [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Amasai answered for the men who came to the hold: "Thine are we, David, and on thy side, thou son of Jesse" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Then the tribes come to Hebron with their numbers: Judah 6,800, Simeon 7,100, Levi 4,600, Benjamin only 3,000, Ephraim 20,800, Zebulun 50,000, and 120,000 from across the Jordan [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. Issachar is singled out for understanding — men that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do — and Zebulun for being not of double heart [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text]. They come with a perfect heart to make David king, and eat and drink with him for three days [^source-mechon-mamre-1-chronicles-masoretic-text].

## Why This Context Matters

These three chapters are about how a kingdom changes hands — and how a new king is made by the men who come to him rather than by the army he raises. Knowing the world of Gilboa keeps the reader from treating Saul's death as a mere battle report: the head in Dagon's temple is a theological claim by the victors, the night march of Jabesh is a debt repaid across a generation, and the Chronicler's verdict is the sentence that turns the kingdom. Knowing the world of Hebron keeps the reader from treating David's anointing as a formality: the covenant, the oil, and the word by Samuel are what make a shepherd into a king, and the muster that follows is the whole people saying so with their feet and their food. The chapter's last line is the measure of the turn: there was joy in Israel. The kingdom that began with a king Israel asked for, and lost on a ridge in the north, is given again to a king the tribes choose together — and the joy is the sign that the turn is complete.
