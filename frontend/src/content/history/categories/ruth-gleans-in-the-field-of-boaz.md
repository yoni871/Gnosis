## Overview

Naomi and Ruth reach Bethlehem as the barley harvest begins, two widows with nothing but each other. Ruth goes out to glean — to pick up the grain the reapers leave behind, the share the law reserves for the poor, the stranger and the widow.[^source-biblicaltraining-gleaning] She asks leave to work behind whoever will show her favour, and by what the narrator calls her hap she lights on a field belonging to Boaz, a kinsman of her dead husband Elimelech.[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text]

Boaz comes out from Bethlehem, greets his reapers, and notices the stranger in his field. He tells her to glean only there, to stay close by his young women, and to drink from the water jars his young men have drawn; he has charged the young men not to touch her.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] When she asks why a foreigner has found such favour, he answers that all she did for Naomi has been fully told to him — how she left father, mother and homeland for a people she did not know — and blesses her: may her reward be complete from the God of Israel, "under whose wings thou art come to take refuge."[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth][^source-bdb-lexicon]

At mealtime he calls her to eat beside the reapers, bread dipped in vinegar and parched corn, and she eats until she is satisfied, with some left over.[^source-jfb-commentary] Then he orders his men to let her glean even among the sheaves, and to pull stalks from the bundles for her on purpose. She works until evening and threshes out about an ephah of barley — a large yield for one day's gleaning.[^source-biblicaltraining-ephah-measure]

She carries it home, and Naomi sees at once that someone has taken notice of her. When Ruth names Boaz, Naomi blesses him: the LORD has not left off His kindness to the living and to the dead. "The man is nigh of kin unto us," she says — one of our redeemers.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] Ruth stays with Boaz's young women to glean until the end of barley harvest and of wheat harvest.[^source-biblicaltraining-harvest]

Three times in the chapter Ruth is called "the Moabitess." The law excludes the Moabite from the assembly, yet here a Moabite woman is welcomed into the field, protected, and blessed — a tension the story leaves standing, not one it pauses to resolve.[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text] The chapter is set in the days of the judges, but no date is given for it; what the chapter measures is a harvest season, from the first barley to the last wheat.

## Ancient World

### The law behind the gleaning

In ancient Israel the harvest was never gathered bare. The law told landowners what to leave: the corners of the field unreaped, the grain that fell during reaping ungathered, a forgotten sheaf unfetched, an olive tree not beaten twice, a vineyard not gone over a second time.[^source-biblicaltraining-gleaning] What remained belonged to the poor, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow — people without land of their own. The law gave reasons, too: the LORD would bless the work of the obedient farmer's hands, and Israel was to remember that it had once been slaves in Egypt.[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text]

But a right on paper was not a welcome in the field. Hard-hearted farmers, Keil and Delitzsch observe, threw obstacles in the way of the poor and even forbade their gleaning altogether — which is why Ruth goes looking for favour, not claiming a right.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown draw the line precisely: gleaning the edges and the fallen grain was a right conferred by law, but liberty to glean behind the reapers among the sheaves was a privilege, granted or refused according to the owner's goodwill.[^source-jfb-commentary] Everything Boaz does for Ruth sits on the privilege side of that line. He does not merely leave the corners; he opens his whole field to her, feeds her at his table, and orders extra grain put within her reach.

The four provisions were distinct, and the Hebrew marks the difference. Gathering fallen stalks is *laqat*, to pick up; going over vines and olives a second time is *'alal*, a different verb for a second pass.[^source-biblicaltraining-gleaning] A forgotten sheaf is a whole bound bundle overlooked in the field, not a few dropped ears. Olives were knocked from the branches with poles, and the few left on the highest boughs belonged to the gleaner. Ruth's work is the first kind: she follows the reapers and gathers what falls, and then — by Boaz's order — what is deliberately pulled from the bundles for her.

### A working harvest field

Ruth 2 gives one of the fullest pictures of a harvest field anywhere in the Bible. A servant is set over the reapers: an overseer who superintends the work, supplies provisions, and pays the labourers in the evening.[^source-jfb-commentary] The reapers cut the stalks with a sickle, or pull them up where the crop is thin, and bind them into sheaves to be carried to the threshing floor.[^source-biblicaltraining-reaping] Work begins early in the morning, before the heat, and runs until evening. Ruth works from morning with only a brief rest.

Whether the women cut and the men bound, or the women reaped and the men sorted the sheaves, the older commentators disagree — and the text does not settle it.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth][^source-jfb-commentary] What it does settle is Ruth's place in the workforce: she is told to keep close by the maidens, to drink from the vessels the young men have drawn, and to glean in Boaz's field alone. Naomi's advice at the chapter's end explains why: in another field, a foreign woman gleaning alone could expect rough treatment. Boaz's orders are protection as much as generosity — a fence of his authority set around a vulnerable worker. Fields in those days were unenclosed, open ground marked by landmarks rather than walls, so a gleaner moved from one man's ground to another's without a fence to show it.[^source-jfb-commentary]

At mealtime the workers eat together: bread dipped in vinegar and parched corn — new grain roasted on the spot and rubbed in the hands.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] Boaz serves Ruth himself, and gives her so much that she carries the leftover home to Naomi. The meal is the moment the chapter turns: Ruth stops being a stranger tolerated at the edge of the field and becomes a guest at the workers' table.

### An ephah of barley

When the day ends, Ruth beats out what she has gathered — with a small quantity, a stick would do — and it comes to about an ephah of barley.[^source-jfb-commentary] The ephah was the standard dry measure of ancient Israel: ten omers, a tenth of a homer, and equal in size to the bath, the liquid measure.[^source-bdb-lexicon] It was the measure of everyday life as well as of worship — the same quantity appears when Gideon makes bread for a visitor, when Hannah brings flour to Shiloh, and when Jesse sends David parched grain at the battlefront.[^source-bdb-lexicon]

How much was it? No complete ephah vessel has survived, so the figure has to be worked out from the ratios the Bible gives and from fragments of jars marked "bath" found at Lachish and Tell Beit Mirsim.[^source-biblicaltraining-ephah-measure] Most estimates today put it at roughly twenty litres, about half a bushel — somewhere between thirteen and twenty-three litres, or roughly twenty-five to thirty pounds of barley. Older books said about a bushel; that figure is now generally regarded as too large.[^source-biblicaltraining-ephah-measure] Whatever the exact figure, a full ephah from one day's gleaning was a large yield for one worker, and the text measures it precisely so the reader can see it: ordinary gleaning would not have filled a measure like that. The extra stalks Boaz ordered pulled from the bundles are the difference between a poor woman's handful and a measure she has to carry home.

### Barley first, then wheat

The chapter is built on the farming year. Barley ripened first, in about April, and wheat followed a few weeks later, reaped in about May and June; the grain harvest as a whole lasted about seven weeks, from Passover to Pentecost.[^source-biblicaltraining-harvest][^source-isbe-harvest] The timing shifted with place — the hot Jordan Valley ripened weeks before the hill country — but the order never changed: barley first, wheat after, then grapes, figs and olives through the summer and autumn.

An inscribed limestone tablet found at Gezer in 1908, the Gezer Calendar, lists the same order: a month of barley harvest followed by a month of general harvest, in a year of sowing, tending and gathering.[^source-dospel-gezer-calendar] It is usually dated to the tenth century BCE by its script, though it was found without a secure archaeological context, and scholars disagree over whether its language is Hebrew or Phoenician and whether it was a farmer's almanac or a school exercise. It does not prove anything about Ruth. What it does is show that the sequence Ruth 2 follows — barley, then wheat, then the season's end — was the working year of the land, written down by people who lived by it.

Ruth arrives at the beginning of barley harvest and gleans until the end of wheat harvest. Seven weeks of mornings in the field stand behind a single closing sentence, and the chapter's two time scales — one day told in detail, a whole season summed in a line — are the measure of a life being remade: not in a moment, but across a harvest.

### Wings of refuge

When Boaz blesses Ruth, he uses a word that carries two meanings at once. *Kanaph* is the wing of a bird, and it is also the edge or corner of a garment.[^source-bdb-lexicon] The same word describes the LORD spreading His wings over His people like an eagle over its young, and the psalms' refuge "under His wings."[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth][^source-bdb-lexicon] In chapter 2 the word is used of God: Ruth has come to take refuge under His wings. In chapter 3 Ruth will use the very same word to Boaz himself, asking him to spread his wing — his garment corner — over her. The blessing in the field is the image the threshing floor will answer.

### The kinsman named

Only at the end of the day does Naomi give Boaz his true title. "The man is nigh of kin unto us," she says — in Hebrew, one of our redeemers, a *go'el*.[^source-keil-delitzsch-ruth] A redeemer was the near kinsman on whom it fell to buy back family land sold in poverty, and to redeem a kinsman who had sold himself; the land itself belonged to the LORD, and the kinsman acted within that claim.[^source-mechon-mamre-ruth-masoretic-text] Boaz is not the nearest — one other kinsman has precedence — but he is near, and near enough that Naomi can already see the LORD's kindness in him, "to the living and to the dead." What he will do with that standing belongs to the next part of the story. Here, in the field, he is simply the man who let a foreign widow glean in safety, fed her at his table, and sent her home carrying a full measure.

## Why This Context Matters

Ruth 2 is a chapter about how provision actually worked. The law left food in the field, but food in the field only fed the poor when a landowner let them in — and Boaz let Ruth in further than the law required, protected her while she worked, and fed her himself. Knowing the gleaning laws, the size of an ephah, and the shape of the harvest season turns a quiet chapter into a story about generosity with a measure: a day's gleaning that fills a vessel, a season of mornings that ends with a household fed. It also sets up everything that follows. The blessing about refuge under God's wings is answered when Ruth asks Boaz to spread his own garment over her, and the kinsman named over the evening meal is the redeemer the rest of the book will test. The chapter's tension — a Moabite woman welcomed where the law excludes the Moabite — is left standing, and the story does not hurry past it.
