## Overview

Joshua 3–5 narrates Israel's passage over the Jordan and its first acts in the land. The priests carrying the ark enter the river, the flow is halted in the account, and the people cross on dry ground. Joshua instructs twelve men to take stones from the riverbed for a public memorial. The story also speaks of stones set up in the Jordan itself, an important detail that should not be flattened into one monument without examining the passage's wording. The memorial teaches future generations what the crossing means to Israel.

At Gilgal, east of Jericho in the narrative, the people make camp. Joshua 5 describes circumcision, Passover, and the cessation of manna after the people begin eating produce from the land. The sequence draws a line between wilderness provision and settled agricultural life. Joshua then encounters a figure designated commander of the Lord's army and is instructed to remove his sandals. These scenes prepare for the siege of Jericho without treating the crossing as a merely military maneuver. The narrative's focus remains on divine agency, remembrance, and the renewal of communal obligations. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Joshua 3–4 gives the crossing two different scales. The priests bear the ark into the riverbed while the people pass over, and representatives from each tribe take stones from the Jordan to establish a memorial. Chapter 5 then moves from crossing to belonging: circumcision, Passover, the cessation of manna, and the encounter near Jericho prepare the reader for the next narrative. The memorial’s significance is explained in terms of future questions from children (4:6–7, 21–24), not through an independently recovered stone installation. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Joshua 3–5 contains a sequence of public acts with different purposes: crossing, preserving memory, circumcision, Passover and the encounter near Jericho. Each happens after a preceding command or explanation. The crossing is narrated first from the priests' position in the river, then from the experience of the entire community; the stones turn that episode into something future generations will ask about (4:6–7).

## Ancient World

### The river, Gilgal, and the stones

Joshua 3 describes the Jordan as overflowing its banks during harvest (3:15), while the priests carry the ark and remain in the riverbed until the people pass. This observation is part of the story; it supplies neither a year nor a geological explanation. No modern proposal for the exact crossing site is securely established by this wording alone.

Gilgal functions as a place of encampment, ritual transition, and subsequent memory. Joshua 4:19 situates the camp east of Jericho, but identifying an excavated site as the actual camp is disputed. Archaeological proposals use distributions of ruins, topographic clues, and interpretations of the name. No proposal makes the place-name alone sufficient proof of a Joshua-period encampment. Place names can in principle be reused or refer to more than one location, and later traditions may attach narratives to prominent landscapes; but for Gilgal itself Na'aman argues the opposite — that every biblical reference to Gilgal denotes the one cult site east of Jericho, and that apparent references to other sites result from scribal error or misinterpretation. The uncertainty should remain visible on any future map, including the difference between a text-based approximate locality and a proven archaeological point. [^source-naaman-biblical-gilgal-tel-aviv-2024]

The stones are twice used as acts of remembrance in Joshua 4, with the account emphasizing that children may ask what they mean (4:6–7, 21–24). The narrative is thus also about transmitting a story through a physical reminder. It does not follow that a particular modern stone circle has been excavated and identified as Joshua's memorial.

### Rites, the commander, and the ceremony

Joshua 5:2–12 connects circumcision and Passover to earlier covenant legislation (Genesis 17; Exodus 12) and marks the end of the manna (Joshua 5:12). These are the literary sequence and ritual meanings supplied by Scripture. The chapter does not provide archaeological evidence identifying the location of the rite or the physical form of its celebration.

The figure with a drawn sword in Joshua 5:13–15 does not simply endorse Joshua's side; the dialogue redirects attention to the 'commander of the LORD's army' and commands removal of sandals. Interpretations of the figure differ. Its identity should not be presented as independently determined from the text, and later Christological readings must be labeled as theological reception.

The placement of a covenant ceremony is also a live textual issue. Deuteronomy 27 commands rites associated with the crossing and mountains, while the Masoretic form of Joshua places the Ebal episode after Ai. Evidence from 4Q47 and Greek Joshua has generated scholarship about differences of literary arrangement. It would be misleading to suggest that every witness has precisely the same sequence. Because the manuscripts are fragmentary and analyses differ, an article should identify the specific witness and the reconstruction under discussion instead of making a sweeping claim about a single original order. [^source-makipelto-tekniemi-tucker-large-scale-transposition-2017]

### The Jordan and its three settings

The Jordan River changes with season, flow and channel conditions, making it difficult to turn the literary account into a fixed hydrographic map. Joshua 3:15 specifies harvest-time flooding and Joshua 3:16 names Adam as a location upstream; the narrative’s own geography is more precise than a generic reference to a river crossing. Identifying the precise crossing point or the Gilgal encampment requires more evidence than the survival of later place names. Gilgal has been interpreted as a locality, a descriptive term and a cultic place-name in different biblical contexts; one proposed site should not be imposed on every occurrence. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The text itself gives three different settings to keep distinct: the Jordan riverbed, the west-bank camp at Gilgal, and Joshua's encounter near Jericho. Archaeologists' proposed sites for Gilgal are not interchangeable with locations on a modern map simply because they lie in the valley. The narrative uses Gilgal as a camp associated with circumcision and Passover, but it does not supply coordinates or a continuously occupied archaeological layer proving every ceremony (Joshua 4:19–5:12).

### The crossing remembered

The priests' participation is narrated with a deliberately extended sequence of commands and fulfillment. Joshua 3:6–8 instructs the carriers of the ark to enter the river; verses 14–17 then report their feet reaching the water and their standing while the people cross. Chapter 4 returns repeatedly to the same moment from the perspectives of the tribes, the memorial stones, and the priests' eventual emergence. The repetition is part of the compositional texture: it makes the crossing an event remembered publicly rather than a feat reported in one sentence. The writer's detail does not amount to a surveyed channel width, a known river discharge figure, or a fixed calendar year.

The text names a stopping of water at a place called Adam, by Zarethan (3:16). It is tempting to correlate that language with known cases in which the Jordan's banks have slumped and temporarily obstructed water. Such comparisons can be described as proposed natural analogies, but none identifies Joshua's event or dates it independently. The passage gives no record of seismic shaking, precise duration of blockage, or measurable flood height. Equally, the descriptive note that the Jordan overflows in harvest time is not evidence that the scene took place during the particular flood season of a modern year. The best historical treatment preserves the distinction between the river's broadly known landscape and the exceptional occurrence as narrated in Joshua 3. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The new camp at Gilgal also functions as a place where the transition to the land is ritually expressed. Joshua 5:2–9 describes circumcision and the explanation of the name Gilgal; 5:10–12 places Passover at the camp and marks the cessation of manna. The wordplay around 'rolling away' in 5:9 has a narrative purpose even if a proposed archaeological Gilgal identification remains contested. The meal and the end of manna are joined directly to eating the land's produce. These details are literary and theological evidence for how the transition is represented; they do not require inventing the architecture of a sanctuary at the camp.

## Why This Context Matters

The Jordan crossing is presented as a beginning that deliberately recalls an earlier deliverance, not as a simple change of location. A reader who notices the river geography and the memorial-stone instructions can see why the passage combines movement, teaching, and covenant language. The practices at Gilgal make sense within a narrative of people emerging from the wilderness and preparing to inhabit farmland.

At the same time, physical setting and ritual analogy should not be made to carry more historical weight than they can bear. A natural river blockage elsewhere cannot verify this crossing, and a proposed Gilgal site is not the camp identified by excavation. Keeping those limits explicit preserves space for the text's own theological claims while giving readers a disciplined understanding of what the material evidence does, and does not, tell us.

The manna ceases when the people eat produce from the land (5:11–12). That transition links the wilderness provision of Exodus and Numbers to a new life oriented around land, agriculture and worship. It also provides a more textually grounded way to explain the significance of the crossing than an attempt to assign an unverified flood, landslide or seismic date.
