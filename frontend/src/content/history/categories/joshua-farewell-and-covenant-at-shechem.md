## Overview

Joshua 23–24 closes the book with two farewell assemblies and covenant reflection. In chapter 23 Joshua reminds Israel that the Lord has fought for them and warns that continuing relationships with the remaining nations could draw them into other worship. The language balances fulfilled promises with the seriousness of future unfaithfulness. It is not simply a victory celebration: it asks whether a community that has received an inheritance will remain loyal after the leader's death.

Chapter 24 gathers the people at Shechem and retells Israel's story from the ancestors through liberation, wilderness, and settlement. Joshua challenges the assembly to choose whom it will serve, the people answer with a commitment, and the covenant is recorded with a stone set up as witness. The chapter ends with death and burial notices, including those of Joshua and Eleazar and the burial of Joseph's bones. The witness stone's narrative function is clear, but no archaeological monument has been securely identified as that very object. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Joshua 23 is a farewell warning framed by the Lord’s past help and the danger of serving other gods. Joshua 24 moves to an assembly at Shechem and rehearses a narrative from Abraham’s ancestors through the exodus and entrance into the land. The people choose to serve the Lord, and Joshua sets up a stone witness. The book closes with the deaths of Joshua and Eleazar and Joseph’s burial (24:29–33). These closing notices matter because covenant commitment is followed by generational transition rather than another conquest campaign. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Joshua 23 consists of instructions framed by the passage of time and Joshua's approaching death. The speech moves from remembrance of the LORD's past help to urgent warnings about foreign worship and covenants. Chapter 24 then brings the people to Shechem for a public decision and a witness, shifting from private leadership advice to collective covenant language.

## Ancient World

Joshua 24 places the assembly at Shechem, a central-highland setting between Mount Ebal (north) and Mount Gerizim (south). This geographical relationship is secure. Identification of particular architectural remains with Joshua's covenant ceremony is not established by the text or archaeology.

Joshua 24 combines historical rehearsal, collective decision, obligation, and warning. Ancient Near Eastern treaties and covenant texts may provide formal comparisons, including introductory historical material and stipulations. Yet similarities of form are not a decisive proof of direct borrowing from one particular Hittite treaty or a secure date for the composition of Joshua. Joshua 24:2–13 begins with a retrospect from the ancestors beyond the River, through Abraham, the exodus and land inheritance. This is the narrative's own condensed history. Its sequence should be described as the covenant speech rather than treated as an independently verified historical record for each episode.

Joshua 24:14–24 makes rejection of other gods a condition of allegiance to the LORD and records a public response. The ethical and theological stakes are clear from the dialogue. It would be misleading to infer from this speech that archaeologists can establish the precise practices of every household present.

Joshua 24:25–27 records Joshua writing covenant words and setting a stone beneath an oak near the sanctuary. He personifies the stone as a witness. The same device is part of the narrative's rhetoric; the passage does not identify a specific excavated standing stone as that witness.

Joshua 23 addresses fidelity after Joshua's retirement from leadership, while chapter 24 stages a covenant commitment at Shechem. They belong together yet have different speech settings and emphases. Treaty comparisons are useful as comparisons of literary features, not proof of direct borrowing from a named ancient diplomatic tablet.

The death and burial notices, including Joseph's bones and Eleazar's burial, link the book's conclusion to earlier ancestral and exodus traditions. Exodus 13:19 supplies a text that recounts carrying Joseph's bones from Egypt; Hebrews 11:22 later interprets Joseph's instruction as an expression of faith. These are linked biblical traditions and later reception, not archaeological proof of the precise burial site. The ending deliberately looks backward to promise and forward to life after Joshua, making its grave notices significant beyond their geographical details. [^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Shechem sits in the central hill country between Mount Gerizim to the south and Mount Ebal to the north. Its later religious and political importance makes it a meaningful setting in biblical tradition, but no archaeological object independently records the complete conversation of Joshua 24. Shechem's setting between Ebal and Gerizim makes it a meaningful geographical anchor within the narrative. The association with a covenant stone under an oak (24:26–27) remains a textual claim; no particular standing stone may be assigned to Joshua without independent evidence. Joshua 23 opens with the people experiencing a period of rest and Joshua nearing the end of his life. The speech presents divine assistance retrospectively and warns that continued possession must not become an excuse for religious accommodation. The vocabulary of loyalty, remaining nations and covenant obligations belongs to its theological argument. Editorial writing should attend to its conditional rhetoric rather than extract one triumphal sentence and treat it as an archaeological certificate of total occupation.

Shechem's geography anchors Joshua 24 in the central highlands. The two mountains associated with covenant memory in Joshua 8, Ebal and Gerizim, stand on opposite sides of the valley. However, the place-name relationship does not establish that any particular standing stone discovered near ancient Shechem is Joshua's witness stone. The speech's witnesses are literary and ritual devices: Joshua writes covenant words and places a large stone near the sanctuary (24:25–27). Treaty comparisons can illuminate oath-taking, collective assent and witness imagery, yet similarity in form cannot by itself identify a single foreign treaty that the biblical author copied.

The covenant speech stretches from the ancestors beyond the River to the exodus and the gift of land, then turns toward a present decision about whom Israel will serve (24:2–15). Joshua's own family declaration is followed by dialogue: the people respond, Joshua challenges the adequacy of their promise, and they reiterate their commitment (24:16–24). After the writing and the stone, the book closes with the burial of Joshua, Joseph's bones and Eleazar. These three notices draw together leadership, remembered ancestral promise and priestly succession. The end is thus both a covenant conclusion and a narrative ending for several figures, rather than an isolated treaty ceremony.

## Why This Context Matters

Shechem's location between Ebal and Gerizim and its long settlement history help readers picture why a covenant gathering could be imagined there. The comparison with ancient treaties also clarifies that public commitments can be framed through shared memory, commands, witnesses, and consequences. Such similarities illuminate literary form, but they cannot prove dependence on a specific treaty or provide an exact composition date.

The final speeches make loyalty a future responsibility rather than an automatic result of past victory. The witness stone and burial notices give the covenant a material and generational dimension inside the narrative. A disciplined historical article can preserve these meanings while saying plainly that the particular witness stone has not been found and the dialogue is not independently attested as a meeting transcript. That honesty serves readers better than either exaggerated certainty or unnecessary dismissal.

The burial notices at the book's end (24:29–33) provide a separate closing movement for Joshua, Joseph's bones and Eleazar. They tie the covenant assembly to mortality, inheritance and memory. Omitting these verses would turn the farewell into an abstract sermon; including them shows how the narrative closes the lives of major figures and the journey of Joseph's remains.
