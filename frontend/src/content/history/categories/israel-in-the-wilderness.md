## Overview

After the song at the sea, Moses leads Israel from the Red Sea into the wilderness of Shur. For three days they find no water, and when they reach Marah the water is bitter and undrinkable. The people murmur against Moses, and Moses cries out to the LORD. The LORD shows him a tree, which he casts into the water, and the water turns sweet. There the LORD gives them a statute and an ordinance and tests them, promising that if they listen to his voice, none of Egypt's diseases will come on them. From Marah they travel to Elim, with its twelve springs and seventy palm trees, and camp by the water.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

From Elim the congregation journeys to the wilderness of Sin, arriving on the fifteenth day of the second month after leaving Egypt. There the whole congregation murmurs against Moses and Aaron, longing for Egypt's fleshpots and saying they would rather have died there than be brought out to die of hunger. The LORD answers that he will rain bread from heaven: each day the people will gather a day's portion, so he may test whether they will walk in his law. In the evening quail covers the camp; in the morning a fine flaky substance lies on the ground. The people call it manna. Each gathers an omer; on the sixth day they gather double, because the seventh day is a Sabbath and none is found. An omer is kept before the Testimony for their generations, and Israel eats manna forty years, until they reach Canaan's border.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon]

At Rephidim there is again no water, and the people quarrel with Moses, asking why he brought them out of Egypt to die of thirst with their children and cattle. Moses cries to the LORD, who tells him to take the elders and his rod and strike the rock at Horeb. Water comes out of the rock and the people drink. Moses names the place Massah and Meribah, for the striving of Israel and their testing of the LORD.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Then Amalek comes and fights with Israel at Rephidim. Moses sends Joshua out to fight while he stands on the hilltop with the rod of God in his hand. While Moses holds his hand up, Israel prevails; when he lets it down, Amalek prevails. When his hands grow heavy, Aaron and Hur seat him on a stone and hold his hands up until sunset, and Joshua defeats Amalek. Moses builds an altar called Jehovah-nissi and records that the LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Jethro, priest of Midian and Moses' father-in-law, brings Zipporah and her two sons, Gershom and Eliezer, to Moses in the wilderness at the mount of God. Moses tells him all the LORD has done to Pharaoh and Egypt for Israel's sake; Jethro rejoices, blesses the LORD, and offers sacrifices, sharing a meal with Aaron and the elders before God. The next day Moses sits judging the people from morning to evening. Jethro warns the burden is too heavy for one man and advises him to teach the people the statutes and laws and appoint able, God-fearing, truthful men who hate covetousness as rulers of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, to judge small matters and bring only hard cases to Moses. Moses does as Jethro says, and Jethro returns to his own land.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Two questions stay open: when the Exodus happened, and where exactly the march went — the text gives an internal date and a station order, but no date fixed against outside history and no station fixed on a map.

## Ancient World

### The road through the desert

The march has a firm order and an uncertain map: from the Sea through Shur to Marah, Elim, the wilderness of Sin, and Rephidim, and on to the mount of God. Numbers 33:8–14 gives the same stations in the same order, with three more that Exodus omits: an encampment by the Red Sea, Dophkah, and Alush. Numbers also calls the first wilderness Etham where Exodus says Shur — two names for one stretch.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Every proposed identification is conjectural; none is established. Marah is most often identified with Ain Hawarah, a bitter spring said to lie about fifty miles south of Suez, with Ayun Musa, the Springs of Moses, as the alternative; Elim is commonly identified with Wadi Gharandel, a well-watered wadi with palms, with Wadi Taiyibeh as the alternative. Both pairings rest on secondary summaries, and both are assessed as debated. A standing objection notes the two are only about two hours' walk apart, too short for a day's march — a caution, not a refutation, since the text gives no distances. The wilderness of Sin is traditionally placed on the el-Markha coastal plain. Rephidim is believed on good grounds to be Wadi Feiran, at the entrance of the Horeb district, and the text itself puts the rock "in Horeb" — the two readings are compatible. Dophkah, Alush, and the Red Sea encampment stay unlocated; no station has any archaeological trace, and no station name is attested outside the Bible.[^source-wikipedia-stations-of-the-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

### Quail, manna, and the desert's own food

Two real desert phenomena stand behind the food stories, and neither explains the biblical account. The common quail migrates in great flocks across the Mediterranean in spring and autumn; exhausted birds fly low and are easily caught. The Exodus 16 episode falls in spring, on the fifteenth day of the second month, which matches the spring migration. The manna's parallel is the honeydew of the tamarisk: scale insects suck the sap and excrete a sweet, sticky, honey-like substance that crystallises on the twigs, which Bedouin collect in June and July and still call man. It appears only in that season, only a few hundred kilograms in a year, melts in the heat, and does not breed worms overnight.[^source-bodenheimer-manna-of-sinai]

The biblical manna is daily, forty years, equal to an omer however much is gathered, doubled on the sixth day, absent on the Sabbath, and wholesome only in its Sabbath portion. The classic commentators agree the tamarisk gum fails the profile on every distinctive point: small quantities, not every year, unable to be baked or boiled, a medicine rather than food, well known to the natives while Israel were strangers to it. Sarna draws the modern line: the quail was wholly natural except for its timing, while the manna possesses a numinous quality, and no naturalistic explanation does justice to the manna tradition as the Bible presents it. The wilderness of Sin is not the wilderness of Zin: different places, different spelling, a standing confusion to guard against.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-sarna-exodus]

### When did this happen?

The passage dates itself internally and leaves its absolute date open. The wilderness of Sin is reached exactly one month after the departure from Egypt, on the fifteenth day of the second month, and the manna lasts forty years. Beyond that, two positions are both live and neither is established. The early date, about 1446 BCE, counts the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1 back from Solomon's fourth year, placing the Exodus in the mid-fifteenth century — a textual inference with no external corroboration. The late date, in the thirteenth century, argues from the store-city names and the Ramesside building horizon, roughly the reign of Ramesses II — a convergence argument, not a proof.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-kitchen-reliability-ot]

The one fixed external point is the Merneptah Stele, from year five of Merneptah, about 1208 BCE: a black granite monument found by Flinders Petrie in 1896 at Thebes, whose Canaan lines name Israel — "Israel is laid waste, his seed is not" — with the determinative for a people, not a land. It is the earliest extrabiblical mention of Israel, and it fixes a terminus ante quem: Israel was a recognised people in Canaan by about 1208 BCE. It says nothing about the Exodus itself, and it bounds the debate without settling it. No consensus date can be assigned to this category.[^source-merneptah-stele]

### The Sabbath before Sinai

The Sabbath arrives in the manna story before the Decalogue, and the classic commentators read Exodus 16 as evidence it was known before Sinai. Calvin writes that the seventh day was really hallowed before the promulgation of the Law, "although it is questionable whether it had already been observed by the patriarchs. It appears probable that it was; but I am unwilling to make it a matter of contention." Clarke holds that the Sabbath is spoken of as perfectly well known, and was considered a divine institution before the giving of the Mosaic law. Keil and Delitzsch read the manna rules as the opening of the Sabbath's sanctification: the gift was bestowed so that the Sabbath was sanctified by it, opening the way for its sanctification by the law. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown take the opposite tack on the institution question, treating the Sabbath as a human institution already in force, not newly created here. The four agree the Sabbath predates Sinai; they differ on whether it predates the Exodus. That is their reading, not an established fact about patriarchal observance, and this category does not decide between them.[^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-jfb-commentary]

### Jethro and the judges

Jethro's visit carries the chapter's chronological puzzle. He meets Moses "at the mount of God" (18:5), yet Israel does not reach Sinai until the third month (19:1–2). Most commentators, following Jamieson, Fausset and Brown, hold the episode is inserted out of its chronological order — placed here topically, as a bridge between deliverance and covenant order, while the event itself belongs at or near Horeb. Deuteronomy 1:6–17, retelling the appointment of leaders as spoken at Horeb, supports the topical reading. The counsel has two parts: Moses remains the people's representative before God, teaching them ordinances and laws, and he appoints able men who fear God, men of truth who hate covetousness, as rulers of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, to judge at all seasons while every great matter comes to Moses. The condition is explicit — "If thou shalt do this thing, and God command thee so" — a suggestion, not to be adopted without God's own sanction. Moses hearkens and does all that Jethro says, and Jethro returns to his own land.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-josephus-antiquities]

### Amalek: the first battle

Exodus gives no motive for the attack; three readings are on record, none established. Josephus says the Amalekites, fearing Israel's spreading renown, stirred the neighbouring nations by embassy against the Hebrews. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown read it as an old grudge: Amalek descends from Esau's grandson (Gen 36:12), and the attack is the desert raider falling on travellers. Keil and Delitzsch, with Deuteronomy 25:18, hold that Amalek fell on the rear, attacking the stragglers. The battle itself is told without explanation: when Moses holds up his hand, Israel prevails, and when he lets it down, Amalek prevails. Philo allegorises the hands as a balance, and an older note reads them as the earliest picture of the posture of solemn prayer. The text reports the correlation, not a theory of it. Two institutions follow: the first command to write in the Pentateuch — "Write this for a memorial in a book, and rehearse it in the ears of Joshua" — and the altar Jehovah-nissi, "The LORD my banner", with the oath of war against Amalek from generation to generation. The Hebrew of 17:16 is ambiguous between "hand on the throne of Jah" and "hand on the banner of Jah", and the ambiguity is recorded, not resolved.[^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-philo-life-of-moses]

### How later readers heard it

The ancient retellings add colour the text does not contain; they are reception, not a second source for the events. Josephus retells the whole march in Antiquities 3.1–4: the stick split lengthways, Elim's palms ill-grown, manna still raining in his own day, and Hur named as Miriam's husband — none of it in Exodus, all of it recorded as tradition. Philo treats the manna, the rock, and Amalek in Life of Moses I: the double portion proves the seventh day is the day creation was finished; the rock's mechanism he leaves open; the enemy he calls "the Phoenicians", his own label, not the text's. Jubilees 50 reads the wilderness of Sin as "the desert of Sinai, which is between Elim and Sinai"; Augustine surveys the whole Exodus in a single chapter of the City of God — forty years in the desert, the law given on the fiftieth day after the Passover. The Septuagint transliterates the manna as "man" rather than translating "What is this?", renders the flakes "fine like coriander, white like frost on the ground", and at 16:29 reads "this day, the sabbaths" for the Hebrew "the sabbath".[^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-philo-life-of-moses][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus]

## Why This Context Matters

These chapters are the first stretch of the journey after the sea, and they set the shape of everything that follows. The people move from deliverance into need: water at Marah, food in the wilderness of Sin, water again at Rephidim. At each stop the pattern is the same — the people murmur, Moses cries out, and provision comes — and each stop adds something: a statute at Marah, a daily rule of gathering and a Sabbath at the manna, a named place of testing at the rock.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The fighting with Amalek is the first battle of the journey, and it is told as a battle won while Moses' hands are held up on the hill and Joshua fights below. The altar that follows, and the word about war with Amalek from generation to generation, close the episode with a memorial written down and repeated to Joshua.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Jethro's visit closes the passage by changing how the people are governed. Moses has been judging the whole people alone from morning to evening; Jethro's counsel spreads the judging across able men in ranks of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, while Moses keeps the hard causes and the teaching of the statutes. The passage ends with Jethro returning to his own land, and with the people organised under judges, camped at the mount of God, on the edge of the covenant that comes next.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]
