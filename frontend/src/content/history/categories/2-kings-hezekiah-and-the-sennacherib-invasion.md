## Overview



Hezekiah son of Ahaz comes to the throne of Judah in the third year of Hoshea of Israel, twenty-five years old, and reigns twenty-nine years in Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He does right "according to all that David his father did," and his reform goes further than any king's before him: he removes the high places, breaks the images, cuts down the groves, and breaks in pieces the brasen serpent Moses made, which the people had been burning incense to and calling Nehushtan [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. No king before him had removed the high places, and the book will remember him for it: "so that after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah, nor any that were before him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In his years Samaria falls. The northern kingdom is besieged three years and taken, and its people are carried away to Assyria — the fall told again, inside Hezekiah's reign, with its own one-verse explanation [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then Assyria comes for Judah. In Hezekiah's fourteenth year Sennacherib takes all the fenced cities, and Hezekiah sends to Lachish: "I have offended; return from me: that which thou puttest on me will I bear" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He pays three hundred talents of silver and thirty of gold, cutting the gold from the doors of the temple and from the pillars he had overlaid [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. The payment does not end the threat. Three Assyrian officers come to Jerusalem with a great host and stand by the conduit of the upper pool, and Rabshakeh speaks — first to Hezekiah's officers, then in a loud voice in the Jews' language to the men on the wall — mocking their trust in Egypt, in the reform, and in the LORD himself [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The people answer nothing, as the king commanded. Hezekiah rends his clothes, covers himself with sackcloth, and sends to Isaiah the prophet [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The letter comes, and Hezekiah spreads it before the LORD and prays that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that the LORD alone is God [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Isaiah answers with an oracle: the king of Assyria shall not come into the city or shoot an arrow there, for the LORD will defend it "for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. That night the angel of the LORD smites a hundred fourscore and five thousand in the Assyrian camp, and Sennacherib goes home to Nineveh, where his own sons slay him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. The reign closes quietly and strangely. Hezekiah falls sick unto death, prays, and is given fifteen years more, with the shadow turned backward ten degrees on the dial of Ahaz as the sign [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Envoys come from Babylon, and Hezekiah shows them everything in his house — and hears that all of it shall one day be carried into Babylon, and nothing shall be left [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He is remembered for the pool and the conduit by which he brought water into the city, and then he sleeps with his fathers, and Manasseh his son reigns in his stead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World



### The reform that reached the relic

Hezekiah's reform is the most thorough the book of Kings records before Josiah's, and its most startling act is the breaking of an object Moses himself had made. When the people were bitten by fiery serpents in the wilderness, Moses made a serpent of brass and set it on a pole, and everyone bitten who looked on it lived [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Centuries later the Israelites were burning incense to it, and it was called Nehushtan — a name that plays on the Hebrew words for "bronze" and "snake" [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. A relic linked to Moses had become an object of worship, and Hezekiah destroyed it. The reform reached the relic itself: nothing was too old, too holy, or too closely tied to Moses to be spared once it had become an idol. The same reform removed the high places — the open-air shrines scattered through the towns and hills where the people sacrificed and burned incense [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Every other reforming king had left them standing: Joash, Amaziah, Azariah, and Jotham are all praised and all leave the high places in place [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Hezekiah alone removes them, and his enemy will use the removal against him, arguing that he has offended the very God he claims to trust [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The reform also had a practical side. Chronicles, which tells the reform at greater length, describes the temple cleansed and reopened, the Passover kept with couriers sent through Judah and into the north, and the people going out to smash the sacred stones and destroy the high places and altars [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. Kings tells it in a single verse, and the difference is worth noting: Kings gives the reform as a verdict on the king, Chronicles as a programme of worship. Either way, the reform made Jerusalem the one place of sacrifice — and made the king who ordered it the target of the empire's argument.

### Assyria and the campaign

Hezekiah came to power when the Assyrian Empire dominated the Near East [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. Tiglath-Pileser III had pushed Assyrian control to the borders of Egypt, and Samaria had fallen in 722 BC, its people deported [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. Sennacherib, king of Assyria from 705 to 681 BC, inherited an empire in revolt: when his father Sargon II died in battle, subject peoples from Babylon to the Mediterranean coast tested the new king [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. The western revolt was led by the kings of Sidon and Ashkelon, the nobles of Ekron, and Hezekiah of Judah, who looked to Egypt — then ruled by the Kushite 25th Dynasty — for help [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. The people of Ekron deposed their pro-Assyrian king, Padi, and handed him over in chains to Hezekiah, who held him prisoner in Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. In 701 BC Sennacherib marched west, defeated an Egyptian and Cushite army at Eltekeh, captured Ekron, and turned on Judah [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. His annals say he besieged and captured forty-six of Hezekiah's walled cities and claimed 200,150 captives [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. The numbers are the king's own boast, recorded on clay prisms that still survive — the Taylor Prism in the British Museum among them — and the Bible tells the same campaign from the other side [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. The two accounts agree on the shape of the war: the revolt, the Egyptian army, the fallen towns, the base at Lachish, Hezekiah shut up in Jerusalem, and a heavy tribute [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. They differ on the silver — 300 talents in Kings, 800 in the annals — and on the ending: the annals say nothing of a disaster and do not claim that Jerusalem fell [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib]. Assyrian kings did not record defeats. Hezekiah was the only major rebel of 701 BC who kept both his throne and his capital [^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib].

### Lachish: the city that fell

Lachish was the strongest fortress of Judah after Jerusalem, standing in the Shephelah, the foothills between the highlands and the coastal plain, about 25 miles southwest of Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish]. Any army moving from the coast toward Hebron or Jerusalem had to reckon with it, and Sennacherib made it his headquarters: Hezekiah sent word to him there, confessing his wrongdoing and agreeing to pay whatever was demanded, and from Lachish the Assyrians sent their field commander to demand Jerusalem's surrender [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish]. The Bible does not expressly say that Lachish fell, but the rest of the evidence leaves no doubt. Sennacherib had its capture carved in stone reliefs lining a room of his palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum: the walled city on its hill, archers and slingers, battering rams pushed up siege ramps, defenders hurling torches, captives executed, and families of deportees leaving with their belongings [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish]. Excavation has confirmed the picture in striking detail. At Tel Lachish, Ussishkin's team exposed the Assyrian siege ramp at the southwest corner of the mound — the only Assyrian siege ramp so far excavated anywhere — and the counter-ramp the defenders raised inside the wall, with hundreds of arrowheads, sling stones, and pieces of scale armour nearby [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish]. The city, Level III, ended in a fierce fire, dated to 701 BC largely through the royal storage jars found in the destruction layer, stamped *lmlk*, "belonging to the king" [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish]. Lachish is the one place in this story where the biblical account, the Assyrian record, the carved reliefs, and the excavated remains all describe the same episode [^source-biblicaltraining-lachish].

### Jerusalem: water, walls, and the siege

Jerusalem's strength was also its weakness: the city had no river, and its one true spring, the Gihon, rose outside the walls on the eastern slope [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Water set the limits of settlement, and a siege could turn the spring into a trap [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Hezekiah prepared for that. He blocked the springs outside the city so the Assyrians would not find water, and channelled the Gihon down to the west side of the City of David — a tunnel cut through solid rock, about 1,750 feet long, from the spring to the Pool of Siloam, which still carries water [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem][^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. An inscription found in the tunnel in 1880 describes the last moments of the work, when two teams cutting toward each other heard each other's picks and broke through; it gives the length as 1,200 cubits and names no king, and most scholars attribute the work to Hezekiah on the basis of its script, the biblical references, and radiometric dating [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. Hezekiah also repaired the broken sections of the wall, built towers, added a second wall outside, and made weapons and shields in abundance [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. After Samaria fell, refugees streamed south, and the city spread across the western hill and grew several times larger, enclosed by a wall about 23 feet thick, a stretch of which was found in the Jewish Quarter in 1970 [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. It was at the conduit of the upper pool — the watercourse that fed the city — that the Assyrian officers stood to deliver their demand [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The city held. Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage" and received heavy tribute, but they do not claim that he took the city [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem][^source-biblicaltraining-sennacherib].

### The temple's gold and the price of peace

Solomon's temple had stood for nearly four centuries when Hezekiah stripped it, and its gold was the kingdom's reserve [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. The doors of the main hall were made of juniper, carved and covered with gold; the doors of the Most Holy Place were of olive wood, carved with cherubim, palms, and flowers and covered in gold; the two bronze pillars Jachin and Boaz stood eighteen cubits high before the porch [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. It is this gold that Hezekiah cuts away: all the silver found in the house of the LORD and in the treasures of the king's house, and the gold from the doors and from the pillars he himself had overlaid [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. The payment was not the first of its kind. Joash had sent the temple's gold to Hazael of Syria, and Ahaz had sent its treasures to Tiglath-Pileser; the gold would leave the house a fifth time as spoil to Babylon [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The temple was the place where God had chosen to put his Name, and it was also the treasury the kings reached for when an empire came calling [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple][^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. The tribute bought time and nothing more: Sennacherib took the payment and still sent his officers to demand the city's surrender [^source-biblicaltraining-hezekiah]. The gold that left the temple in 701 BC would be carried out again, a century later, by Nebuchadnezzar's men — this time not as tribute but as spoil, "as the LORD had said" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The house that Hezekiah stripped to pay Assyria is the house he shows to Babylon's envoys, and the house Babylon will empty.

## Why This Context Matters



These three chapters are about a king who does everything right and is invaded anyway — and about what saves a city when its walls, its water, and its gold have all been spent. Knowing the world of the invasion keeps the reader from treating the deliverance as a simple reward for the reform. The reform itself becomes the enemy's argument, the tribute buys nothing, and the city is saved by a prayer and an oracle, not by the preparations that fill the chapter's margins. The Assyrian annals, the Lachish reliefs, and the tunnel under Jerusalem show the same campaign from the outside: an empire that took forty-six cities and left one standing, a king shut up in his capital who kept his throne. And the reign's close turns the story forward: the king who paid Assyria with the temple's gold shows his treasures to Babylon and hears that Babylon will carry them away. The deliverance of Jerusalem is real, and so is the word that follows it.
