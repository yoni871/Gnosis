## Overview

Judges 2 begins with the messenger of the LORD confronting Israel at Bochim. The speech recalls divine deliverance and the covenant while announcing that the nations left in the land will become a source of conflict and religious temptation. The people weep and offer sacrifices, but the ensuing narration traces the persistence of disobedience. Judges 2:6–10 then returns to Joshua's dismissal of the people and his death, creating a retrospective bridge from the preceding biblical book. A later generation is described as not knowing the LORD or his works for Israel.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

The messenger's charge is specific: Israel was told to make no covenant with the inhabitants of the land and to break down their altars, and the failure to do so is named directly (2:1–3). The nations left in place will become "thorns in your sides" and their gods "a snare to you." The people's weeping at Bochim — the place takes its name from that weeping — is followed not by reform but by the generational turn of 2:10, where a new generation arises that does not know the LORD. The bridge passage therefore does two things at once: it closes the Joshua era and it diagnoses the condition that will govern the book.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

Judges 2:11–23 presents the book's organizing pattern: Israel worships other gods, comes under the power of enemies, receives judges who bring deliverance, and relapses after a judge dies. The narrator insists both on God's anger at infidelity and his compassion toward those who groan under oppression. Yet this summary is not a rigid checklist reproduced in every episode; Shamgar and several brief judges receive no full version of it. In Judges 3:1–6 the remaining nations are described in terms of Israel's testing, experience of warfare, and temptation to intermarriage and foreign worship.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

The pattern as stated is the book's own idealized summary, not a uniform rule that every figure called a judge follows. The text itself shows the variation: some deliverers receive long narratives and some a single verse, some are raised up in response to a cry and some appear without one, and the relapses differ in form and severity. The summary in 2:11–23 explains the shape of the book's theology — disobedience, distress, deliverance, relapse — while the episodes that follow test how far that shape actually holds.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

## Ancient World

### What a "judge" is: deliverer and ruler

A 'judge' in these chapters is not simply an official sitting in a courthouse. The Hebrew terminology can refer to agents of deliverance and leadership, while the narrative introduces figures with very different roles, scales, and moral qualities. The literary overview sets a framework that later stories will test and complicate: victory may be temporary, some deliverers are deeply flawed, and the book grows increasingly dark.[^source-moore-judges-icc-introduction-section-1]

The Hebrew supports that broader sense. G. F. Moore, in the Introduction to his *Critical and Exegetical Commentary on Judges*, observes that the verb *shåphaţ* is "not only *judicare*, but *vindicare*, both in the sense of 'defend, deliver,' and in that of 'avenge, punish,'" and that the participle *shōpheţ* is "not only *judex*, but *vindex*, and is not infrequently synonymous with 'deliverer.'" He adds that the noun is "sometimes equivalent to 'ruler,' and the verb signifies, 'rule, govern.'" The *Encyclopaedia Britannica* puts the same point in plain terms: the Hebrew term *shofet*, translated into English as "judge," is closer in meaning to "ruler," a kind of military leader or deliverer. A judge in Judges is therefore best understood as one who vindicates and delivers — and sometimes rules — rather than as a magistrate deciding cases in a court.[^source-moore-judges-icc-introduction-section-1]

That breadth matters because the figures themselves differ so sharply. Othniel is raised up and empowered by the Spirit; Ehud is a lone assassin who seizes a strategic crossing; Shamgar appears for a single verse with an oxgoad; Gideon refuses kingship and then makes an ephod that becomes a snare; Abimelech seizes power by murder and is called king at Shechem. No single office, procedure, or moral profile covers them all. The overview in Judges 2 names the pattern they will be measured against; the narratives show how unevenly any one figure fits it.

### Joshua's death, told twice

The report of Joshua's death after an earlier account of his death is a literary feature of the transition between Joshua and Judges. Its purpose can be read as orienting the audience to the change between generations and to the problem of covenant memory. Questions about the composition and shaping of the books belong to scholarly literary inquiry; a sequence in the final text alone cannot establish precisely when each passage was written.[^source-moore-judges-icc-introduction-section-3]

Moore reads Judges 2:6–3:6 as the author's own introduction to the book. In the Introduction, he writes, the author "gives a comprehensive survey of the history of the entire period," describing how "after the death of Joshua and his contemporaries, Israel fell away from Yahweh, the God of their fathers, and worshipped the Baals and Astartes, the gods of the nations about them." Moore is explicit about the passage's purpose: "It is clear that in all this the author's purpose is not merely to interpret the history, and explain upon religious principles why such evils befell Israel in the days of the judges, but to impress upon his readers the lesson that unfaithfulness to Yahweh is always punished." The double notice of Joshua's death — first at the end of Joshua (24:29–30), then again at Judges 2:6–10 — is therefore not a lapse to be explained away but the hinge on which the new book turns: one generation ends, another begins, and the introduction surveys in advance the recurring cycle of defection, subjugation, and deliverance that will follow.[^source-moore-judges-icc-introduction-section-3]

The question of when and how this introduction was composed belongs to the wider discussion of the Deuteronomistic History. In 1943 Martin Noth proposed that Deuteronomy through Kings were originally an extended history of Israel written by a single author or compiler, whom he called the Deuteronomist, unified by a common chronology and ideology and structured around summative speeches such as Joshua 23 and 1 Samuel 12. The purpose of that unified history, on Noth's proposal, was to explain the falls of Israel and Judah as punishment for a history of disobedience to Yahweh. Noth's theory was widely accepted, though modifications were proposed from the start, and the current position is more complex: Thomas Römer has argued for a school of court scribes who maintained a library of scrolls revised under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period — a synthesis that retains Noth's basic unity while acknowledging a much more complex process of composition. That history of the theory itself is the reason the paragraph's caution holds: a sequence in the final text alone cannot establish precisely when each passage was written. One precision must be kept: Moore himself dates the work to the 6th century on the basis of its motive and aim, so he must not be presented as dating individual passages from sequence alone; and this article does not assert Noth's single-author thesis, citing the Bible Odyssey summary only for the history of the theory and its complication.[^source-moore-judges-icc-introduction-section-3]

### Baals and Ashtaroth in the wider Levant

The references to Baals and Ashtaroth situate the religious polemic within the vocabulary of the wider Levant. Local cults and divine titles were associated with fertility and communal identity, though any particular form of worship cannot be reconstructed simply from Judges 2. The narrator's concern is specifically Israel's loyalty to the LORD. The account should not become a blanket claim that every Canaanite community followed an identical set of rituals.

The divine names themselves point to that wider world. Ba'al — meaning "Lord" — is the god of rain, thunder, and fertility, whose winter rains are vital to agriculture; Astarte is a goddess of love and fertility, sometimes the consort of Baal or Hadad, and is also associated with sexuality and war. The plural forms in Judges 2 are significant: one could speak of many Baals and Astarths, as numerous as the cities of the land itself, with local manifestations and local titles rather than a single uniform cult. El, Baal, Asherah, and Astarte are distinguished and sometimes conflated in the sources, and the Ugaritic Baal Cycle supplies the mythic background for the storm god's battles and victories. None of this permits a reconstruction of what any particular community in Judges 2 actually did in worship; it establishes only the vocabulary within which the narrator's polemic operates — a vocabulary of fertility, local identity, and divine patronage set against exclusive loyalty to the LORD.[^source-baal-astarte-vocabulary]

The narrator's concern is therefore theological before it is ethnographic. The Baals and Ashtaroth are named not to catalogue Canaanite religion but to name the rival loyalties that draw Israel away. The passage refuses, by its own reticence, to become a field guide to Canaanite ritual: it gives divine titles and a charge of disloyalty, and nothing more. Any fuller picture of local worship must come from evidence outside Judges 2, and even then it must not be read back into the chapter as if every community named there practiced an identical set of rites.

### The nations that remain

Judges 3:1–6 names groups that remain in the land, including Philistine rulers and Canaanite populations. Such lists function within the story as an explanation for the continuing religious and political challenge. They are not a modern census of the early Iron Age. The narrative links marriages and worship in an explicitly covenantal argument; modern readers must identify that argument without replacing it with unsupported demographic claims.

The list is specific and structured. The nations named are the five lords of the Philistines, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites who lived in Mount Lebanon, from Mount Baal-hermon to Lebo-hamath (3:3). The text gives three stated purposes for their remaining: to test Israel, so that the generations who had not known war might learn warfare, and — as the narrative immediately adds — because Israel intermarried with them and served their gods (3:4–6). The Philistine "lords" are a political detail worth noting: the Philistines are presented not as a single king's subjects but as a league of five city rulers, a structure that will matter again in the Samson and Ark narratives. But the list's function in this chapter is argumentative, not statistical: it explains why the test continues, why the temptation persists, and why the cycle of chapter 2 has material to work on. It is not a count of populations, territories, or troop strengths, and it must not be used as one.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

## Why This Context Matters

The introduction establishes the interpretive lens for the book: generational forgetfulness, covenant infidelity, distress, and undeserved relief. It also resists mechanical application. Not every deliverance follows every step, and some later leaders become part of Israel's troubles.

The historical background of religious plurality and changing regional power helps illuminate why Israel's exclusive worship was such a central concern in the narrative. The literary setting keeps attention on what Judges itself claims about the LORD's response rather than treating the cycle as a verified formula for dating events.

That resistance to mechanism is the point a reader should carry forward. The cycle in Judges 2:11–23 is the book's own idealized pattern — disobedience, oppression, a cry, a deliverer, rest, relapse — and the narrator states it plainly so that the reader can recognize it. But the book then shows deliverers who are flawed, victories that are partial, and relapses that deepen, and it includes figures like Shamgar who receive no full version of the pattern at all. Reading the introduction as a rigid checklist would flatten exactly the variation the narratives insist on. Read as a theological frame, it explains why the book returns so insistently to the same question: whether Israel will remember the LORD who delivered them, once the generation that knew his works has passed.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]
