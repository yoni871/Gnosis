## Overview



The book of 2 Samuel does not end with a battle or a deathbed. It ends with a song, a last word, a roll of names, and a plague stayed at an altar. The song is David's thanksgiving for deliverance, sung looking back over his whole reign — out of the hand of all his enemies, and out of the hand of Saul [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The last words are a short oracle about the just ruler, spoken by a man who admits in the same breath that his own house is "not so with God," and yet holds to an everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The roll of names is the mighty men: the three who broke through an enemy host for a cup of water, the man who slew a lion in a pit in time of snow, and the thirty, closing on Uriah the Hittite — the man David had killed [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then comes the book's last story. The LORD's anger is kindled against Israel, David orders a census of the fighting men, and after nine months and twenty days the count comes back: eight hundred thousand in Israel, five hundred thousand in Judah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David's heart smites him. He confesses he has sinned greatly and done very foolishly, and the prophet Gad offers him three choices: seven years of famine, three months fleeing before his enemies, or three days of pestilence [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David chooses the pestilence, to fall into the hand of the LORD rather than the hand of man, for His mercies are great [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Seventy thousand die from Dan to Beersheba before the angel is stayed at the threshingfloor of Araunah the Jebusite, and David buys the floor for fifty shekels of silver, builds an altar, and offers burnt and peace offerings — "neither will I offer burnt offerings unto the LORD my God of that which doth cost me nothing" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The LORD is entreated for the land, and the plague is stayed [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. That is where the book ends: not with a succession, but with an altar bought at a price.

## Ancient World



### A song sung looking back

The longest chapter in 2 Samuel is a poem, and it tells no story at all. David's song of deliverance runs to fifty-one verses, and its heading places it after the whole of his reign: "in the day that the LORD had delivered him out of the hand of all his enemies, and out of the hand of Saul" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. It is a thanksgiving song in the old pattern — distress, a cry for help, God coming in storm, deliverance, and praise — and its storm is vivid: the earth shakes, smoke rises, fire devours, God bows the heavens and rides on a cherub, thunder rolls, arrows fly, and the channels of the sea are laid bare [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Out of that storm comes the singer's rescue: "He sent from above, he took me; he drew me out of many waters… He brought me forth also into a large place: he delivered me, because he delighted in me" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The song is also the near-twin of Psalm 18, which carries the same poem in the psalter; the two texts differ in small details, and no claim here is built on those differences [^source-jfb-commentary]. What matters for the reader is the song's position. It does not belong to any one battle. It is David looking back over everything — the fugitive years, the civil war, the conquests, the rebellion of his own son — and singing of deliverance as a whole. The song's hardest lines are its claim of innocence: "The LORD rewarded me according to my righteousness: according to the cleanness of my hands" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The reader who has followed the book knows about Bathsheba and Uriah, and the song does not answer that knowledge. It stands beside it, in the song's own voice, and the book leaves the two together.

### The last words and the men who stood by David

After the song comes a shorter piece, introduced as "the last words of David" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David calls himself the man raised up on high, the anointed of the God of Jacob, the sweet psalmist of Israel, and says, "The Spirit of the LORD spake by me, and his word was in my tongue" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-jfb-commentary]. The oracle that follows is a portrait of the just ruler: he must be just, ruling in the fear of God, like the light of the morning when the sun rises without clouds, like tender grass after rain [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then comes the verse that is the chapter's centre: "Although my house be not so with God; yet he hath made with me an everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The house has not lived up to the morning light; the covenant stands anyway. That is the book's last word on David's house — an admission and a promise in a single breath, held together by a covenant presented as everlasting and sure, of the unconditional kind rather than a bargain with conditions [^source-bible-odyssey-covenant]. Then the roster. The mighty men are listed in grades — the first three, the three who fetched the Bethlehem water, Abishai and Benaiah, and then the thirty — and their exploits are the things a warrior band remembered: Eleazar fighting until his hand clave to his sword, Shammah holding a lentil field alone, Benaiah killing a lion in a pit in time of snow [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-jfb-commentary]. The most famous exploit is the water. David, holed up at Adullam with a Philistine garrison in Bethlehem, longs aloud for water from the well by the gate; three men break through the host to fetch it, and David pours it out to the LORD rather than drink it — "is not this the blood of the men that went in jeopardy of their lives?" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The roster's names reach back across the whole book, and it closes on the name the reader least expects to find honoured there: Uriah the Hittite, the man David had killed, counted among the thirty-seven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### Counting the people

The book's last story begins with anger: "Again the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David orders Joab to number the people from Dan to Beersheba, and Joab protests — why does the king delight in this thing? — but the king's word prevails [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The count takes nine months and twenty days, a circuit of the whole kingdom from Aroer across the Jordan, north to Zidon and Tyre, and down through the south of Judah to Beersheba [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The sum is eight hundred thousand fighting men in Israel and five hundred thousand in Judah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The text never says why numbering the people was wrong. It gives no motive for the census and no explanation of the sin; Joab's protest goes unanswered, and only after the count does David's heart smite him and he confesses he has sinned greatly and done very foolishly [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Commentators have supplied motives — pride in numbers, self-confidence instead of trust in God, a king mustering an army for conquest — but the narrative itself leaves the question open [^source-jfb-commentary]. What the text does show is the shape of the sin: a king counting his strength, and a prophet then offering him three punishments to choose from. David chooses the pestilence, to fall into the hand of the LORD rather than the hand of man, because His mercies are great [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Seventy thousand die from Dan to Beersheba before the angel is stayed at the threshingfloor of Araunah the Jebusite, and David pleads that the sheep have done nothing — let the LORD's hand be against him and his father's house [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What disease the pestilence was, the text does not say; modern proposals, including bubonic plague, are debated and rest on no symptoms the text gives [^source-freemon-bubonic-plague-book-of-samuel][^source-griffin-plague-rats-bible-again].

### The threshingfloor and the altar

A threshingfloor was an open, level, beaten surface, usually on high ground outside a settlement, where grain was threshed and winnowed in the wind [^source-biblicaltraining-threshing-floor]. Archaeologists working at Iron Age Tel Megiddo have set out the marks by which such floors can be recognised in the ground — the surfaces, the installations, the residues of threshing — but no excavated floor has been identified as Araunah's [^source-shahack-gross-gafri-finkelstein-threshing-floors-megiddo]. Araunah himself is a Jebusite, a member of the people from whom David took the stronghold of Zion, still holding land in Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. When Gad tells David to build an altar on his floor, Araunah offers everything — the floor, the oxen for burnt sacrifice, the threshing instruments for wood — "as a king" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-jfb-commentary]. David refuses the gift: "Nay; but I will surely buy it of thee at a price: neither will I offer burnt offerings unto the LORD my God of that which doth cost me nothing" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He pays fifty shekels of silver for the floor and the oxen, builds the altar, and offers burnt offerings and peace offerings, the two distinct rites of the sacrificial system [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-gane-sacrifice-old-testament]. And the LORD is entreated for the land, and the plague is stayed from Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The book ends there, on an altar bought at a price, on ground that a later tradition would call Mount Moriah, the site of the temple — an identification the Chronicler makes and Samuel does not [^source-jfb-commentary].

## Why This Context Matters



The close of 2 Samuel refuses to tidy David up. The song claims clean hands, and the book has shown the hands bloody; the last words admit the house is not as it should be, and the covenant stands anyway; the roster honours Uriah, the man David killed; the final story is a census, a confession, and a plague. Knowing the world of these chapters — the thanksgiving song sung looking back, the warrior band remembered by name, the census that took nine months, the threshingfloor where grain was winnowed in the wind — keeps the reader from turning the ending into either a triumph or a tragedy. It is both at once, and the book's last sentence is neither. David buys the ground, builds the altar, and offers what cost him something, and the LORD is entreated for the land. The plague is stayed, the book closes, and the king's story ends not with his death but with worship on ground he paid for. That is the note 2 Samuel leaves ringing: deliverance remembered, a house admitted wanting, a covenant kept, and an altar bought at a price.
