## Overview


Two kingdoms, two clocks. In Judah, one king, Asa, reigns forty-one years. In Israel, five kings pass in a single decade, and a sixth takes the throne by civil war. These two chapters are the record of that difference — the succession itself, told king by king, house by house [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text].

It opens in Jerusalem. Abijam reigns three years, walking in his father's sins, and is kept on the throne only because a lamp is held for David's sake: "for David's sake did the LORD his God give him a lamp in Jerusalem, to set up his son after him, and to establish Jerusalem" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The lamp is granted for another man's sake, and David's record carries the text's own exception — "save only in the matter of Uriah the Hittite" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Then comes Asa, who reigns forty-one years and does right as David did: he clears out the idols, deposes his own mother Maachah from being queen for her idol in a grove, and burns it by the brook Kidron — "but the high places were not removed: nevertheless Asa's heart was perfect with the LORD all his days" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-jfb-commentary].

North of the border, the war never stops. Baasha builds Ramah to cut Judah off, and Asa answers by sending the temple's remaining treasure to Benhadad of Damascus, who strikes Israel's northern cities until Baasha abandons the work [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-mizpah]. Then Asa takes Ramah apart stone by stone and builds Geba and Mizpah from its ruins [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. In Israel, the kings fall faster than they rise. Nadab reigns two years before Baasha murders him at Gibbethon and wipes out Jeroboam's whole house [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Baasha reigns twenty-four years, and the prophet Jehu dooms his house in turn — dogs for the city dead, fowls for the field dead — "because he killed him" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Elah reigns two years and is murdered drunk by his chariot captain Zimri, who reigns seven days, wipes out Baasha's house, and burns the palace over himself when the army makes Omri king in the camp [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Omri outlasts a rival, Tibni, reigns twelve years, and buys a hill from a man named Shemer for two talents of silver, building the new capital Samaria on it [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. His son Ahab reigns twenty-two years, marries Jezebel of Sidon, and builds a temple for Baal in Samaria [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-ahab][^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. The chapters close with Hiel of Bethel rebuilding Jericho at the cost of his sons Abiram and Segub, "according to the word of the LORD, which he spake by Joshua the son of Nun" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. A word spoken at the conquest comes true in the reign of the worst king yet.

## Ancient World


### Five kings in a decade

Kingship in the ancient Near East was normally dynastic and hereditary in principle, though not necessarily through simple primogeniture — passing from the king to a son, preferably the first-born, or another male relative [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. Israel had begun differently: Saul was anointed privately by Samuel, chosen by lot in a public gathering, and confirmed by victory over the Ammonites [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. After Solomon, the two kingdoms parted ways. In Judah, God promised David that his house would rule forever, and the Davidic dynasty prevailed there until the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. In the north, dynastic succession was never fully established, because of rebellion, warfare, and coups; Israel was ruled by an alternating series of ruling families and independent kings until its destruction in 722 BC [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession].

These chapters are that sentence acted out. Judah has two kings; Israel has five, plus a civil war. The pattern repeats like a wheel. Baasha conspires against Nadab and exterminates Jeroboam's house, fulfilling Ahijah's word — and Jehu's word then dooms Baasha's house for the same sins, and for the killing itself [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Zimri conspires against Elah and exterminates Baasha's house, fulfilling Jehu's word — and the army makes Omri king in the camp the same day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Each usurper performs the prophecy against the house before him and earns the prophecy against his own. The verdicts escalate as the wheel turns: Nadab walks in his father's way; Baasha walks in the way of Jeroboam; Omri "did worse than all that were before him"; Ahab "did evil in the sight of the LORD above all that were before him" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The north does not decline gradually. It falls house by house, each house killing the last one and being killed by the next.

### The lamp in Jerusalem

"A lamp" in one's house is an Oriental phrase for continuance of family name and prosperity [^source-jfb-commentary]. Abijam is not rejected only in consequence of the divine promise to David [^source-jfb-commentary]. The promise behind the lamp is the word spoken to David: a house that would endure. The lamp is therefore not a reward for Abijam — his heart "was not perfect" — but a grant for David's sake, "to set up his son after him, and to establish Jerusalem" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. In the north there is no lamp. Each house is cut off "according to the word of the LORD," and the sentence is carried out by the next conspirator [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The contrast is the point of the two chapters, not a detail inside them: one kingdom survives an unworthy king because a lamp is kept, and the other cannot keep a house alive for a generation.

The same contrast runs through the border war. Baasha builds Ramah to cut off passage to and from Asa — a fortress on an eminence overhanging the ravine that separated Israel from Judah, only six Roman miles from Jerusalem [^source-jfb-commentary]. Asa answers with foreign gold: the treasure left in the house of the LORD and the king's house goes to Benhadad of Damascus, who strikes Ijon, Dan, Abel-beth-maachah, and all Cinneroth with Naphtali, until Baasha abandons Ramah and goes home to Tirzah [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-mizpah]. Then Asa takes Ramah apart and builds Geba and Mizpah from its stones and timber, with a levy on all Judah from which "none was exempted" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. What was built to shut Judah in is rebuilt to shut Israel out. Mizpah, the border town, would keep its military character for centuries: most scholars today identify it with Tell en-Nasbeh, where excavations uncovered a massive stone wall with towers and a strong gate, which many scholars link with Asa's work [^source-biblicaltraining-mizpah].

### A capital bought for silver

Omri reigned twelve years, six of them at Tirzah [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. He then bought the hill from Shemer for two talents of silver and built a city there, about 880 BC in the usual reckoning [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The site lies in the hill country of Ephraim, about seven miles northwest of ancient Shechem, about thirty-five miles north of Jerusalem; the hill stands on its own, surrounded by valleys, with steep sides and a broad, fairly level top [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. The earlier capitals, Shechem and Tirzah, lay in more exposed positions, and Tirzah had just been burned in civil war [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. A new city on a site with no prior tribal claims belonged wholly to the crown, much as Jerusalem had belonged to David [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Its western outlook also faced Phoenicia, with which Omri sealed an alliance through the marriage of his son Ahab to Jezebel, daughter of the king of Sidon [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria].

The name itself tells the story. The Hebrew name is *shomeron*, and it echoes the Hebrew root *shmr*, "to watch" or "to guard," so it is often rendered "watch post" — and the setting suits that meaning, since the summit commands a wide view in every direction [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. But Omri named the city after Shemer, the hill's former owner, not after himself [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The northern capital is founded by purchase, and it keeps the seller's name. Outside the Bible, Omri is remembered very differently from the way Kings remembers him. The Mesha Stele from Moab says that Omri subdued Moab, and Assyrian records continued for generations to call Israel "the house of Omri" [^source-biblicaltraining-ahab]. Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk describes Jehu, who had in fact overthrown that dynasty, as a "son of Omri" [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria]. Kings gives Omri six verses and the worst verdict yet; the ancient Near East remembered him by name. Excavations at Samaria have uncovered the Israelite palace, ivory inlays, and inscribed potsherds — the material traces of the capital Omri founded and Ahab adorned [^source-biblicaltraining-samaria][^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel].

### Baal in the new capital

Ahab built a temple for Baal in Samaria, set up an altar in it, and made an Asherah pole [^source-biblicaltraining-ahab]. Baal was the Canaanite storm god, believed to bring the rains on which farming depended, and his cult was bound up with fertility [^source-biblicaltraining-ahab]. The marriage that brought him there made sound political sense: Phoenicia was the leading maritime and commercial power of the day, and a royal marriage secured trade routes and protected Israel's northern flank [^source-biblicaltraining-ahab]. Yet the law had forbidden such unions precisely because foreign spouses would turn Israel's heart to other gods, and the account in Kings shows that warning coming true [^source-biblicaltraining-ahab]. Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, grew up in a court where the worship of Baal and of the goddess Astarte was the religion of the state [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. Which Baal she served is debated: many scholars identify him with Melqart, the chief god of Tyre, while others think of Baal Shamem, the "lord of heaven" [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. In either case the cult was the Phoenician one, not a local Canaanite survival [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel].

Two distinctions matter here, and the text keeps them straight. Asherah was the mother goddess of the Canaanites, and in the Bible the name also refers to a wooden cult object, the Asherah pole; Asherah and Ashtoreth are distinct in the Hebrew text, although both were associated with Baal worship [^source-biblicaltraining-ashtoreth]. Ashtoreth is the Hebrew form of Astarte, the goddess of the Sidonians, whom Solomon had followed in his old age [^source-biblicaltraining-ashtoreth]. The Bible does not name Ashtoreth in the stories of Ahab and Jezebel: the prophets Jezebel supported were prophets of Baal and of Asherah, a different goddess [^source-biblicaltraining-ashtoreth]. Ahab's offence is therefore stated twice over — the house of Baal with its altar, and the Asherah — and the verdict is comparative: he "did more to provoke the LORD God of Israel to anger than all the kings of Israel that were before him" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The chapters close on the same note of escalation. In Ahab's days Hiel of Bethel rebuilds Jericho, laying its foundation in his firstborn Abiram and its gates in his youngest son Segub, "according to the word of the LORD, which he spake by Joshua the son of Nun" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. A curse spoken at the conquest waits centuries and is fulfilled in the reign of the worst king yet — the book's own way of saying that the word outlasts the kings.

## Why This Context Matters


These two chapters are about what holds a kingdom together and what pulls it apart. Judah survives a bad king because a lamp is kept in Jerusalem for David's sake; Israel cannot keep a house alive for a generation, because each house rises by conspiracy and falls by prophecy. Knowing that contrast keeps the reader from treating the northern kings as a list of names. Each one is a turn of the same wheel: the murder at Gibbethon, the drunken murder at Tirzah, seven days on the throne, a king made in the camp, a capital bought for silver. And the wheel stops at Samaria, where Omri builds a city that will outlast his house by centuries, and where his son sets up Baal's altar beside the throne. The world of these chapters is a world of border forts built from an enemy's stones, of temple gold spent to hire a foreign king, and of a word spoken at Jericho's fall coming true in Ahab's reign. The kings change every few years; the word does not change at all.
