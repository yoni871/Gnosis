## Overview



The prophet dies, and the two kingdoms begin to die with him. Elisha's death opens the passage, and nothing after it stops the decline — only a reprieve interrupts it. Joash of Israel weeps over the dying prophet with Elisha's own cry over Elijah, "O my father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof"[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text], and the last oracle of the ministry gives him three victories over Syria, no more. The king smites the ground thrice and stays his hand, and the arithmetic is the oracle: thrice, not five or six times.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Then the prophet is buried, and a dead man cast into his sepulchre revives at the touch of his bones — the last miracle of the ministry, worked after the prophet's death.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]

Amaziah of Judah defeats Edom and is lifted up, and the thistle asks the cedar for its daughter; the parable is answered in battle at Beth-shemesh, where Judah flees and Jerusalem's wall is broken, and the temple's gold is carried to Samaria — by Israel, not by a foreign king.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Jeroboam II, who does evil, restores Israel's coast from Hamath to the sea of the plain, "according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which he spake by the hand of his servant Jonah, the son of Amittai"[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jonah], and the text gives the reason plainly: "the LORD said not that he would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven: but he saved them by the hand of Jeroboam."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] It is the longest reign the north ever has, and it is also the last.

Chapter 15 moves fast: six kings in thirty-eight verses — Zachariah six months, Shallum a full month, Menahem, Pekahiah, Pekah, Hoshea — and the fourth-generation promise made to Jehu is fulfilled, "And so it came to pass."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Assyria enters as a payee, taking a thousand talents of silver from Menahem, and returns as a conqueror, carrying Naphtali captive.[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] In Judah, Ahaz meets the Syro-Ephraimite crisis by buying Assyria: "I am thy servant and thy son."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] He copies the altar he sees in Damascus and has the priest build it before he returns; the old brasen altar is not removed but demoted, kept for the king "to enquire by."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The house itself is altered "for the king of Assyria."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The reprieve of 13:23 — "the LORD... would not destroy them, neither cast he them from his presence as yet"[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] — still stands. But the word "as yet" already dates the fall, and it is coming in chapter 17.

## Ancient World



### The prophet's last oracle

Elisha's death is the passage's hinge. The ministry began in 1 Kings 19, when Elijah cast his mantle on a ploughman of Abel Meholah; Elisha asked for a double portion of his spirit, the firstborn's share, and received it when he saw the chariot of fire carry Elijah away.[^source-biblicaltraining-elisha] He cried over that departure, "My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof" — the same words Joash speaks over his deathbed, with an added "O."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha] The ministry's last oracle is given on a sickbed: the bow and arrows, the hands laid on the king's hands, the window opened eastward, and the arrow of the LORD's deliverance shot toward Aphek.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Then the king smites the ground thrice and stays his hand, and the man of God is wroth: five or six times would have consumed Syria, but thrice will only beat it three times.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The three victories of 13:25 match the three smitings exactly, and the text never explains why the king stopped. The prophet dies, and the last miracle comes after his death: a dead man, thrown hastily into the sepulchre when a Moabite band is spied, revives at the touch of Elisha's bones and stands on his feet.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The text gives the dead man no name and no explanation.

### Aram and the deliverer

For a generation the enemy has been Aram: Hazael, whom Elisha anointed at Damascus by the LORD's word at Horeb, and his son Benhadad.[^source-biblicaltraining-ben-hadad][^source-bible-odyssey-syria] Hazael's oppression has reduced Jehoahaz's army to fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and ten thousand footmen, "like the dust by threshing."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Jehoahaz besought the LORD, and the LORD heard — and then, in parentheses, the deliverance: "the LORD gave Israel a saviour, so that they went out from under the hand of the Syrians: and the children of Israel dwelt in their tents, as beforetime."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The saviour is not named, and the dossier does not name him: the deliverance is real, the worship is unchanged — "there remained the grove also in Samaria" — and the next chapter opens with the same sins.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The deliverances that can be named are Jehoash's three victories over Benhadad (13:25) and the LORD's saving of Israel by the hand of Jeroboam II (14:25–27) — "the LORD said not that he would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The reprieve is stated as the LORD's own restraint, tied to the covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and dated by a single word: "as yet."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Damascus itself, the Aramean capital that has oppressed Israel for a generation, is recovered for Israel under Jeroboam — Damascus and Hamath, "which belonged to Judah," stated without explanation.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Aram's long war against Israel ends not in a battle but in an Assyrian deportation.

### The north's last strength

Jeroboam II reigns forty-one years, the longest reign the north ever has, and the text measures him the same way it measures every northern king: he does evil, departing not from the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Yet it is in his reign that the coast is restored from Hamath to the sea of the plain, "according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which he spake by the hand of his servant Jonah, the son of Amittai, the prophet, which was of Gathhepher."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Jonah is named here once — the only reference to him outside his own book; his prophecy's words are not recorded, and the book of Jonah's narrative is not the verse's story.[^source-biblicaltraining-jonah] The restoration returns Israel's borders to roughly what Solomon held — from Lebo Hamath to the Dead Sea — and it happens while Assyria is weak: a half-century of revolts, plague, and the solar eclipse of 15 June 763 BC, during which Jeroboam II and Uzziah both enlarge their kingdoms.[^source-biblicaltraining-jonah][^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] The two reigns overlap: Uzziah's fifty-two years in Judah, and Jotham's sixteen after him, each judged by the same formula — he does right, "save that the high places were not removed."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The north's last strength is real: territory recovered, a long reign, a coast restored. It is also the last — Zachariah, Jeroboam's son, reigns six months.

### Assyria enters — tribute, then deportation

Assyria has been on the horizon since 853 BC, when Shalmaneser III fought a coalition at Qarqar that included "Ahab the Israelite" with 2,000 chariots and 10,000 foot soldiers, and since 841 BC, when Jehu bowed before the same king on the Black Obelisk, the inscription calling him "son of Omri."[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] But Assyria enters the book of 2 Kings only in chapter 15, and it enters as a payee. Pul — Tiglath-pileser III, whom the Bible also calls Pul, his throne-name — comes against the land, and Menahem buys him off with a thousand talents of silver, exacted from the mighty men of wealth at fifty shekels each, "that his hand might be with him to confirm the kingdom in his hand."[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] The transaction is recorded by the Assyrian side too: in 738 BC Tiglath-pileser received tribute from Rezin of Damascus and Menahem of Samaria.[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] The same king returns in Pekah's reign, takes Ijon, Abelbethmaachah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali, "and carried them captive to Assyria" — places named, no headcount given.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] It is the first deportation in the book: tribute first, then a puppet king, then annexation and the carrying away of the people.[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] Hoshea, who murders Pekah, is installed by Tiglath-pileser's own claim; the fall that "as yet" already dates is one reign away.

### Ahaz and the copied altar

In Judah the decline takes a different shape: not a fast-motion collapse but a deliberate imitation. Ahaz, sixteen years in Jerusalem, does not that which is right like David his father; he walks in the way of the kings of Israel, "yea, and made his son to pass through the fire, according to the abominations of the heathen."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] When Rezin and Pekah besiege Jerusalem and cannot overcome him, Ahaz sends to Tiglath-pileser, "I am thy servant and thy son: come up, and save me out of the hand of the king of Syria, and out of the hand of the king of Israel."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The silver and gold of the house of the LORD and of the king's house go as a present — the same treasure Asa sent to Benhadad and Jehoash sent to Hazael — and Damascus falls: its people carried to Kir, Rezin slain.[^source-biblicaltraining-assyria] Ahaz goes to Damascus to meet his new master, sees an altar there, and sends Urijah the priest its fashion and pattern "according to all the workmanship thereof."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The priest builds it before the king returns, and Ahaz offers on it when he comes. Then the brasen altar is moved to the north side, and the great altar takes all the offerings, morning and evening. The old altar is not removed. It is demoted: "the brasen altar shall be for me to enquire by."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The house itself is altered, the bases cut off, the sea taken from the brasen oxen, the sabbath covert turned — "for the king of Assyria."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Isaiah 7 tells the same crisis from the prophet's side: Isaiah tells Ahaz not to fear "the two tails of these smoking firebrands" and offers a sign, which Ahaz refuses. Hezekiah will undo the altar work two reigns later; the dial of Ahaz will still bear his name.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]

### The fast-motion collapse

Chapter 15 is the book's fastest chapter. Six kings in thirty-eight verses: Zachariah, who reigns six months and dies by conspiracy; Shallum, who reigns a full month and dies the same way; Menahem, who takes the throne from Tirzah and smites Tiphsah because it opened not to him; Pekahiah, who reigns two years and is murdered in the palace with Argob and Arieh and fifty Gileadites; Pekah, who reigns twenty years and loses Naphtali to Assyria; Hoshea, who murders Pekah and takes the throne "in the twentieth year of Jotham" — though Jotham reigns sixteen years.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The notices do the telling: each reign shorter, each succession a conspiracy, each king measured by the same formula. The fourth-generation promise made to Jehu — his sons shall sit on the throne unto the fourth generation — is fulfilled at 15:12, and the text closes it in four words: "And so it came to pass."[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] The north's kings no longer die in their beds; they die by the sword of the man who replaces them. The throne of Israel has become a place men take and lose, not a house men inherit. Meanwhile in Judah the kings die in their beds and are buried in the city of David, and the lamp given for David's sake keeps burning while the north's houses are cut off one by one.[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] One kingdom collapses in a generation; the other endures by a promise it does not deserve. The fall of Samaria is one reign away, and the word "as yet" has already dated it.

## Why This Context Matters



This category matters because it is where the reprieve is stated and where it runs out. The LORD "would not destroy them, neither cast he them from his presence as yet"[^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] — and the word "as yet" is the whole of the passage's theology, a mercy that has a date. The deliverance in chapter 13 is real and unnamed; the salvation in chapter 14 comes through an evil king; the restoration of the coast comes by a prophet whose words are not recorded. None of it is earned, and none of it lasts. The north's longest reign and its fastest collapse sit in the same category, and the reader is meant to hold both: Jeroboam's forty-one years and Zachariah's six months are the same story, told at two speeds. In Judah, Ahaz shows the other shape of decline — not conspiracy but imitation, a king who copies a foreign altar and alters his own house for the king of Assyria, and keeps the old altar only as his private oracle. The passage matters for what comes next: the fall of Samaria in chapter 17, the reform of Hezekiah in chapter 18, and the sign read on the dial of Ahaz in chapter 20 are all already present here, in promise and in shadow. Read it as the book reads it: as a reprieve, not a recovery.
