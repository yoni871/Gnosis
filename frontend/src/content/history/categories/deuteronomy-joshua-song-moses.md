## Overview

Deuteronomy 31–32 is the farewell's closing movement: the transfer of leadership, the writing of the law, and the commissioning of a song that will stand as a witness against the people who sing it. Moses is 120 years old and can no more go out and come in; the LORD has said he shall not go over Jordan. He encourages the people — "Be strong and of a good courage, fear not, nor be afraid of them: for the LORD thy God, he it is that doth go with thee; he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee" — and then calls Joshua and charges him in the sight of all Israel to go with the people into the land sworn to their fathers and cause them to inherit it, with the LORD going before him and not failing or forsaking him.

Moses writes "this law" and delivers it to the priests, the sons of Levi who bear the ark of the covenant, and to all the elders of Israel, ordering it to be read before all Israel — men, women, children, and the stranger within the gates — at the end of every seven years, in the year of release, at the Feast of Tabernacles, so that they may hear, learn, fear the LORD, and observe all the words of the law.[^source-jfb-commentary] The LORD then summons Moses and Joshua to the tabernacle, appears in a pillar of cloud over its door, foretells Israel's apostasy after Moses' death, and commands a song to be written and taught, "that this song may be a witness for me against the children of Israel."

The Song itself, Deuteronomy 32:1–43, is the longest poem in the book. It opens by summoning heaven and earth as witnesses, sets God's perfection — "He is the Rock, his work is perfect" — against Israel's corruption, recalls God's care from the division of the nations to the abundance of the land, and then turns to apostasy, judgment, restraint for God's name's sake, and a closing vindication that ends with the nations summoned to rejoice with his people. Moses and Hoshea speak it to the people, and Moses exhorts them to set their hearts on all the words, "for it is not a vain thing for you; because it is your life." The same day the LORD commands Moses to ascend Mount Nebo, view the land of Canaan, and die there, because of the trespass at the waters of Meribah-Kadesh.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing]

No absolute year is given for any of this, and the category's date is unknown. The Song's own date is debated, and the ancient witnesses differ at two verses — 32:8 and 32:43 — in ways that change the meaning. This page presents the witness readings as they stand, records where scholars disagree about which reading came first, and treats explanations of why the readings differ as hypotheses, not as attested events.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-anselm-project-deuteronomy-32-8]

## Ancient World

### Succession in the sight of all Israel

Joshua's commission in Deuteronomy 31 is the third of three distinct moments, and they must not be collapsed into one. The earlier designation came in Numbers 27: the LORD told Moses to take Joshua, "a man in whom is the spirit," lay his hand on him, and set him before Eleazar the priest and the congregation; Deuteronomy 3:28 records the command to charge, encourage, and strengthen Joshua, "for he shall go over before this people." The second is Moses' public charge in 31:7–8, "in the sight of all Israel." The third is the LORD's own charge in 31:23: "thou shalt bring the children of Israel into the land which I sware unto them: and I will be with thee." Joshua 1:1–9 repeats the charge after Moses' death.

The tabernacle appearance confirms the appointment: the LORD summons Moses and Joshua to the tabernacle, "that I may give him a charge," and appears in a pillar of cloud over the door.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-jfb-commentary] Moses, who led Israel out of Egypt, does not lead them in; Joshua does.

### The written law and its public reading

The deposit of the written law is the book's own answer to the forgetting the Song names. When Moses had made an end of writing the words of the law in a book, he commanded the Levites who bear the ark to take the book and "put it in the side of the ark of the covenant of the LORD your God, that it may be there for a witness against thee" — against Israel, because of their rebellion and stiff neck. Whether the book was placed beside the ark or within it is not settled: 1 Kings 8:9 says the ark contained only the tables of stone, while Hebrews 9:4 attests other things once inside it.[^source-jfb-commentary]

The scope of "this law" is likewise unsettled — "either the whole book of Deuteronomy, or the important part of it contained between the twenty-seventh and thirtieth chapters," in one commentary's words. The reading ordinance is prospective: at the end of every seven years, in the year of release, at the Feast of Tabernacles, the law is to be read "before all Israel in their hearing," including children "which have not known any thing."[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-jfb-commentary]

### The Song as witness

The Song's stated function is forensic, not celebratory: "that this song may be a witness for me against the children of Israel," and "this song shall testify against them as a witness; for it shall not be forgotten out of the mouths of their seed." Its witnesses are heaven and earth, the covenant renewal's witnesses re-invoked. The mechanism is the one a commentary states plainly: "National songs take deep hold of the memories and have a powerful influence in stirring the deepest feelings of a people."[^source-jfb-commentary] Moses wrote the song "the same day" and taught it, then spoke it "in the ears of all the congregation of Israel, until they were ended."

How the Song functioned in Israel's actual worship is debated, and the Song names no specific historical event as the fulfilment of its judgments.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing]

### The Song itself

One scholar outlines the Song as overture (verses 1–7); Yahweh's care for Jacob (8–15a); apostasy and punishment (15a–25); Yahweh's change of mind (26–27); and the enemies' punishment and Jacob's restoration (28–43).[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing]

The Rock (צור, *tsur*) is the Song's governing image for God, appearing in verses 4, 15, 18, 30, 31, and 37. The eagle of verse 11 is the tenderest image in the Song: the female eagle stirring the nest, fluttering over the young, bearing them on her wings — the Hebrew ירחף, *yeracheph*, "broodeth over."[^source-clarke-commentary] "Jeshurun" (verse 15) is the poetic name for Israel, from ישר, *yashar*, "upright" — and the name's irony is the point: the once-upright people "waxed fat, and kicked."[^source-clarke-commentary]

The judgment turns on the jealousy reversal of verse 21: they moved God to jealousy with what is not God, so he will move them to jealousy with those who are not a people. The restraint of verses 26–27 is for God's name's sake. The vengeance is stored up — "To me belongeth vengeance, and recompence" — and the vindication ends with the monotheistic climax, "See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me." The Song is poetry, built on parallelism, and was written colographically at Qumran, on a separate scroll.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-notarius-archaic-hebrew-poetry]

### Two verses with divided witnesses

At Deuteronomy 32:8 the Masoretic Text reads "sons of Israel" (KJV "children of Israel"). The Qumran scroll 4QDeut-j, from about the first century BCE, reads "sons of God," and the Septuagint reflects divine or angelic beings: most Greek manuscripts read "angels of God," a few "sons of God," and the Göttingen text adopts "sons of God."[^source-anselm-project-deuteronomy-32-8][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy] Heiser (2001), read in full, corroborates the witness table and records Wevers's view that the majority Greek reading was "clearly a later attempt to avoid any notion of lesser deities."[^source-heiser-deuteronomy-32-8] A reconstructed original "sons of El" is a scholarly conjecture, not a manuscript reading: no witness reads it.[^source-anselm-project-deuteronomy-32-8]

The priority of the readings is disputed. Most scholars, as one scholar reports, regard "children of God/gods" as probably the best reading; Keil and Delitzsch, writing before Qumran, judged the Greek "angels" rendering "of no critical value."[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-keil-delitzsch-deuteronomy] Explanations of the variant by editorial motive — copyists softening a polytheistic wording, or the Greek "angels" reading as a deliberate avoidance of lesser deities — are hypotheses about scribal intention, not attested events. No ancient source attests the motive.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-heiser-deuteronomy-32-8]

At Deuteronomy 32:43 the Masoretic Text has the verse, in a shorter form of four cola: "Rejoice, O ye nations, with his people." The verse is commonly counted at four cola in the Masoretic Text, six in 4QDeut-q, and eight in the Septuagint — the Greek eight counted directly, the six resting on a Dead Sea Scrolls overview (Feldman), with 4QDeut-q dated 50–1 BCE. The critical edition has not been read directly, so the count stays Probable.[^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy][^source-academia-dss-deuteronomy] The longer forms are not identical: Qumran has the heavens rejoicing and "all gods" bowing down, while the Septuagint has "all the divine sons" doing obeisance and "all the angels of God" prevailing.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy]

Hebrews 1:6 — "And let all the angels of God worship him" — follows the longer Greek/Qumran text-form of 32:43, not the Masoretic Text. But the exact wording is an adaptation, and Psalm 97:7 ("worship him, all ye gods") is a live alternative source, so which passage Hebrews draws on is not settled. Romans 15:10, by contrast, quotes the Masoretic form of 32:43.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy] The stored King James text checked for this range has no differences in chapter 31 and two accepted variants in chapter 32: the capitalisation of "Most High" in 32:8 and the possessive casing in 32:9.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### Date and genre — both debated

The Song's date is disputed between two live positions. The archaic side rests on the Song's linguistic features — the relative pronoun זו, *zu*, instead of אשר; unusual suffixes, spellings, and vocabulary also found in Ugaritic and Amarna Canaanite — and one scholar reports Sanders (1996) concluding a pre-exilic, possibly pre-monarchical date, before the eleventh century. The late side holds the poem was produced by Jerusalem scribal groups in the late sixth to early fifth century, with the archaic elements as deliberate archaizing.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing] The "late second millennium" formulation is not verified by a direct read and is not used here.

The genre is disputed in the same way. Before the 1950s the Song was commonly read as Moses' farewell speech — yet nothing in the poem itself suggests a farewell speech, and it may have existed independently of its narrative framework. Since Wright (1962), the popular view is that the Song is a *rîb*, a prophetic lawsuit imitating court proceedings — though parts of the poem do not fit court proceedings. Other genres are present in parts. The open verdict is that it is "a real song after all, but drawing on elements from many different genres."[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing]

### What archaeology does and does not show

There is no direct archaeological evidence for the commission of Joshua, the writing or deposit of the law, the composition or performance of the Song, or Moses' ascent of Nebo. No inscription, sanctuary, artefact, or settlement has been identified as evidence for any of them — a negative fact, stated plainly. A leadership transfer, a written deposit, and a song taught by rote leave little that archaeology could recover even in principle.

The Qumran scrolls are the archaeological fact for the Song's text, not for its events: first-century BCE Hebrew copies in stichographic layout establish its textual plurality and its special status at Qumran — a separate scroll — not its date, author, or occasion.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing][^source-notarius-archaic-hebrew-poetry] Mount Nebo, in the Abarim range in Moab over against Jericho, is traditionally identified with Jabal Nebo; no excavation has identified the mountain as the place of Moses' death, and the identification belongs to the following category.

## Why This Context Matters

These two chapters are the hinge on which Deuteronomy turns from speech to succession. Moses, who led Israel out of Egypt, does not lead them in; the leadership passes in public, before the priest and the congregation, with the LORD's own charge confirming it, and the law passes with it — written down, deposited beside the ark, and ordered to be read aloud to the whole people, children and strangers included, every seventh year.[^source-jfb-commentary] The book's answer to forgetting is therefore twofold: a written copy kept with the sanctuary's most sacred object, and a song put in the people's mouths, so that the words will be known before they are broken.

The Song is the book's memory in poetic form — God's care remembered, apostasy named in advance, judgment announced and then restrained for God's name's sake, and a closing summons in which the nations rejoice with his people. Its afterlife confirms its weight: Paul quotes it three times, naming Moses as its speaker, and the victors in Revelation sing "the song of Moses the servant of God." What the passage leaves unresolved is left unresolved here too: the Song's date and genre, the priority of the readings at 32:8, and whether Hebrews 1:6 draws on Deuteronomy 32:43 or on Psalm 97:7 are recorded as they stand, not settled. The reader meets the text as it stands — a commission, a book, and a song laid up as a witness.[^source-nilsen-did-moses-sing]
