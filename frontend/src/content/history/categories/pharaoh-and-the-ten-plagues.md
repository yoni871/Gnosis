## Overview

Moses and Aaron go in to Pharaoh with the demand: "Let my people go, that they may hold a feast unto me in the wilderness." Pharaoh answers, "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go." He calls the demand idleness and orders the straw withheld: the people must gather their own straw and still deliver the same tally of bricks. When they fail, the Hebrew officers are beaten, appeal to Pharaoh in vain, and turn on Moses and Aaron. Moses returns to the LORD and asks why he has sent him, since Pharaoh has only done evil to the people since he came.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The LORD answers that Pharaoh shall now see what he will do: with a strong hand he shall let them go and drive them out. God speaks again as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, known to the patriarchs as God Almighty but not by his name JEHOVAH — a verse whose meaning is debated among commentators — and renews the covenant with a sevenfold promise of deliverance. The people cannot listen "for anguish of spirit, and for cruel bondage." The chapter then gives the genealogy of Reuben, Simeon, and Levi, down to Amram and Jochebed, the parents of Aaron and Moses, and closes by naming these as the men who spoke to Pharaoh.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The confrontation begins: the LORD makes Moses "a god to Pharaoh" and Aaron his prophet, and says Pharaoh's heart will be hardened and the wonders multiplied, so that "the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD." Moses is eighty and Aaron eighty-three. Before Pharaoh, Aaron's rod becomes a serpent; the magicians do the same with enchantments, but Aaron's rod swallows theirs, and Pharaoh's heart is hardened.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-clarke-commentary]

Then the plagues begin. The river turns to blood, and the Egyptians cannot drink; frogs cover the land and enter the houses; gnats (or lice) come from the dust, and the magicians cannot bring them forth: "This is the finger of God." Swarms of flies corrupt the land, but Goshen is severed, and Pharaoh bargains and breaks his word. Murrain kills Egypt's cattle while none of Israel's die. Boils break out on man and beast, and the magicians cannot stand before Moses. Hail smites all in the field while Goshen is spared; Pharaoh confesses and then sins yet more. Locusts eat all the hail has left; Pharaoh begs forgiveness and hardens his heart again. Darkness lies over Egypt three days while Israel has light, and Pharaoh offers to let the people go without their flocks — Moses answers that not a hoof shall be left behind. Pharaoh tells Moses to see his face no more. One plague more is announced: at midnight the firstborn of Egypt shall die, while against Israel not a dog shall move its tongue.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

The narrative never numbers the plagues; the count of ten is a later convention, and the title of this category follows that tradition rather than the text's own numbering. No date for the Exodus is given in the text, and the Pharaoh is never named, so the date of these events is unknown: an early dating around 1446 BCE and a late dating in the thirteenth century are both live scholarly positions, and the earliest extrabiblical reference to Israel, the Merneptah Stele of about 1209 BCE, fixes only that Israel already existed as a people in Canaan by then.[^source-bible-odyssey-plagues][^source-merneptah-stele]

## Ancient World

### Egypt and the brick-fields

The Egypt of this narrative is the Nile valley in its broad outlines: a river-centred civilisation living by the annual inundation, a divine kingship in which Pharaoh stood as ruler and son of the gods, and a priestly bureaucracy at court. The Nile was an object of worship, so the first plague strikes at the centre of Egyptian religion and economy at once.[^source-clarke-commentary][^source-jfb-commentary]

The burden of Exodus 5 is state corvée labour: straw tempered the mud brick, and the demand — gather your own straw, deliver the same tally — turns building into punishment. The Hebrew officers, foremen caught between the Egyptian taskmasters and their own people, are beaten first. The Hebrew word for the heavier work, *kaved*, is the same root as the "heavy" heart of Pharaoh, and as God's "glory" — a probable wordplay carried through to the sea.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon]

### The nine plagues and the tenth announced
Nine plagues are inflicted and a tenth announced. The waters become blood, the fish die, and the Egyptians dig for water; seven days pass, the only stated duration. Frogs come up into houses, ovens, and kneadingtroughs; Pharaoh names the removal himself — "To morrow." The third plague is *kinnim*, from the dust: "gnats" in the Septuagint and most modern versions, "lice" in the King James Version — the rendering is debated. The fourth is *'arov*, a swarm of unnamed species: the King James Version supplies "flies," the Septuagint "dog-fly."[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus]

From the fourth plague Goshen is severed, "that thou mayest know that I am the LORD in the midst of the earth"; the first three fall on Israel as well, and from the fourth the distinction is explicit and repeated. The murrain kills Egypt's cattle — "all" is popular usage, since cattle remain in the field later — while none of Israel's die, and Pharaoh sends to verify it. Boils break out on man and beast, and the magicians cannot stand before Moses. Hail falls with fire mingled in it, but not in Goshen; the flax and barley are smitten, the wheat and rye not yet grown — probably late winter or early spring. The east wind brings the locusts, and a mighty west wind casts them into the sea. Darkness that "may be felt" covers Egypt three days, while Israel has light. Then one plague more is announced: at midnight every firstborn in Egypt shall die.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

The plagues can be read as three triads — the first, second, fourth, fifth, seventh, and eighth announced beforehand, the third, sixth, and ninth without warning — a probable compositional observation, not a textual datum. The tenth differs in kind: a direct judgment by God, not mediated through a natural occurrence. Psalms 78 and 105 each list only seven plagues, in a different order.[^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus][^source-bible-odyssey-plagues]

Whether each plague targeted a specific Egyptian god is debated: the plagues demonstrate the LORD's supremacy over Egypt's gods, but one-to-one identifications falter on the story's vagueness about Egyptian religion. The plagues are based on Egyptian natural phenomena — the Nile's reddening, the frogs of the inundation, the rare hail, the east-wind locusts — made signs by excess and accumulation, a probable description of their setting. But naturalistic explanations of the plagues as historical events are debated: highly speculative, without support in ancient Egyptian records, and ignoring the narrative's escalating confrontation and miraculous intent.[^source-bible-odyssey-plagues][^source-keil-delitzsch-exodus]

### The hardening: three readings

Through the first five plagues the hardening is stated without explicit divine agency, or with Pharaoh as subject: "he hardened his heart." From the sixth plague, the boils, God is the explicit subject for the first time: "the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh." The pattern then alternates and closes with divine hardening in chapter 11. Three Hebrew verbs are involved — *chazaq*, to grow firm; *kaved*, to grow heavy; *qashah*, to be hard — and the Septuagint mirrors the variety with three Greek verbs of its own.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-nets-septuagint-exodus]

Three classic readings stand side by side, and none is adopted here. Clarke reads the hardening as permissive: God permits Pharaoh's stubbornness to remain. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown reads it as resultative: the divine message would be the occasion, not the cause, of the king's obduracy. Calvin reads it as sovereign appointment: on 9:16, God placed Pharaoh on the throne, or preserved him, so that his power might be displayed; on Romans 9:17, the same verse is Pharaoh's predestination to ruin. Paul's citation follows the Septuagint's "I have raised thee up" rather than its "thou hast been preserved" at Exodus 9:16. The correct reading is debated.[^source-clarke-commentary][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-calvin-harmony-of-the-law][^source-calvin-romans]

### The magicians

The magicians, the *chartummim* — possessors of occult knowledge — appear at the rod, the blood, the frogs, the gnats, and the boils, and their arc is a defeat in three stages: they imitate the staff-serpent, the blood, and the frogs; at the gnats they try and cannot, confessing, "This is the finger of God" — the only Old Testament instance of the phrase from a non-Israelite; at the boils they "could not stand before Moses." Their chief names, Jannes and Jambres, come only from 2 Timothy 3:8, a traditional identification.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bdb-lexicon]

How they imitated is debated: Jamieson-Fausset-Brown argues for sleight of hand — serpent-charmers press the nape of the neck to induce catalepsy, making the serpent stiff as a rod; Clarke argues for familiar spirits; Jubilees, that Mastema helped the sorcerers — "the evils indeed we permitted them to work, but the remedies we did not allow to be wrought by their hands."[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary][^source-charles-jubilees]

### Ancient witnesses: Josephus, Jubilees, Philo, Augustine
Josephus merges the flies and the murrain into one plague, so his ten are not Exodus's ten. His purpose is apologetic: to show that Moses "did not fail in any one thing that he foretold them." Jubilees lists the ten in Exodus's order, adding that "on all their idols the Lord took vengeance and burned them with fire."[^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-charles-jubilees]

Philo treats the plagues as a philosophic theodicy, beginning with water because the Egyptians honoured water as the first principle of all things. The lice show God admonishing through insignificant agents — "what can be more insignificant than a louse?" Philo divides the ten by element and minister — three by Aaron, three by Moses, one by both, three by God alone — reordering them to fit, so his scheme is interpretive. His central observation is the distinction: Israel is untouched by every plague "though dwelling in the same houses."[^source-philo-life-of-moses]

Augustine's summary in the *City of God*: Moses "overcame the *magi* of Pharaoh," and "ten memorable plagues were brought by Him upon them." He adds that Pharaoh is "the name of all the kings of Egypt," and that 144 years passed from Joseph's death to the Exodus — his own chronology, not the Hebrew text's.[^source-augustine-city-of-god]

### Ipuwer and the Merneptah Stele
The only Egyptian text regularly adduced for the plagues is the *Admonitions of Ipuwer*, a New Kingdom copy of a Middle Kingdom composition, translated by Gardiner in 1909. The verbal parallels are real: "Plague is throughout the land. Blood is everywhere"; "the river is blood, yet men drink of it"; grain perished on every side. But the differences are decisive: a lament over social inversion — the poor enriched, slaves taking their masters' goods, trade ceasing, the king absent — with no Moses, no Pharaoh confronted, no Israel, no tenfold sequence, no divine agent, and no deliverance. The mainstream position, stated by Lichtheim, is that the *Admonitions* is a literary lament on order versus chaos. Whether it corroborates the plagues is debated at best, and the parallels must never be presented as proof.[^source-ipuwer-papyrus]

No Egyptian record describes the plagues; royal inscriptions are triumphal and do not record defeats. The one Egyptian inscription bearing on the Exodus is the Merneptah Stele, of about 1209 BCE, recording "Israel is laid waste, its seed is not" — the earliest extrabiblical reference to Israel, a people, not a city or land. Its significance is chronological, not corroborative: Israel already existed as a people in Canaan by about 1209 BCE, a terminus ante quem for the Exodus on either dating, fixing nothing more precise.[^source-bible-odyssey-plagues][^source-merneptah-stele]

### When? Two datings, no decision

The narrative names no Pharaoh and gives no date. Two datings dominate, presented without deciding between them. The early date, around 1446 BCE, is computed from 1 Kings 6:1: the Exodus occurred 480 years before Solomon's fourth year — the figure is in the text; the resulting date is debated. The late date, in the thirteenth century under Ramesses II, rests principally on Exodus 1:11, the store-city "Raamses," a name fitting the capital Pi-Ramesses; it is a live position, not established by the text, and the name could be anachronistic. The court's location is likewise debated: Rameses, Memphis, or Tanis "in the field of Zoan." Ussher's chronology, placing the ten plagues within a single month, is debated — Clarke called most of its dates "destitute of proof."[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bible-odyssey-plagues][^source-jfb-commentary][^source-clarke-commentary]

## Why This Context Matters

These seven chapters are the long contest between Pharaoh and the LORD, and they carry the book from a demand refused to a people on the edge of freedom. The demand is the same every time — let my people go, that they may serve me — and Pharaoh's answers move from open refusal, to promises made and broken when the pressure lifts, to bargaining over who may go and what they may take. Each plague is heavier than the one before it, and each time the purpose is stated plainly: the wonders are multiplied so that the Egyptians shall know that he is the LORD, and so that Israel's children may tell their sons and their sons' sons what was done in Egypt.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Two threads run through the whole passage. One is the hardening of Pharaoh's heart, which the narrative records plague after plague, sometimes as Pharaoh's own act and sometimes as the LORD's — and which later Scripture turns into a warning: "harden not your heart," says the Psalm, and the letter to the Hebrews repeats it to the church. The other is the division the LORD puts between Egypt and Israel: Goshen is spared the flies and the hail, not one of Israel's cattle dies in the murrain, and Israel has light in their dwellings while Egypt sits in darkness. By the end of the passage the contest has reached its limit. Pharaoh has told Moses never to see his face again, Moses has announced one plague more, and the LORD has said that after it Pharaoh will thrust the people out altogether. The deliverance is still one night away.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-bible-hub-interlinear]
