## Overview

Saul's kingship was renewed at Gilgal, and at Gilgal he lost the kingdom. Samuel had told him to wait seven days for him to come and offer the burnt-offerings; Saul waited, the people scattered, the Philistines gathered at Michmash, and he forced himself and offered the sacrifice [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel came as he finished and said the kingdom would not continue: the Lord had sought a man after His own heart [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The war went on without him. Jonathan, his son, crossed to the Philistine garrison with only his armour-bearer, climbed the crag on hands and feet, and killed about twenty men — and a trembling came upon the camp, and the earth quaked, and the Philistines broke [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul's rash oath nearly undid the victory: no man was to eat until evening, and Jonathan, who had not heard it, ate honey in the forest and said, "My father hath troubled the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The lot fell on Jonathan, Saul swore he must die, and the people rescued him — not one hair of his head would fall [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then came the second test. Samuel gave Saul the standing command against Amalek: utterly destroy all that they have, and spare none [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul spared Agag the king and the best of the sheep and oxen, and said the people kept them to sacrifice to the Lord [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel answered with the sentence that has outlived the kingdom itself: "to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The robe tore as Samuel turned to go, and the kingdom was rent from Saul and given to a neighbour better than he [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel hewed Agag in pieces before the Lord in Gilgal, went to Ramah, and came no more to see Saul until the day of his death [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The reign that began with an anointing at dawn and a lot at Mizpah now stands under sentence, and the king who was made by the Lord's choice must live with the word that his kingdom will pass to another.

## Ancient World

### The first test: waiting at Gilgal

Samuel had told Saul to go down to Gilgal and wait seven days for him to come and offer burnt-offerings and tell him what to do [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul waited, and Samuel did not come, and the people scattered, and the Philistines were gathered at Michmash [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. So Saul forced himself and offered the burnt-offering, and as soon as he finished, Samuel came [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The offence has been read in two ways — as a king taking on priestly duty, and as disobedience to the specific command to wait — and the text itself places the emphasis on the broken command: "thou hast not kept the commandment of the LORD thy God, which He commanded thee" [^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. The sentence was dynastic, not personal: "now would the LORD have established thy kingdom upon Israel for ever. But now thy kingdom shall not continue; the LORD hath sought Him a man after His own heart" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The first judgment concerned Saul's dynasty; the second, at Amalek, concerned Saul himself — and yet he remained on the throne for years afterwards [^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. The reign's own numbers are a problem the text does not solve: the Hebrew lacks the age at which Saul became king, and the two years it gives for his reign cannot contain the campaigns that follow [^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. No number is supplied here, and none is invented.

### Michmash: the pass and the crags

Michmash, also spelled Michmas, is the modern village of Mukhmas, about seven miles northeast of Jerusalem, in the hill country of Benjamin [^source-biblicaltraining-micmash]. The Philistine garrison held the pass — the Wadi es-Suweinit, a deep gorge running east from Michmash toward the Jordan — and the Israelite force under Saul held Geba on the south side [^source-biblicaltraining-micmash]. Between the two, at the pass itself, stood two sharp rocks: Bozez on the north, toward Michmash, and Seneh on the south, toward Geba [^source-biblicaltraining-micmash]. The identification of Michmash rests on the survival of the name and on the topography — the gorge, the pass, the crags — rather than on excavation: Mukhmas has not been extensively dug [^source-biblicaltraining-micmash]. The Philistines sent out three raiding companies from Michmash: one toward Ophrah to the north, one toward Beth-horon to the west, and one toward the Valley of Zeboim to the east [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The same pass appears later in Isaiah's description of the Assyrian advance on Jerusalem, which names Michmash among the towns the invader passes [^source-biblicaltraining-micmash]. The gorge was the route an army took from the north to Jerusalem, in Saul's day and centuries later.

### "No smith in all the land"

"Now there was no smith found throughout all the land of Israel; for the Philistines said: Lest the Hebrews make them swords or spears" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. So every man went down to the Philistines to sharpen his agricultural tools — share, coulter, axe, and mattock — and the price was a *pim*, a weight of about two-thirds of a shekel, confirmed by inscribed stone weights found in the region [^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. On the day of battle there was neither sword nor spear in the hand of any of the people with Saul and Jonathan, but with Saul and Jonathan there was found [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The text presents the shortage as the Philistines' deliberate policy: an embargo on metalworking that kept Israel disarmed. The archaeology must not be overstated. Recent work on Philistine industry notes that there is no special evidence for metallurgical or iron technological innovations at Philistia, in workshops, installations, or objects, and that some scholars' links between iron metallurgy and the Philistines rest on this very passage [^source-ben-shlomo-industry-cult-philistia]. Iron objects have been found at Ekron, but they do not show a Philistine monopoly on iron [^source-ben-shlomo-industry-cult-philistia]. The "no smith" passage is therefore kept as the text's own explanation of Israel's disarmament, not as an archaeologically established fact.

### Jonathan's exploit and the rash oath

Jonathan went over to the Philistine garrison with only his armour-bearer, without telling his father, and proposed a sign: if the Philistines said "come up," that was the sign the Lord had delivered them into Israel's hand [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He climbed the crag on hands and feet, and he and his armour-bearer killed about twenty men within half a furrow's length; then a trembling came upon the camp, the field, and all the people, and the earth quaked [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The rout that followed drew in the Hebrews who had been with the Philistines and the Israelites who had hidden in the hill-country of Ephraim, and the Lord saved Israel that day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Saul made the oath that nearly undid the victory: "Cursed be the man that eateth any food until evening, and I be avenged on mine enemies" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The men came into a forest and there was honey upon the ground, but none dared eat for fear of the oath. Jonathan, who had not heard it, dipped his rod in the honeycomb and ate, and his eyes were enlightened, and he said, "My father hath troubled the land" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The oath cost the army its strength, and the slaughter was less than it might have been. When the lot fell on Jonathan, Saul swore he must die — and the people rescued him: "there shall not one hair of his head fall to the ground; for he hath wrought with God this day" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The rescue is the people's first act of judgment on their king, and it is in Jonathan's favour.

### The Amalek campaign

Samuel's commission was explicit: "go and smite Amalek, and utterly destroy all that they have, and spare them not," because of what Amalek did to Israel on the way up from Egypt [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The command reached back to the Amalekite attack at Rephidim and the standing order to blot out the remembrance of Amalek [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul mustered two hundred thousand footmen and ten thousand men of Judah at Telaim, warned the Kenites to depart — for they had shown kindness to Israel when they came up out of Egypt — and struck the Amalekites from Havilah to Shur [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He took Agag the king alive and spared him and the best of the sheep and oxen and fatlings and lambs, destroying only what was vile and refuse [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. When Samuel confronted him, Saul said the people had spared the best to sacrifice to the Lord [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel's answer is the chapter's centre: "Hath the LORD as great delight in burnt-offerings and sacrifices, as in hearkening to the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The sentence followed: "Because thou hast rejected the word of the LORD, He hath also rejected thee from being king" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The robe tore as Samuel turned to go, and the tearing became the sign: the kingdom was rent from Saul and given to a neighbour better than he [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel hewed Agag in pieces before the Lord in Gilgal, went to Ramah, and came no more to see Saul until the day of his death [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Amalekites were still present after Saul — David would fight them at Ziklag — so the campaign's incompleteness is not only a moral fact but a historical one, visible in the story that follows [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text].

## Why This Context Matters

Two tests, one pattern: a command, a partial obedience, a confrontation, and a sentence. At Gilgal the command was to wait, and Saul kept back the timing; at Amalek the command was to destroy utterly, and Saul kept back the king and the best of the spoil. Knowing the pass at Michmash — the gorge, the two crags, the army with no swords in its hands — keeps the reader from treating the campaign as an abstraction: Jonathan's climb was a real climb up a real rock, and the victory was won by a man with an armour-bearer against a garrison. And knowing that the "no smith" passage is the text's own explanation, not an archaeologically proven embargo, keeps the reader honest about what the evidence shows and what it does not. The context matters most at the sentence itself. "To obey is better than sacrifice" is not a proverb about ritual; it is a king being told that keeping back the best of the spoil and calling it worship is rebellion, and that rebellion has cost him the kingdom. The torn robe is the sign: what was kept back is what was lost.
