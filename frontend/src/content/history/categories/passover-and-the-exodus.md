## Overview

The Passover and the Exodus is the founding deliverance of Israel, told in Exodus 12–15. In Egypt, in the first month of the year, the LORD institutes the Passover: every household takes a lamb on the tenth day, keeps it until the fourteenth, and kills it in the evening. The blood is struck on the doorposts, and the flesh is eaten that night, roast with fire, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, in haste, with loins girded, shoes on, and staff in hand. That night the LORD passes through Egypt and smites all the firstborn, man and beast, and executes judgment against all the gods of Egypt — but where he sees the blood, he passes over the house. The day is made a memorial, kept as a feast throughout the generations, with seven days of unleavened bread.

At midnight the firstborn of Egypt die, from Pharaoh's throne to the captive in the dungeon, and there is a great cry in Egypt. Pharaoh calls for Moses and Aaron by night and sends Israel out in haste. The people take their dough before it is leavened, receive silver and gold and raiment from the Egyptians, and journey from Rameses to Succoth — about six hundred thousand men on foot, beside children, with a mixed multitude and very much cattle — after a sojourning of four hundred and thirty years. The ordinance follows: no stranger eats of the Passover, no bone of the lamb is broken, and one law holds for the homeborn and the stranger.

Then the LORD says, "Sanctify unto me all the firstborn... it is mine." The people are to remember the day they came out, to keep the feast of unleavened bread in the month Abib, and to redeem every firstborn son, telling their children why. God does not lead them by the way of the Philistines, though it is near, lest they see war and return — he leads them about, through the wilderness of the Red Sea. Moses carries the bones of Joseph, as Joseph swore, and the LORD goes before them in a pillar of cloud by day and of fire by night.

At the sea the LORD has the people encamp before Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, over against Baal-zephon, so that Pharaoh will follow after them. Pharaoh takes six hundred chosen chariots and pursues, overtaking them by the sea. Israel is sore afraid, but Moses says, "Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD." The pillar moves behind the camp, standing between the Egyptians and Israel. Moses stretches out his hand, the LORD drives the sea back by a strong wind all that night, and Israel crosses on dry ground, the waters a wall on either side. The Egyptians follow, and the waters return and cover them all. Israel sees the Egyptians dead upon the sea shore, fears the LORD, and believes. Then Moses and Israel sing, and Miriam the prophetess takes a timbrel and leads the women in song.

Two things are openly unresolved. The date of the Exodus has no independently established answer: the early date of about 1446 BCE and the late date of about 1260 BCE are both argued, and neither is established. And the crossing's location is unknown: the Hebrew calls the water the sea of reeds, the ancient versions call it the Red Sea, and scholars propose several places — none of them established.

## Ancient World


### The Passover meal

The Passover begins as a household meal eaten in haste, and becomes Israel's yearly memorial. The lamb is a male of the first year, without blemish, taken on the tenth day and kept until the fourteenth, when the whole assembly kills it between the evenings. Its blood, struck on the doorposts and lintel with hyssop, marks the houses to be passed over; its flesh is eaten roast with fire, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. The unleavened bread carries the haste: the dough is taken before it is leavened, because the people are thrust out and cannot tarry.

Passover and Unleavened Bread began as two distinct observances, which the narrative joins: the early laws name only Unleavened Bread, the later calendars ordain both.

The name is explained in the text by "pass over": "when I see the blood, I will pass over you." Calvin reads it the same way — a passing-over, not of the people, but of God himself. A scholarly proposal reads the word as "protect", resting on an Assyrian parallel where a similar word means to soothe or placate a deity. The text's own etymology is "pass over"; the "protect" reading is a proposal, not settled. The theology of memory is explicit — "this day shall be unto you for a memorial" — and the yearly meal lets each generation re-enact the night.

### The night of departure

The tenth plague falls at midnight: all the firstborn of Egypt die, and there is a great cry. The text distinguishes the LORD, who passes through and passes over, from "the destroyer," kept from the marked houses. Whether the destroyer is an angel, a personification of the plague, or the LORD's own action under another name is not stated.

Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron by night, and the Egyptians give the departing people silver, gold, and raiment — gifts, Josephus notes, not loans.

The text counts the departure: about six hundred thousand men on foot, beside children, with a mixed multitude and very much cattle. A total of about two million souls is an inference, not the text's number, and whether the six hundred thousand is literal or schematic is debated. The sojourning is four hundred and thirty years, but the traditions differ: the Hebrew text counts the sojourning in Egypt, while the Greek version adds "and in the land of Chanaan," counting from Abraham's entry into Canaan.

### The firstborn, the pillar, and the road not taken

The consecration of the firstborn answers the tenth plague: because the LORD slew the firstborn of Egypt and spared Israel's, every firstborn belongs to him. The firstling of an ass is redeemed with a lamb, or its neck is broken; every firstborn son is redeemed. When the son asks, "What is this?", the answer is the story: "By strength of hand the LORD brought us out from Egypt." The sign on the hand and the memorial between the eyes is the origin of the later practice of phylacteries.

God leads the people by the long way round: the road through Philistine country is refused, lest the people see war and return to Egypt. Philo adds a second reason — that they might also be tested. Instead they go about, through the wilderness of the Red Sea. Moses carries the bones of Joseph, and the LORD goes before them by day in a pillar of cloud and by night in a pillar of fire. Philo reads the pillar as one of the ministers of the mighty King; the commentary tradition identifies the angel of God with the pillar itself. The text presents the two moving together, and the identifications are interpretations.

### The crossing of the sea

The people turn south and encamp before Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, so that Pharaoh will think them entangled and follow. Pharaoh takes six hundred chosen chariots. Josephus enlarges the force to fifty thousand horsemen and two hundred thousand footmen, but the text's figure is the six hundred chosen chariots.

The wind is the stated means, and the traditions disagree: the Hebrew text says a strong east wind, the Greek version a strong south wind. An east wind suits a north–south body of water, a south wind an east–west one. Philo follows the Greek south wind, and adds a north wind to bring the water back.

Where did this happen? The Hebrew calls the water the sea of reeds. The Greek version renders it the Red Sea, and the Latin and English versions follow; no convincing explanation of why the Greek chose "red" is established. The same phrase is used for the Gulf of Aqaba. Six locations have been proposed — the Gulf of Suez, Lake Ballah, Lake Timsah and the northern Bitter Lakes, the southern Bitter Lakes, Lake Menzaleh, and Lake Sirbonis — and none of them is established. Pi-hahiroth, Migdol, and Baal-zephon are likewise unidentified, and the landscape has changed since then. No coordinates are given here, because none are known.

### The Song of the Sea

Then Moses and the children of Israel sing. The Song is the first great song in the Bible: "I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea." It interprets: the LORD is a man of war; "Who is like unto thee, O LORD, among the gods?"; and the deliverance is a journey to a sanctuary — "Thou shalt bring them in, and plant them in the mountain of thine inheritance... The LORD shall reign for ever and ever."

The Song's language is considerably older than the surrounding prose: its archaic forms resemble the Canaanite poetry of Ugarit, and Cross and Freedman judged the poem truly archaic, not merely archaizing. But its date is contested: Cross and Freedman dated it to the twelfth or eleventh century BCE, while other scholars argue for a post-exilic date, around 450 BCE. The Song is therefore presented here as ancient poetry, its date debated, not settled.

Miriam takes a timbrel, and all the women go out after her with timbrels and dances; her song repeats the opening verse, and its relationship to the longer song is debated. Josephus reports that Moses composed the song in hexameter verse — his own description, not a modern judgment.

### Ancient witnesses

Josephus dates the sacrifice to the tenth of Xanthicus against the fourteenth — Xanthicus the Macedonian name for Nisan — and the departure to the fifteenth; the leaders' ages are Moses eighty and Aaron eighty-three. He also issues his famous disclaimer: he has delivered the history as he found it in the sacred books — offering, as a parallel, the sea that parted for Alexander at Pamphylia, an apologetic device, not an independent attestation.

Philo reads the calendar philosophically: the first month is reckoned from the vernal equinox, and Nisan is first in the festival calendar and seventh in the civil calendar.

Jubilees retells the story with a hidden agent of its own: Mastema, the adversary, urges the pursuit and lets loose his powers to slay the firstborn. Jubilees counts a million Egyptians destroyed at the sea, and requires the Passover to be eaten only before the sanctuary, by males of twenty and over — a requirement of the later law, not of the Egyptian night. All of this is Jubilees' own expansion, Second Temple reception, not Exodus.

Augustine reads the passage typologically: he counts a hundred and forty-four years from Joseph's death to the Exodus, and places the giving of the law on the fiftieth day after the Passover lamb. Moses, he notes, did not lead the people into the land — Joshua did.

### The date of the Exodus

No independently established date exists for the Exodus. The early date, about 1446 BCE, counts four hundred and eighty years back from the temple's foundation, and takes Amenhotep II as the pharaoh of the Exodus. The late date, about 1260 BCE, takes Ramesses II, whose name the store-cities Pithom and Raamses bear, and reads the four hundred and eighty years as schematic — twelve generations of forty years. The late date is the position Kitchen defends.

Both models must satisfy one fixed point: the Merneptah Stele. Set up in the fifth year of Pharaoh Merneptah, about 1208 BCE, and found by Petrie at Thebes in 1896, it names Israel in its twenty-seventh line with a determinative denoting a people: "Israel is laid waste, his seed is not." It is the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible: a people called Israel existed in Canaan by about 1208 BCE, so the Exodus, if it happened, happened before then. The early date satisfies this easily; the late date narrowly. But the stele cannot confirm the Exodus itself: it records no departure, no plagues, and no drowning — only a claimed victory over Israel in Canaan.

## Why This Context Matters

The Passover and the Exodus is the night Israel becomes a people. The lamb, the blood, and the unleavened bread are not ornaments on the story; they are how the story is remembered. The Passover is instituted before the deliverance happens, so that the meal itself becomes the memorial — and the firstborn law, the pillar, and the Song all carry the same night forward. To read Exodus 12–15 without this context is to miss why the ritual and the narrative cannot be separated: the ritual exists to remember the narrative, and the song exists to celebrate it.

The context also teaches restraint. The date is debated, the crossing's place is unknown, the numbers are stated but their sense is argued, and the Song's age is contested. Knowing what is not established is part of understanding the passage — it keeps the deliverance itself, which the text states plainly, from being confused with the reconstructions built around it. The sea parts, the army drowns, and Israel sings: that is the text. Where it happened, and in which century, the evidence does not yet say.
