## Overview

Saul is dead on Mount Gilboa, and Israel has two kings. The book of 2 Samuel opens the day after the disaster: David, two days back in Ziklag from fighting the Amalekites, hears the news from a messenger with torn clothes and dust on his head [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The man says he found the wounded Saul on Gilboa and killed him at the king's own request, and he brings the crown and the bracelet to prove it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David mourns, weeps, and fasts until evening — and then has the man executed for striking the LORD's anointed, judging him on his own testimony [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Then he sings. His lament for Saul and Jonathan, the Song of the Bow, orders the people of Judah to learn it by heart: "How are the mighty fallen!" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-book-of-jashar]. The lament closes the Saul era, but the throne is not settled. David goes up to Hebron, where the men of Judah anoint him king over their tribe, while Saul's commander Abner sets up Saul's surviving son Ish-bosheth at Mahanaim, east of the Jordan, as king over all the rest of Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. The divided kingdom goes to war at once. At the pool of Gibeon twelve young men from each side kill each other in a single moment, and the day ends in a sore battle that Abner loses [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. In the retreat Abner kills Joab's brother Asahel, who would not stop pursuing him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The war drags on for years, David growing stronger and the house of Saul weaker, until Abner himself quarrels with Ish-bosheth over the king's concubine Rizpah and changes sides [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. David demands the return of his wife Michal, Abner wins over the elders of Israel, and the kingdom is almost whole again — until Joab lures Abner aside in the gate at Hebron and murders him in revenge for Asahel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David mourns Abner in public, curses Joab's house, and confesses that the sons of Zeruiah are too hard for him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Without Abner, Ish-bosheth's government collapses. Two of his own captains murder him in his bed at noon and carry his head to David, expecting a reward [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David executes them too, citing the Amalekite's fate in chapter 1, and buries Ish-bosheth's head in Abner's grave [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The way to the throne is now open, and the elders of all Israel are on their way to Hebron.

## Ancient World

### Two kings, two capitals

When a king died in the ancient Near East, his son was normally expected to follow him — kingship was dynastic and hereditary in principle, though not always through the first-born son [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. Israel had done it differently at the start: Saul was anointed privately by the prophet Samuel, chosen by lot in a public gathering, and confirmed by his victory over the Ammonites — three different accounts of one appointment [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. The civil war after Gilboa is a contest between the two principles. Ish-bosheth, Saul's surviving son, claims the throne by descent, and it is Abner, Saul's commander, who makes him king; David claims it by anointing and by the people's choice, and it is the men of Judah who anoint him at Hebron [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. The geography is the politics. Hebron sits in the hill country of Judah, David's tribal home; Mahanaim sits east of the Jordan, in Gilead, the territory Abner could still hold after the Philistine victory [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. David's kingdom is Judah in the south; Ish-bosheth's is Gilead, the Ashurites, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, and all Israel — everything else [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. And the numbers tell their own story: Ish-bosheth reigns two years, David seven years and six months in Hebron, and the text never explains the gap [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. Perhaps Ish-bosheth's reign began some time after Saul's death, once Abner had regained the north from the Philistines; perhaps the two years cover only part of the period [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. Either way, the real power in the northern kingdom is not the king but the commander: Abner shows himself strong in the house of Saul, and when Abner leaves, the house collapses in a single verse — "his hands were feeble, and all Israel was troubled" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### The pool of Gibeon

The war begins as a contest and ends as a slaughter. The two armies meet at the pool of Gibeon and sit on opposite sides of the water, and Abner proposes that the young men "play before us" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Twelve men of Benjamin and twelve of David's servants rise, each seizes his opponent by the head, each thrusts his sword into his opponent's side, and all twenty-four fall dead together [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The place is named for it — Helkath-hazzurim — though the name itself is not translated in the text, and English versions render it differently, as the Field of Swords, the Field of Flints, or the field of sword-edges [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What began as a display becomes a sore battle, and Abner is beaten. The day's most remembered moment comes in the retreat. Asahel, the youngest of David's three nephews, is light of foot as a wild roe, and he pursues Abner without turning aside to the right or the left [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Twice Abner warns him off and offers him a young man's armour instead; twice Asahel refuses [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Abner strikes him with the hinder end of his spear, under the fifth rib — the same stroke, in the same words, that will kill Abner himself and then Ish-bosheth [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The men who come to the place where Asahel fell simply stand still. At sunset Abner calls across to Joab, "Shall the sword devour for ever? Knowest thou not that it will be bitterness in the latter end?" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Joab blows the trumpet, and the two armies walk away — Abner's men marching all night through the Arabah and across the Jordan to Mahanaim, Joab's men carrying Asahel's body to his father's grave at Bethlehem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The losses are counted like a ledger: nineteen men and Asahel on David's side, three hundred and sixty on Abner's [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### The Song of the Bow

David's lament is the book's opening note, and it is also one of the oldest poems in the Bible. He orders it taught to the people of Judah, and the text says it was written in the Book of Jashar, a lost collection of national songs that the Old Testament cites only twice — here, and at Joshua 10:13, for the poem about the sun standing still [^source-biblicaltraining-book-of-jashar]. Both quotations are poems about Israel's battles and leaders, so the book was most likely an anthology of songs gathered over time, and its name means "book of the upright one" [^source-biblicaltraining-book-of-jashar]. The lament itself is built on a refrain. "How are the mighty fallen!" sounds three times — at the opening, at the turn to Jonathan, and at the close — and the whole poem hangs from it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-book-of-jashar]. David forbids the news in Gath and Ashkelon, the Philistine cities, lest their daughters rejoice; he curses the mountains of Gilboa, asking that no dew or rain fall on them, because there the shield of the mighty was cast away, the shield of Saul, not anointed with oil [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The curse is poetry, not weather: it asks the battlefield to share in the mourning [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. Then the praise, in the paired lines Hebrew poetry loves best: Saul and Jonathan were lovely and pleasant in life and undivided in death, swifter than eagles, stronger than lions; Saul clothed the daughters of Israel in scarlet and gold [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The poem ends not with the king but with the friend: "I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan; very pleasant hast thou been unto me; wonderful was thy love to me, passing the love of women" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Jonathan was the heir who gave up his own claim to the throne and handed David the robe, the sword, and the bow that marked his rank [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. The lament is David's answer to that gift: a public song, learned by a whole tribe, that honours the house he is replacing.

### Blood for blood

Three murders drive these chapters, and each one is answered as blood for blood. Abner kills Asahel in battle, after warning him twice; Joab kills Abner in the gate at Hebron, luring him aside "to speak with him quietly," for the blood of Asahel his brother [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-david]. The narrator states the motive twice, and David's own verdict is the lament, "Died Abner as a fool dieth?" — Abner died like a man murdered by treachery, his hands unbound and his feet unfettered, fallen before the children of iniquity [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David declares himself guiltless, curses Joab's house, orders the whole people into sackcloth, follows the bier, and fasts till sundown; and all Israel understands that it was not of the king to slay Abner [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. But the confession that follows is the honest middle of the story: "I am this day weak, though anointed king; and these men the sons of Zeruiah be too hard for me" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David keeps the murderer as his commander. The same pattern closes the book's first movement. Rechab and Baanah murder Ish-bosheth in his bed at noon, behead him, and carry his head through the night to Hebron, claiming the LORD has avenged David [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David answers with the Amalekite's fate: the man who brought news of Saul's death expecting a reward was slain in Ziklag, and how much more these wicked men who slew a righteous person in his own house on his bed [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He has them slain, their hands and feet cut off and hanged over the pool in Hebron, and buries Ish-bosheth's head in Abner's grave [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The king who would not kill Saul, and would not let Joab's murder stand, will not take the throne from the hands of assassins either. That is the rule these four chapters establish, twice over: David does not seize the kingdom by blood, and he will not receive it by blood.

## Why This Context Matters

These four chapters are about how a king gets his throne, and the answer they give is: not by murder. David is anointed, chosen, and promised the kingdom, yet he spends seven years watching other men kill for it — Abner killing Asahel, Joab killing Abner, Rechab and Baanah killing Ish-bosheth — and refusing to profit from any of it. Knowing the world of the civil war keeps the reader from turning David's rise into a simple triumph. The divided kingdom was real: two capitals, two armies, a pool at Gibeon where twenty-four young men died in a single moment, and a king who confessed that his own commanders were too hard for him. The lament that opens the book sets the tone for everything after it. David honours the king he replaces and the friend whose throne he inherits, and he does it in a song the whole tribe was ordered to learn. The throne David finally receives at Hebron is one he waited for, mourned for, and refused to take by blood — and that refusal is the measure of the king the rest of the book will test.
