## Overview

Deuteronomy 16:18–20:20 is the constitutional core of Deuteronomy: who may judge, who may rule, who may minister, and who may speak for God — and how those offices protect life and truth and govern war. It opens with judges and officers appointed "in all thy gates… throughout thy tribes," to judge "with just judgment," bound by three prohibitions: do not wrest judgment, do not respect persons, do not take a gift, "for a gift doth blind the eyes of the wise, and pervert the words of the righteous." Then the positive command — "that which is altogether just shalt thou follow, that thou mayest live, and inherit the land" — tying justice to life and to the land itself.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The ban on grove and pillar beside the altar (16:21–22) bridges worship to justice.

The judiciary is completed by a central tribunal. A matter "too hard" for local judgment goes up to the place the LORD shall choose, to "the priests the Levites, and unto the judge that shall be in those days," and their sentence is binding. The man who acts presumptuously and will not hearken to priest or judge "shall die." Idolatry likewise requires a report, a hearing, and diligent inquiry, with punishment only "if it be true, and the thing certain"; no one dies on one witness, and the witnesses' hands are first upon the condemned.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Then the three offices of 17:14–18:22. The king law answers the people's future request — "I will set a king over me, like as all the nations that are about me" — a provision, not a command. The king must be God-chosen, "one from among thy brethren"; must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold, nor return the people to Egypt; and must write "a copy of this law in a book" and read it all his life, "that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The priests and Levites "shall have no part nor inheritance with Israel… the LORD is their inheritance," kept from the offerings, with equal portions for the visiting Levite. The prophet answers the forbidden practices of 18:9–14: where the nations consult diviners, Israel is given a prophet like Moses — God's words in his mouth, death for the presumptuous prophet, fulfilment as the test of a word.

Chapters 19 and 20 apply the offices. The cities of refuge protect the unintentional slayer from the avenger of blood — the axe-head slipping from the helve is the example — while the deliberate murderer is delivered to the avenger. The landmark stands, one witness is insufficient, and the false witness receives what he intended for his brother: "life shall go for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth." In war the priest addresses the army, the officers exempt the man with unfinished business or a fearful heart, and only then are captains appointed. A distant city is first offered peace; a city of the inheritance is put under the ban, "thou shalt save alive nothing that breatheth." In a long siege the fruit trees are spared, "for the tree of the field is man's life."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Two cautions govern this page. First, the herem of Deuteronomy 20:16–18 is not softened: rhetoric, lethal semantics, historicity, and ethical appraisal are separate analyses, and the commands are not made harmless by asserting hyperbole. Second, prescribed law and attested practice diverge — Solomon multiplies the horses and wives the king law forbids — and the divergence is the finding. No date is assigned to these laws: their composition is debated, and the category is undated.

## Ancient World

### The gate as courtroom

The gate was the place of public resort among the Israelites and other Eastern peoples, "where business was transacted and causes decided," as Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown put it on Deuteronomy 16:18 — the same setting as Ruth 4 and 2 Samuel 15.[^source-jfb-commentary] "Judges in all thy gates" therefore means a decentralised local judiciary, with the central tribunal of 17:8–13 for cases the local gate cannot decide. The officers (*šōṭĕrîm*) were the heralds or bailiffs who executed the judges' sentences, and the same officers reappear in Deuteronomy 20:5–9.[^source-jfb-commentary] Gate complexes with chambers and benches, such as those at Gezer, Hazor, Megiddo, and Tel Dan, are the physical setting for the "judges in the gates" — architectural context, not evidence for the law.

### Bribery and partiality

The three prohibitions of 16:19 recur across the law and the prophets: Exodus 23:8 has the bribery warning near-verbatim with Deuteronomy 16:19; Leviticus 19:15 forbids unrighteousness in judgment; Deuteronomy 1:17 charges the judges to hear "the small as well as the great." The law names the temptation every judiciary faces. Hammurabi's Code answers the same temptation with a penalty: its fifth section expels a judge who reverses his sealed verdict and makes him pay twelvefold. But the Deuteronomic remedy is a prohibition on the judge, not a penalty for reversal, grounded in God: "the judgment is God's" (Deut 1:17).[^source-hammurabi-code][^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### Kingship in the ancient Near East

In the ancient Near East the king was normally supreme judge, lawgiver, military commander, and cult patron — often its priest. The Deuteronomic king inverts that figure: he copies the law instead of making it; the priests and the judge of 17:9 judge; the Levites command the cult; in war the priest addresses the army (20:2–4) before captains are appointed (20:5–9). Kingship was normally dynastic and hereditary in principle — yet in ancient Israel it was not originally hereditary: Saul was anointed by the prophet, selected by lot, and confirmed by victory, and hereditary kingship began after David's death. Deuteronomy 17:20's "he, and his children" is the law's own concession to heredity, conditional on obedience.[^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession][^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Bernard Levinson argues that Deuteronomy redefines each branch of public office, subordinating each to cultic centralisation and to the Torah's textual authority, so that the king's tools of power are neutralised. Joshua Berman notes that the king's limits are without parallel in the ancient Near East. Both are recorded as scholarly readings, not established fact: Levinson's 2001 article and Berman's *Created Equal* were not read directly here, only through second-hand summaries, so the claim rests on them at Probable, never Established.[^source-levinson-reconceptualization-kingship][^source-berman-created-equal] The directly read sources make the same point: Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown observe that the king "was to be absolutely independent of the people and had nothing but the divine law to restrain his passions," and that the law's breach was "exemplified in the history of Solomon and other princes."[^source-jfb-commentary]

### A landless tribe and its keep

The Levites' landlessness (18:1–2) is the economic corollary of centralisation: a tribe with no territory must live from the other tribes' offerings. The priest's due is specified in kind — shoulder, cheeks, maw; firstfruits of corn, wine, and oil; first of the fleece — and whether this is new or a repetition with more detail is uncertain: Leviticus 7:31–33 gives breast and shoulder; Deuteronomy 18:3 adds cheeks and maw.[^source-jfb-commentary] The visiting Levite's equal portions (18:6–8) presume rotation, and the law admits the Levite who comes "with all the desire of his mind" to full participation, "beside that which cometh of the sale of his patrimony."[^source-jfb-commentary] Paul's appeal in 1 Corinthians 9:13–14 reuses the same principle: those who minister about holy things live of the temple.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### Blood vengeance and the cities of refuge

*Goelism* — the nearest kinsman's duty to avenge a slaughtered relative — was the customary law of that age; Moses incorporated it in an improved form.[^source-jfb-commentary] The improvement is the point: the avenger's right is not abolished but channelled — the slayer may flee to a city, roads prepared and the land divided in three, the deliberate murderer expressly excluded. Deuteronomy 19 states the institution without two features of Numbers 35 — the congregation's trial and the term of exile until the high priest's death. The omission is a textual fact; its reason is debated. Three more cities are conditional on enlarged borders and full obedience, and Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown note the condition was never fulfilled.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] No site has been identified as a city of refuge by inscription or assemblage. Deuteronomy 19 itself names no city — the eastern three are named in Deuteronomy 4:41–43 as Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan — so nothing in this category's own text attaches to a site, and the western names are not asserted here.

### War, the ban, and the sparing of trees

Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown identify the priest who addresses the army (20:2–4) as one "appointed to this duty" by a special ceremony; Josephus places the high priest alongside the king in counsel. That is a commentary reconstruction — the text says only "the priest shall approach and speak."[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-josephus-antiquities] Distant cities are first offered peace; if taken, they yield women, little ones, cattle, and spoil. The cities of the inheritance are put under the ban — "thou shalt save alive nothing that breatheth" — the six nations named, "that they teach you not to do after all their abominations."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The ban (*ḥērem*, H2764) is "devotion, ban": a thing devoted to the LORD, hostile to theocracy, to be destroyed or set apart to sacred uses. The same word has a sanctuary side (Lev 27:28), but in Deuteronomy 20:16–17 it is the lethal one.[^source-bdb-lexicon] Four analyses must be kept separate: the rhetoric is totality language, as in Deuteronomy 7:2 and the conquest narratives; the lethal semantics are the ban as the lexicon defines it. The historicity is open: no direct archaeological evidence ties the ban to a datable destruction event, and the narratives complicate a simple reading — Rahab's family spared at Jericho, the Gibeonites surviving by treaty, the six nations persisting through Judges. The ethical appraisal is debated. The text's own rationale in 20:18 is prevention of idolatry; the page records text and difficulty without softening or endorsing either. The Mesha Stele's *ḥērem* language at Nebo is the closest epigraphic parallel, but it was not read directly here and is recorded as a follow-up, not a verified source. Even in a long siege fruit trees are spared, "for the tree of the field is man's life"; only non-fruit trees may be cut for bulwarks.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

### Words that carry the argument

One Hebrew word spans this category. *Mišpāṭ* (H4941) is judgment, justice, ordinance, and right: deciding a case ("just judgment," 16:18), the sentence itself (17:9), the "law of king" (1 Sam 8:9, 11; 10:25), and the priest's due (18:3). *Nābîʾ* (H5030) means "spokesman, speaker, prophet": the genuine prophet of the LORD, "of prophet like Moses, Deut 18:15, 18," and the false prophet of 18:20, 22.[^source-bdb-lexicon] And *mišneh hattôrâ* (17:18) means "a copy of this law," not a second law: the Septuagint's *to deuteronomion touto*, "this Second Law," is the origin of the book's Greek name, Deuteronomy. Whether "this law" is the king-law passage, the Deuteronomic code, or the whole Torah is debated.[^source-net-bible-notes-deuteronomy][^source-thetorah-second-torah]

Josephus, in *Antiquities* 4.8, is the earliest surviving systematisation of these laws. He gives seven judges per city with two Levite officers each, and a king who does nothing "without the high priest and the votes of the senators" — a senate the text does not name. Both are Josephus' elaborations, not Deuteronomy's text.[^source-josephus-antiquities] The Temple Scroll tightens the king law further, requiring a monogamous king who takes a wife from his father's house — evidence for how the law was read, not a source for the law itself.[^source-thetorah-second-torah]

### When were these laws written?

No year is given in Deuteronomy 16:18–20:20, and the composition date of Deuteronomy is debated: the traditional position dates the laws to Moses; the critical position dates the core to the late monarchy, commonly Josiah's reform (2 Kings 22–23, 622 BCE) or the exile. The king law is a crux for both: on the late dating, a critique of Solomon or the monarchy in general; on the early dating, a prophetic provision. The centralisation law of Deuteronomy 12 is the usual anchor for the late dating; the Arad and Tel Moza evidence does not settle a date. The text's own anchors — a settled land and a future request for a king, and possession of the land west of the Jordan — yield no year, and the category's date is unknown. The laws are presented as Moses' address in the fortieth year after the exodus, on the plains of Moab — the narrative setting, not an established historical date.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

## Why This Context Matters

This passage is a constitution, and its most striking feature is what it refuses to give the powerful. The king does not make the law; he copies it by hand and reads it daily so that his heart is not lifted up above his brethren. The judge does not take a gift, respect persons, or wrest judgment. The priest owns no land and lives from the offerings of others. The prophet is tested by whether the thing comes to pass, and dies if he presumes. Each office is checked by the written word and by the others, and the sequence matters: the judiciary comes first and the king last among the human offices, and the prophet is given in place of the diviners the nations consult.

That structure still frames how the rest of the passage is read. Justice begins locally, in the gates where people live, and only hard cases travel to the centre. Blood vengeance is not abolished but restrained by roads, cities, and elders. War is not waged without a priest's address, exemptions for unfinished lives, an offer of peace to distant cities, and a limit that spares even the fruit trees of a besieged town. And the hardest passage, the ban of Deuteronomy 20:16–18, is presented honestly rather than explained away: its rhetoric, its lethal meaning, its uncertain history, and its ethical difficulty are kept apart so that the reader can see each clearly. The narratives then show the law and the practice diverging — Solomon multiplying what the law forbids, the nations surviving what the law commands destroyed — and that divergence is itself part of the story this category tells.
