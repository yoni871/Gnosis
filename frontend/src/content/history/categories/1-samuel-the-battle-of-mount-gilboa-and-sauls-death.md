## Overview

The Philistines fight Israel, and the men of Israel flee and fall slain on Mount Gilboa [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. It is the battle the whole book has been moving toward: the Philistines came up to Jezreel, Saul camped on the ridge, and the LORD answered him neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets. On the field the archers find Saul and wound him sorely, and his three sons — Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua — are slain beside him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul asks his armour-bearer to thrust him through, lest the uncircumcised abuse him; the armour-bearer refuses, afraid, and Saul falls on his own sword, and the armour-bearer falls on his sword with him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. "So Saul died, and his three sons, and his armour-bearer, and all his men, that same day together." The towns across the valley and across the Jordan are forsaken, and the Philistines come and dwell in them [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. On the morrow the Philistines strip the slain, cut off Saul's head, and send the tidings to the house of their idols and among the people; his armour is put in the house of the Ashtoreths, and his body is fastened to the wall of Beth Shan [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan]. Then the men of Jabesh-gilead, the town Saul saved at the start of his reign, march all night to take the bodies down, burn them at Jabesh, bury the bones under the tamarisk, and fast seven days [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The Chronicler will add one more detail — that Saul's head was put in the temple of Dagon — and David, hearing the news at Ziklag, will answer with the lament of the bow [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. But inside this chapter there is no lament and no explanation, only the battle, the display, and the recovery. David is nowhere in the chapter. He is at Ziklag, recovering what the Amalekites took, while his king dies on Gilboa. The book of 1 Samuel ends not with an accession but with a burial and a fast.

## Ancient World

### The valley and the ridge

Mount Gilboa is a ridge at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley, known in Arabic as Jebel Fuquʿa; its summits rise somewhat over sixteen hundred feet [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The town of Jezreel stands on a spur at the ridge's north-west end, by the spring where Israel camped; the Hill of Moreh, Shunem, and Endor lie to the north, and Beth Shan to the east, guarding the Jordan crossings [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The valley of Jezreel — "God sows" — is the only natural east–west passage through the central hill country, the route linking the coastal road with the Jordan, Gilead, and Damascus [^source-biblicaltraining-valley-of-jezreel]. It is fertile, open ground, chariot country, where iron chariots gave the advantage, and where the Philistines' archers and chariots could be used at full effect [^source-biblicaltraining-valley-of-jezreel]. The valley had seen battle before: Deborah and Barak against Sisera, and Gideon against the Midianites at the spring of Harod, at the foot of this same ridge [^source-biblicaltraining-valley-of-jezreel]. The armies' positions in chapter 28 follow the valley's own logic: the Philistines at Shunem on the northern side, Saul on Gilboa, Israel camped by the spring in Jezreel [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa][^source-biblicaltraining-valley-of-jezreel]. The Israelites gave way before the archers and were cut down as they fell back onto the ridge.

### The Philistine army

The Philistines were organised around five cities — Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron — each ruled by a lord, a *seren*, and together the five could act as a council [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. Their army combined infantry, archers, and chariots, and stationed garrisons in occupied territory [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. At Gilboa the arm the text names is the archers: they hit Saul and wound him sorely before any sword reaches him, and the commentators attribute the Israelite collapse to the Philistines' superiority at a distance, destroying men before they came to close combat and throwing them into panic [^source-jfb-commentary]. Early Philistine weapons and tools were mostly bronze, with iron coming into common use gradually through the early Iron Age; Goliath's bronze armour and iron spear point fit that transitional period [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. The passage about the Philistines controlling metalworking describes political control of smiths by an occupying power, not a secret technology [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. The Philistines' pressure on the tribes forms the setting for the rise of the monarchy itself: Israel asked for a king who would "go out before us and fight our battles," and the LORD sent Saul to deliver his people from Philistine domination [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. At Gilboa, the king sent to deliver Israel from the Philistines is killed by them.

### The death of a king

Saul's death is told without euphemism and without moral comment in the chapter itself. His stated reason for asking his armour-bearer to kill him is the fear of abuse at enemy hands — "lest these uncircumcised come and thrust me through, and abuse me" — and the Philistines' later treatment of the bodies shows the fear was well placed [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The armour-bearer refuses, "for he was sore afraid," and then falls on his own sword when he sees Saul dead [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The text names no one: the tradition that the armour-bearer was Doeg comes from Jewish writers reported by the commentators, and it is recorded here as tradition, not as text [^source-jfb-commentary]. The summary sentence gathers the day into one line: "So Saul died, and his three sons, and his armour-bearer, and all his men, that same day together" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Chronicler retells the same deaths and adds an explanation — Saul died for his unfaithfulness, and for seeking counsel of a medium — but that verdict belongs to the Chronicler, not to 1 Samuel, and the two accounts are kept distinct here [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. The chapter itself fulfils two earlier words without quoting either: Samuel's verdict at Endor, "to-morrow shalt thou and thy sons be with me," and David's prediction that Saul would go down into battle and be swept away [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The reader is left to hear the fulfilment.

### The display

The Philistine treatment of the dead is a victory ritual, not a burial. On the morrow they come to strip the slain, cut off Saul's head, strip his armour, and send the tidings "to publish it in the house of their idols, and among the people" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The armour is put in the house of the Ashtoreths, and the body is fastened to the wall of Beth Shan [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Ashtoreth is the Canaanite goddess Astarte, and the Chronicler adds that Saul's head was put in the temple of Dagon — a detail 1 Samuel does not give, and one whose location the Bible does not state [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines][^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan]. The victory is reported to the gods first, in their houses, and then to the people; the Philistines reported victories to their idols [^source-biblicaltraining-philistines]. Beth Shan itself stands at the eastern end of the valley at the Jordan, at Tell el-Husn, about fourteen miles south of the Sea of Galilee [^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan]. It was an Egyptian garrison town in the Late Bronze Age, allotted to Manasseh, its Canaanites never driven out, and the narrative implies it was in Philistine hands or allied with them — though little distinctively Philistine material has been excavated there, and it may have been a Canaanite town cooperating with the Philistines [^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan]. Two early Iron Age temples excavated there were identified by the excavator as the temples of Ashtoreth and Dagon; some accept the identification, others note that the Bible does not place the Dagon temple at Beth Shan and that the deities cannot be identified with certainty [^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan]. The dispute is recorded here without resolution. What is not disputed is the meaning of the display: the king's body on the wall of a town at the Jordan crossing announced the victory to every traveller on the road.

### Jabesh-gilead

When the inhabitants of Jabesh-gilead hear what the Philistines have done to Saul, all the valiant men arise and go all night, take the bodies from the wall of Beth Shan, come to Jabesh, and burn them there [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Jabesh was an Israelite town in the hill country of Gilead, east of the Jordan, and its bond with Saul began at his reign's start: Nahash the Ammonite threatened to put out the right eye of every man in the town, and Saul mustered Israel, attacked in the last watch of the night, and delivered it [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The recovery is the repayment of that debt, and the sources present it as loyalty to the king who saved the town [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead][^source-jfb-commentary]. The journey was a feat: the valiant men marched through the night, crossed the Jordan valley, and took the bodies from the wall of a fortified enemy-held city — about ten miles from Jabesh, by a narrow upland passage west of the Jordan — and returned to their own side of the river in a single night [^source-jfb-commentary]. Where Jabesh stood is not settled: the name survives in Wadi Yabis, and the most widely favoured candidates are Tell Abu Kharaz and Tell el-Meqbereh, with Iron Age remains; no inscription names the town [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The burning is the chapter's hardest detail. Cremation was not normal Israelite practice, and the text gives no reason for it. The interpretations on record are that the mutilated and exposed bodies required it, that it prevented the men of Beth Shan from disinterring the remains for further insult, or that it followed the burning of spices at later royal funerals — but the plain reading is that the bodies themselves were burned [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead][^source-jfb-commentary]. No interpretation is adopted here as the reason. The bones are buried under the tamarisk at Jabesh, and the men fast seven days [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. David later moved the bones to the tomb of Kish at Zela, but that belongs to the next book [^source-biblicaltraining-beth-shan].

### The lament

David hears the news in Ziklag, and his lament over Saul and Jonathan is the book's true epitaph for its first king [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The "lament of the bow" is recorded in the Book of Jashar, and its refrain — "How are the mighty fallen!" — runs through it three times [^source-biblicaltraining-gilboa]. It curses the mountains of Gilboa, "let there be no dew nor rain upon you," because there the shield of the mighty was cast away — a poet's curse, not a claim about the ridge's climate [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. It forbids the telling of the news in Gath and Ashkelon, "lest the daughters of the Philistines rejoice" — the same cities whose temples received the tidings of the victory [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. And it remembers Jonathan: "wonderful was thy love to me, passing the love of women" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. It is quoted here as poetry, not as history. The book of 1 Samuel does not contain it. The book ends earlier, with the burial and the fast.

## Why This Context Matters

Chapter 31 is thirteen verses long, and it ends a book. Knowing the valley helps the reader see why the battle was lost where it was lost: open, chariot country, where archers could destroy men before they came to close combat, and a ridge the fleeing army could not hold. Knowing Beth Shan and Jabesh helps the reader feel the two movements of the chapter: the display, which turns a dead king into a message on a wall at the Jordan crossing, and the recovery, which turns a night march across the river into the book's last act of loyalty. And knowing the Chronicler's verdict — and keeping it distinct from Samuel's silence — keeps the reader from importing an explanation the chapter does not give. The chapter fulfils Samuel's word and David's prediction without announcing either, and it ends with a burial and a seven-day fast rather than an accession. That ending is the point: the first king of Israel dies on Gilboa, David is absent from the chapter entirely, and the book stops in mourning. The kingship that follows is a matter for the next book.
