## Overview



Josiah is eight years old when he begins to reign, and he reigns thirty-one years in Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He does right, walks in all the way of David his father, and turns not aside to the right hand or to the left [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the eighteenth year of his reign he orders the repair of the house of the LORD. The king sends Shaphan the scribe to Hilkiah the high priest to sum the silver brought into the house and deliver it to the overseers — carpenters, builders, and masons — and no reckoning is made with the workmen, "because they dealt faithfully" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And in the course of the repair Hilkiah says to Shaphan, "I have found the book of the law in the house of the LORD." [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text] Shaphan reads it, carries it to the king, and reads it before him — and the king, hearing the words of the book of the law, rends his clothes [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king sends five men to enquire of the LORD for him, for the people, and for all Judah, concerning the words of the book that is found [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. They go to Huldah the prophetess, who answers with two words in one mouth: the evil will come upon this place, all the words of the book, and the wrath "shall not be quenched" — but the king, whose heart was tender, "shalt be gathered into thy grave in peace; and thine eyes shall not see all the evil which I will bring upon this place" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The king gathers all the elders and all the people, small and great, reads the book of the covenant in their ears, and makes a covenant before the LORD to walk after him and keep his commandments with all heart and soul [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the purge: the vessels for Baal, the grove, and the host of heaven are burned in the fields of Kidron and their ashes carried to Bethel; the high places are defiled from Geba to Beersheba; Topheth is defiled; the sun's horses are taken away; the mount of corruption is defiled; and at Bethel the altar Jeroboam made is broken down and burned, "according to the word of the LORD which the man of God proclaimed, who proclaimed these words" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Passover is kept as written in the book — "Surely there was not holden such a passover from the days of the judges that judged Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And the chapter gives Josiah the highest praise any king in the book receives: "like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the LORD with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses; neither after him arose there any like him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then, in the next verse: "Notwithstanding the LORD turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Josiah is slain at Megiddo by Pharaohnechoh, and his sons undo nothing and restore nothing [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World




### The book found in the house

The reform begins with a repair. The temple needs work, the silver is summed, the overseers are paid, and the workmen are trusted without reckoning, "because they dealt faithfully" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. And in the middle of it, the high priest finds a book. He says only, "I have found the book of the law in the house of the LORD" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The text gives the book no title beyond that name, no account of its contents, and no history of how it came to be in the house. What the book contains is shown by what it does: its reading drives everything that follows — the king rends his clothes, sends to enquire of the LORD, makes a covenant, purges the land, and keeps the Passover [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The book is read twice in public: once by Shaphan before the king alone, and once by the king himself in the ears of all the people, small and great [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The first reading tears the king's clothes; the second binds the whole people to a covenant. A document found in the course of a repair remakes a kingdom's worship.

### Huldah and the word

The king does not act on the book alone. He sends five men — the high priest, the scribe, and three more — to enquire of the LORD concerning the words of the book that is found [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. They go not to Jeremiah but to Huldah the prophetess, wife of Shallum, keeper of the wardrobe, dwelling in Jerusalem in the college [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Her answer has the full prophetic form — "Thus saith the LORD God of Israel" — and it is split exactly in two [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The first half is the sentence on the place: the LORD will bring evil upon it, even all the words of the book the king has read, because the people have forsaken him and burned incense to other gods, "therefore my wrath shall be kindled against this place, and shall not be quenched" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The second half is the king's own portion: because his heart was tender and he humbled himself, rent his clothes, and wept, he will be gathered to his grave in peace and will not see the evil [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. She does not name the king: "Tell the man that sent you to me" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### The covenant and the purge

The king's answer to the book is a public covenant. He gathers the elders and all the people, small and great, and reads in their ears all the words of the book of the covenant [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Standing by a pillar, he makes a covenant to walk after the LORD and keep his commandments with all heart and soul, and all the people stand to it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. In the ancient world a covenant was a formal agreement in the language of the treaties between nations: preamble, history, stipulations, a document kept in the sanctuary and read aloud, witnesses, curses and blessings [^source-lucas-covenant-treaty-prophecy]. The Bible understands the covenant both as an unconditional promise that allows for divine judgment, and as a conditional agreement that might be broken by disobedience — two perspectives on the same covenant [^source-bible-odyssey-covenant]. Then the purge, carried out place by place. The vessels for Baal, the grove, and the host of heaven are brought out of the temple and burned outside Jerusalem in the fields of Kidron, and their ashes are carried to Bethel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The grove is burned at the Kidron; the altars of Ahaz and Manasseh are beaten down and their dust cast into the brook; the high places are defiled from Geba to Beersheba [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The high-place priests do not come up to the altar, "but they did eat of the unleavened bread among their brethren" — fed, not admitted [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Topheth, in the valley of the children of Hinnom, is defiled so that no man might make his son or daughter pass through the fire to Molech [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Valley of Hinnom curves around the western and southern sides of the city and joins the Kidron south of it, and its name became the source of the word Gehenna [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The sun's horses are taken away and its chariots burned; Solomon's high places for Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom are defiled, their places filled with the bones of men [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The defilement is followed to every place it had reached, and the ashes are carried back to Bethel, where the northern apostasy began.

### The man of God of Bethel

At Bethel the purge reaches back three centuries. A man of God had cried against the altar at Bethel that a child would be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and upon the altar would offer the priests of the high places and burn men's bones [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Josiah takes the bones from the sepulchres and burns them on the altar, "according to the word of the LORD which the man of God proclaimed, who proclaimed these words" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The men of the city tell him it is the sepulchre of the man of God who came from Judah and proclaimed these things against the altar [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "Let him alone; let no man move his bones" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. So his bones are left alone, with the bones of the old prophet of Bethel, who had buried him and told his sons to lay his bones beside his [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The bones of the man who spoke the word and the bones of the prophet who kept it lie together, untouched, while the bones around them are burned on the altar. The same is done to the high places in the cities of Samaria, and the king returns to Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### The Passover no king had kept

The Passover is the reform's feast. The king commands all the people to keep it as written in the book of the covenant [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "Surely there was not holden such a passover from the days of the judges that judged Israel, nor in all the days of the kings of Israel, nor of the kings of Judah; but in the eighteenth year of king Josiah, wherein this passover was holden to the LORD in Jerusalem" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Passover combines a springtime sacrificial meal with a feast of unleavened bread; together they celebrate the deliverance of the Israelites from Egypt [^source-bible-odyssey-passover]. The two festivals began in the agricultural calendar and were given their meaning in the exodus: the animal linked to the tenth plague, the unleavened bread to the hasty departure [^source-bible-odyssey-passover]. The comparison is therefore not with one earlier feast but with every Passover since the judges. In the same chapter the workers with familiar spirits, wizards, images, and idols are put away, to perform the words of the law in the book Hilkiah found [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### The king who was not enough

The chapter gives Josiah the highest praise any king in the book receives, and then takes back its power to change the sentence. "And like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the LORD with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses; neither after him arose there any like him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. "Notwithstanding the LORD turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath, wherewith his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations that Manasseh had provoked him withal" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The sentence was pronounced a chapter earlier, for Manasseh's abominations: the line of Samaria and the plummet of Ahab's house over Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The greatest reform in Judah's history does not change the sentence, because the sins it answers are Manasseh's, not Josiah's. And Josiah himself is slain at Megiddo by Pharaohnechoh, marching against the king of Assyria [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The text gives no reason; it states the death and moves on. His servants carry him dead to Jerusalem, and the people of the land make his son Jehoahaz king [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Jehoahaz reigns three months; Pharaoh puts him in bands at Riblah, lays a tribute of a hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold on the land, and makes Eliakim king as Jehoiakim [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Jehoiakim taxes the land to pay Pharaoh, "of every one according to his taxation" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The land purged of idols is now taxed for a foreign king.

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are about a book that is found, a king who obeys it, and a sentence it cannot turn. Knowing the world of the reform keeps the reader from treating Josiah's story as a triumph. The book is found in the course of a repair, read aloud to the king and then to the whole people, and obeyed with a thoroughness no other king shows: the vessels burned, the ashes carried to Bethel, the high places defiled from Geba to Beersheba, the Passover kept as no Passover had been kept since the judges. And the next verse says the wrath was not turned. The chapter also sets the terms for the end of the book. The greatest king is followed by sons who do evil as their fathers did, a land taxed for a foreign king, and a fall attributed to sins decades dead. The reform matters because it shows what obedience looks like at its fullest — and because it shows that even the fullest obedience, offered late, does not undo what a generation of apostasy had already earned. The book is read, the covenant is made, the land is purged, and the sentence stands.
