## Overview

Seven years after David was made king over Judah at Hebron, all the tribes come to him there and make him king over all Israel. They claim him as kin — "we are thy bone and thy flesh" — and remember that he led them out and brought them in when Saul was king, and that the LORD had said he would feed his people and be their prince [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. They make a covenant with him before the LORD, and he is anointed king over all Israel [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The book pauses to sum up his reign before it has run its course: thirty years old when he began to reign, forty years in all, seven years and six months in Hebron over Judah, thirty-three years in Jerusalem over all Israel and Judah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then David turns to the city that has never belonged to Israel. Jerusalem is still a Jebusite stronghold, its people so sure of their walls that they taunt him: even the blind and the lame could keep him out [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. He takes the stronghold of Zion and calls it the city of David, builds round about from the Millo inward, and Hiram king of Tyre sends cedar, carpenters, and masons to build him a house [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David perceives that the LORD has established him king and exalted his kingdom for his people's sake [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Sons are born to him in Jerusalem, among them Nathan and Solomon [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Twice the Philistines come up against him in the valley of Rephaim, and twice he enquires of the LORD before he moves — once told to go up, once told to circle behind and wait for the sound of marching in the tops of the mulberry trees — and twice the LORD delivers them into his hand [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then David turns to the unfinished business of a generation. The ark of God has rested twenty years in the house of Abinadab at Kiriath-jearim, ever since the Philistines sent it back and Israel had nowhere to put it [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He goes with thirty thousand chosen men to bring it up to Jerusalem, and the road runs through death and fear before it reaches gladness: Uzzah steadies the ark with his hand and dies beside it, the ark waits three months in the house of Obed-edom the Gittite, and only then does David bring it up with sacrifices, shouting, and dancing before the LORD in a linen ephod, and set it in the tent he pitched for it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. He blesses the people and gives bread to every man and woman [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. But Michal, Saul's daughter, watching from a window, despises the dancing king, and the chapter closes on her scorn and on her childlessness [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World

### A city that belonged to no tribe

Jerusalem was the prize David needed and the one no tribe had ever taken. It stood on a narrow ridge between deep valleys, above the Gihon spring, on the border between Benjamin and Judah — strong, small, and attached to no tribe at all [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites][^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The Jebusites had held it since before Israel entered the land. Their king had fallen to Joshua, but Judah could not drive them out, and neither could Benjamin, and through the period of the judges a traveller would rather press on to Gibeah than spend a night in a city of foreigners [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. The town was old: people had lived by the Gihon spring since the Early Bronze Age, and by the eighteenth century BC a Canaanite town stood there with a massive wall guarding its water [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. In the fourteenth century BC its ruler wrote to the pharaoh begging for troops, a modest hill town under Egyptian authority [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Beyond the Bible the Jebusites are not clearly attested by name at all, and whether their rulers carried a Hurrian strain is a guess built on two names, not a certainty [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. What made the city worth taking was its position, not its size: steep slopes on three sides, water at its foot, and a border no tribe could claim [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. A capital that belonged to no tribe could belong to all of them.

### Taking the stronghold

The taking itself is told in a few verses, and the few verses are difficult. The Jebusites taunt David that even the blind and the lame could keep him out, and his answering order turns on a single rare Hebrew word, *ṣinnôr*, usually rendered "water shaft" [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. The traditional explanation is that his men entered through a channel connected with the Gihon spring, and in 1867 the explorer Charles Warren found a vertical shaft in the rock above the spring that was long identified as David's route [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites][^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Later digging has complicated that picture: the shaft was probably not exposed until after David's time, and the spring was instead protected by massive fortifications from the Middle Bronze Age, with a rock-cut channel and pool inside the defences [^source-biblicaltraining-jebusites]. The honest answer is that the route is not known. What the text does say is plain enough: David took the stronghold of Zion, called it the city of David, and built round about from the Millo inward — the Millo probably the terraced fill that held buildings to the steep eastern slope [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Then came the house. Hiram king of Tyre sent cedar, carpenters, and masons, and they built David a palace — a foreign king's craftsmen raising the Israelite king's house, which David reads as the sign that the LORD has established him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The alliance Hiram began would continue under Solomon. Jerusalem was now the political centre of Israel; the next chapter would make it the religious centre too.

### Two battles in the valley

The Philistines heard that David was anointed king over all Israel, and they came up to seek him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. They were the standing enemy of Saul's reign and remained the standing enemy of David's [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. They spread themselves in the valley of Rephaim, and David went down to the hold and enquired of the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What follows is told as a pair, and the pairing is the point. The first time the LORD says, "Go up; for I will certainly deliver the Philistines into thy hand," and David smites them at Baal-perazim — "the LORD hath broken mine enemies before me, like the breach of waters" — and the place takes its name from the breaking [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Philistines abandon their images, and David's men take them away; the Chronicler's parallel says they were burned with fire [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The second time the Philistines come up again to the same valley, and David enquires again — and this time the answer is different: do not go up, circle behind them, and wait until you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the mulberry trees, for then the LORD has gone out before you [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David does so, and smites them from Geba to Gezer [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The two battles teach the same lesson from opposite directions: victory comes neither from a fixed tactic nor from the king's own initiative, but from asking first and then obeying the answer given, even when the answer changes. It is the positive image of the chapter that follows in the book, where David does not enquire at all.

### The ark comes home

The ark's journey is the longest road in the chapter, and it begins a generation earlier. The ark had been taken in battle at Aphek, carried through the Philistine cities under plague for seven months, and sent back by a new cart drawn by milch cows to Beth-shemesh; the men of Beth-shemesh sent it on to Kiriath-jearim, where it rested twenty years in the house of Abinadab [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. It was the holiest object in Israel, "whereupon is called the Name, even the name of the LORD of hosts that sitteth upon the cherubim," and for twenty years it had sat in a private house [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David goes to fetch it with thirty thousand chosen men, and sets it on a new cart, as the Philistines had done, driven by Uzzah and Ahio the sons of Abinadab, with music all the way [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. At Nachon's threshingfloor the oxen stumble, Uzzah puts out his hand to steady the ark, and God smites him there, and he dies beside it [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The place is called Perez-uzzah, "the breaking forth against Uzzah," unto this day. David is displeased and afraid — "How shall the ark of the LORD come unto me?" — and carries it aside to the house of Obed-edom the Gittite, where it stays three months, and the LORD blesses Obed-edom and all his house [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Law had assigned the carrying of the ark to the Levites, who were to bear it and not touch the holy things; whether the fault lay in the cart or in the touch, Samuel does not say, and Chronicles alone gives David's later explanation, that the Levites had not carried it as prescribed [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. What the chapter shows is the change the three months work: told of the blessing on Obed-edom's house, David brings the ark up with joy, and this time it is carried by bearers on foot, with a sacrifice of an ox and a fatling every six paces, and David dancing before the LORD with all his might, girded with a linen ephod, as the ark comes in with shouting and the sound of the horn [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The linen ephod is a priestly garment — the child Samuel wore one at Shiloh — and the king in it looks less like a conqueror than like a servant of the sanctuary [^source-bible-odyssey-samuel]. The ark is set in the tent David pitched for it, offerings are made, the people are blessed, and bread is dealt to every man and woman [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The glory that departed at Shiloh has a home again, and the home is a tent in the new capital, waiting for the temple that is still to come.

### The window

The chapter does not end in the street with the shouting. It ends in David's house, at a window. Michal the daughter of Saul has watched the procession from above and despised the king in her heart before she says a word [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. She has been at a window before, when she let the fugitive David down through one to save him [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. Then she saved the fugitive; now she judges the king. Her charge is that he uncovered himself before the eyes of the handmaids, "as one of the vain fellows shamelessly uncovereth himself" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. David's answer turns on the audience: it was before the LORD, who chose him above her father and all his house to be ruler over Israel, and before the LORD he will make merry — and be yet more vile, and base in his own sight, and honoured by the very handmaids she named [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The quarrel is about more than dancing. Michal speaks for the dignity of Saul's house; David speaks for a kingship that kneels. The chapter's last word is the narrator's, not theirs: "Therefore Michal the daughter of Saul had no child unto the day of her death" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The house of Saul ends in the house of David, and the sons born in Jerusalem — among them Nathan and Solomon — are the future the chapter leaves standing.

## Why This Context Matters

These two chapters are the founding of Jerusalem, and they found it twice over: as a capital and as a sanctuary. The anointing at Hebron makes David king of a united people, but a people needs a place, and David's choice of a city that belonged to no tribe is what lets a divided kingdom hold together. The ark's coming makes the same city holy, and the road it travels — through death at the threshingfloor, three months in a stranger's house, and a second procession on foot — teaches what the capture alone could not: that God is not a possession, not even of his own people. The ark that was fetched to guarantee a battle was lost in it; the ark that was carried on a cart killed the man who steadied it; only the ark that is borne, sacrificed to, and danced before comes home. Knowing the world of these chapters — a small ridge city above a spring, a water shaft that probably was not David's route, a king in a linen ephod dancing before the handmaids — keeps the reader from turning the founding into a legend of easy triumph. It also explains everything that follows. The tent David pitched becomes the problem of the next chapter, and the promise of a house for ever its answer; the sons born in Jerusalem become the kings after him; and the dancing king, despised by Saul's daughter and vindicated before the LORD, is the pattern of the kingship the whole book is about.
