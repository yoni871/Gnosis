## Overview


Elijah has just won the greatest victory of his life, and he is running for it. On Mount Carmel the fire fell, the people cried that the LORD is God, the prophets of Baal were slain at the Kishon, and the rain returned after three years of drought [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Ahab goes home and tells Jezebel everything — and Jezebel sends a messenger with an oath: by this time tomorrow, Elijah's life will be like the lives of the prophets he killed [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. Elijah rises and flees south to Beersheba, in Judah, leaves his servant there, and goes a day's journey alone into the wilderness, where he sits under a broom tree and asks to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life; for I am not better than my fathers" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The LORD does not grant the request. An angel wakes him twice with bread baked on hot stones and a cruse of water, because the journey is too great for him, and in the strength of that food he travels forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-horeb]. There, in a cave, the LORD asks him twice, "What doest thou here, Elijah?" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Elijah answers both times the same way: he alone is left, and they seek his life. Between the two questions the LORD passes by — wind tearing the mountains, earthquake, fire — and the LORD is not in any of them; after the fire comes a still small voice [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Elijah covers his face with his mantle and stands at the mouth of the cave. Then comes the commission: return by the wilderness of Damascus and anoint Hazael king over Syria, Jehu king over Israel, and Elisha prophet in his place — and the LORD's answer to his loneliness, seven thousand in Israel who have not bowed to Baal [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Elijah goes north and finds Elisha plowing with twelve yoke of oxen, casts his mantle on him, and Elisha slaughters his oxen, feeds the people with the meat, and follows him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha].

## Ancient World


### The queen who threatened him

Jezebel was a Phoenician princess, the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, who became the wife of King Ahab of Israel in the 9th century BC [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. She grew up in a court where the worship of Baal and of the goddess Astarte was the religion of the state, and her marriage to Ahab sealed an alliance that gave Israel access to Phoenician trade, craftsmanship, and luxury goods [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. Ahab built a temple and an altar for Baal in Samaria and set up an Asherah pole, and Jezebel went further: she supported hundreds of Baal's prophets at the royal table and had the LORD's prophets killed [^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. Her threat to Elijah is not a private quarrel but the voice of that court. She swears it by an oath — "So let the gods do to me, and more also" — the same self-imprecatory formula the Bible uses for the most solemn vows, here spoken with the plural "gods" by a queen who does not worship the LORD [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jezebel]. The deadline is the next day, and the sentence is to make Elijah's life like the lives of the prophets he killed at the Kishon. Elijah does not argue, and he does not stay to see whether she means it. He runs. The prophet who stood alone against 450 prophets on Carmel flees from one woman's message, and the chapter does not explain the change — it only records the flight, south past the border of his own kingdom, to Beersheba in Judah, and then a day's journey beyond help into the wilderness.

### Bread, water, and forty days

The wilderness south of Beersheba is dry country, and the chapter gives Elijah the traveller's fare: bread baked on hot stones and a cruse of water, set at his head while he sleeps under a broom tree [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-horeb]. The angel wakes him twice, and the second time gives the reason: the journey is too great for him. He eats, and goes in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Forty days and forty nights is the span of Moses on the same mountain, and the destination is the same: Horeb is another name for Sinai, where the LORD gave the covenant to Moses [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah][^source-biblicaltraining-horeb]. The Hebrew name for the mountain comes from a root meaning "to be dry" or "to lie waste," which fits the barren granite mountains and stony valleys of the wilderness where Israel camped [^source-biblicaltraining-horeb]. Its exact site is still debated. The traditional site is Jebel Musa, a peak about 7,500 feet high in the southern Sinai Peninsula, where monks were living by the 4th century AD — but no ancient inscription names the site, and no remains from Israel's encampment have been found anywhere in the peninsula [^source-biblicaltraining-horeb][^source-biblicaltraining-mount-sinai]. Elijah is walking back to the beginning: to the mountain where the covenant was made, to complain that the covenant has been forsaken, its altars thrown down, and its prophets slain with the sword.

### Wind, earthquake, fire, and a voice

In the cave the LORD asks, "What doest thou here, Elijah?" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Elijah answers with his complaint, and then he is told to stand on the mount, for the LORD is about to pass by. A great and strong wind tears the mountains apart and shatters the rocks — but the LORD is not in the wind. After it comes an earthquake — but the LORD is not in the earthquake. After the earthquake a fire — but the LORD is not in the fire. And after the fire comes a still small voice [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The Hebrew phrase is hard to translate; it has been rendered as a sound of thin silence and a low murmuring sound, and in the King James Version as "a still small voice" [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. When Elijah hears it, he wraps his face in his mantle and goes to the mouth of the cave, and the question comes again — and he gives the same answer, word for word [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Readers have taken the scene in several ways. Many take it to mean that the LORD's purposes would go forward less through public displays like the one on Carmel than through his quiet word and the faithful remnant. Others stress the link with Sinai, which came with fire, smoke, and trembling, and see a deliberate contrast with the storm imagery of Baal worship. Still others point out that the commission that follows is full of judgment, and caution against reading the episode as a lesson about divine gentleness alone [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The chapter itself does not choose between these readings. It gives the sequence — three great displays, each disclaimed, and then the voice — and leaves the meaning open. What it does show is the prophet's posture: he covers his face, as Moses was sheltered in the cleft of the rock while the LORD's glory passed by [^source-biblicaltraining-horeb].

### Three anointings and one mantle

The LORD sends Elijah back the way he came, to the wilderness of Damascus, with three tasks: anoint Hazael king over Syria, Jehu son of Nimshi king over Israel, and Elisha son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah as prophet in his place [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Those who escape the sword of Hazael will be killed by Jehu, and those who escape Jehu will be killed by Elisha. But Elijah himself carries out only the third of these tasks [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Elisha later tells Hazael that he will become king of Aram, and Elisha sends a member of the company of the prophets to anoint Jehu — the other two commissions are finished years later, through Elisha [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The anointing of a king was the central act in making him: oil poured on the head set the man apart, and the anointed king's person was sacred [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Yet no anointing with oil is recorded for Elisha at all. Elijah finds him plowing with twelve yoke of oxen, himself driving the twelfth pair — a team that size points to a family of some means — and throws his cloak over him, a sign that he is claiming the younger man as his successor [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Elisha asks only to kiss his father and mother goodbye. Elijah's answer is brief and has been read in more than one way: it may mean Elisha is free to go home, or that he should weigh what has been done to him, since the choice to follow must be his own [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Elisha's answer is an act, not a word: he slaughters his oxen, burns the plowing equipment to cook the meat, and gives it to the people — destroying the tools of his trade and making the break with his old life complete [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Then he follows Elijah and becomes his attendant, remembered years later simply as the man who poured water on Elijah's hands [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha].

### Seven thousand who never bowed

Against Elijah's claim — "I, even I only, am left" — the LORD sets a number: seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to Baal, and every mouth that has not kissed him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The two signs are the postures of worship withheld: the knee that never bent and the mouth that never kissed the god's image. Baal was the storm god, the rider of the clouds, who sent the rain and dew and thunder that gave the land its fertility [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. To bow and kiss him was to credit him with the rain — the very rain Elijah had withheld and restored in the LORD's name. The seven thousand are not named, and the chapter does not say where they are. Even Obadiah, who hid a hundred prophets in caves and fed them with bread and water, is one of the faithful the LORD is about to remind Elijah of [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Elijah's loneliness is real — he has fled for his life, and the queen's threat is real — but the LORD's count is larger than the prophet's sight. The chapter ends not with the prophet's complaint but with the LORD's arithmetic, and with a younger man burning his plows behind him.

## Why This Context Matters


This chapter is the hinge of Elijah's life: the prophet who called down fire on Carmel learns that the LORD is not only in the fire. Knowing the world of the Omride court — a Phoenician queen, a royal Baal temple at Samaria, prophets maintained at the queen's table and prophets hidden in caves — keeps the reader from turning Elijah's flight into a simple failure of nerve. He fled a real threat from a real court, into a real wilderness, to the mountain where the covenant was made. The wind, the earthquake, and the fire are Baal's own phenomena, the storm god's display, and the text passes each of them by before the voice comes. And the commission that follows is the point: Elijah is not finished, the faithful are not only one, and the work goes on through other hands — a Syrian king, an Israelite commander, and a plowman from Abel-meholah who burns his old life behind him. The still small voice sends the prophet back to work.
