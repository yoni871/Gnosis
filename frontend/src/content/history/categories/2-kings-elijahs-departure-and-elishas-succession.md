## Overview



Ahaziah, king of Israel, falls through a lattice in his upper chamber at Samaria and lies sick. Instead of asking the God of Israel whether he will live, he sends messengers to Baalzebub, the god of Ekron, to enquire of him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. On the road the angel of the LORD sends Elijah the Tishbite to meet them with a question: is it because there is no God in Israel that the king sends to Baalzebub? [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The sentence follows the question — the king will not come down from his bed, but will surely die — and the messengers carry it back to Samaria [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. When the king asks what manner of man met them, they describe a hairy man girt with a girdle of leather, and he knows at once: it is Elijah the Tishbite [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Twice the king sends a captain of fifty to bring the prophet down, and twice fire falls from heaven and consumes the captain and his fifty [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The third captain falls on his knees and begs for his life and his men's lives, and the angel tells Elijah to go down with him and not be afraid [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elijah delivers the sentence to the king's face, and the king dies according to the word the prophet spoke; his brother Jehoram reigns in his stead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then comes the departure. The LORD is about to take Elijah up into heaven by a whirlwind, and Elijah sets out with Elisha from Gilgal [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. At Bethel, at Jericho, and at the Jordan, Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind, and three times Elisha swears he will not leave him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The sons of the prophets at Bethel and Jericho tell Elisha his master will be taken that day, and he answers that he knows it and they should hold their peace [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. At the Jordan Elijah wraps his mantle, smites the waters, and the two cross on dry ground [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Across the river Elisha asks for a double portion of his master's spirit — the firstborn's share, a claim to be his principal heir and successor — and Elijah calls it a hard thing, granted only if Elisha sees him taken [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha][^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. As they walk and talk, a chariot of fire and horses of fire part them, and Elijah goes up by a whirlwind into heaven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elisha cries, "My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof," takes up the fallen mantle, and smites the waters again — "Where is the LORD God of Elijah?" — and they part, and he crosses back [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The sons of the prophets at Jericho see him and say the spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha, and bow before him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. They still send fifty men to search for Elijah for three days, and find him not [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the new ministry begins: the waters of Jericho are healed with salt from a new cruse, so that there shall be no more death or barren land [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. And going up to Bethel, Elisha is mocked by little children, curses them in the name of the LORD, and two she bears come out of the wood and tare forty and two of them [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He goes on to Mount Carmel, and returns to Samaria [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

## Ancient World



### The prophet and the king

The book of 2 Kings opens on a dying king and a prophet he cannot command. Ahaziah, the son of Ahab, has fallen through a lattice in his upper room at Samaria, and his first act as a sick man is to send beyond Israel's borders for an answer about his life [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Baalzebub, the god of Ekron, is a Philistine god, and the name is widely thought to be a mocking alteration of Baal-Zebul, "Baal the prince," a title known from the Ugaritic texts [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The king passes over the God of Israel to consult a foreign god, and that choice is the whole of the chapter's charge against him. Elijah meets the messengers on the road, and his question is asked three times before the chapter ends: to the messengers, by the messengers to the king, and by Elijah to the king's face [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The question carries its own answer: the king has insulted the God of Israel by asking elsewhere, and the sentence follows — the king will not rise from his bed. The chapter then proves the title the captains use for Elijah. They call him "man of God" as a summons, and Elijah answers on the title's own terms: if he is a man of God, let fire come down from heaven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Twice the fire comes. The third captain uses the same title but kneels with it, begging that his life and his men's lives be precious in the prophet's sight, and the angel tells Elijah to go down with him and not be afraid [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The episode turns on that title, because Elijah's authority came from the God whom the king had passed over [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The king dies according to the word Elijah spoke, and the chapter's last note is the succession: his brother Jehoram reigns, because Ahaziah had no son [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

### The road to the Jordan

Chapter 2 opens by telling the reader what is about to happen: the LORD is going to take Elijah up to heaven in a whirlwind [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. What follows is a journey in four stages — Gilgal, Bethel, Jericho, and the Jordan — and at each of the first three Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Three times Elisha refuses with the same oath: as the LORD lives, and as his master's soul lives, he will not leave him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The repetition is the test: the successor is the one who will not be left behind. At Bethel and at Jericho the sons of the prophets — companies of prophets attached to those towns — come out to tell Elisha that the LORD will take his master from his head that day [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. He answers both times the same way: he knows, and they should hold their peace [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He will not discuss what is coming, and he will not stay behind for it. Fifty of the prophets stand to view afar off at the Jordan, watching [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elijah takes his mantle, wraps it together, and smites the waters, and they divide so that the two cross on dry ground [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The road itself matters. Gilgal may have been a town in the hill country rather than the Gilgal near Jericho, since the two men "went down" from Gilgal to Bethel, though this is debated [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Bethel had housed one of the two royal sanctuaries with golden calves since the days of Jeroboam [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Jericho had been rebuilt in Ahab's day in defiance of Joshua's curse, at the cost of the builder's sons [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Elijah goes to the region east of the Jordan opposite Jericho, the area where Moses died and Joshua took up his work [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The journey ends on the far side of the river, with the master about to be taken and the disciple about to be left.

### A double portion

Across the Jordan, Elijah asks Elisha what he shall do for him before he is taken away, and Elisha asks for a double portion of his spirit [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The phrase comes from the law of inheritance, which gave the firstborn son a double share of his father's estate, and most interpreters understand the request as a plea to be Elijah's principal heir and successor [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha][^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Elisha is not asking to be twice as great as Elijah. He is asking to be recognised as his principal heir, equipped with the same Spirit that had empowered his master [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The share is asked of a master, not a father, and the claim is to succession: the disciple asks to stand in the master's place, with the master's spirit upon him. Elijah calls it a hard thing, and makes it conditional on seeing: if Elisha sees him when he is taken, it shall be so; if not, it shall not be so [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. The condition is the proof: Elisha must be present and watching at the moment of the taking. As they walk and talk, a chariot of fire and horses of fire part them asunder, and Elijah goes up by a whirlwind into heaven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The text says the whirlwind carried Elijah up; the familiar picture of the prophet riding in the fiery chariot goes slightly beyond it [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Elisha sees it — and that seeing is what he was told to do. He cries, "My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof," a cry that honours the prophet as the nation's true defence, worth more than its armies [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah].

### The mantle and the waters

The succession is proved by a mantle and by water. Elijah's cloak was the prophet's distinctive garment, and when he found Elisha plowing he threw it over him as the sign that the younger man was claimed for his office [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The only command to anoint a prophet in the Old Testament is the Lord's instruction to Elijah to anoint Elisha as his successor, and the narrative records no ceremony with oil; instead Elijah throws his cloak around Elisha [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. In chapter 2 the cloak does the work. Elijah wraps it and smites the Jordan, and the waters divide [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. It falls from him as he is taken up, Elisha takes it up, goes back to the river, smites the waters, and asks, "Where is the LORD God of Elijah?" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The waters part again, and Elisha goes over [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The second parting is the proof of the succession, and the sons of the prophets at Jericho are the ones who name it: the spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha, and they bow before him [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The parting also recalls older crossings. The miracle recalls the crossing of the Red Sea under Moses and of the Jordan under Joshua [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah]. Like Joshua, Elisha served a great prophet, succeeded him, and began his work by crossing a divided Jordan near Jericho [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. But the watchers still do not accept what they have seen. The sons of the prophets insist on sending fifty strong men to search for Elijah, fearing the Spirit has cast him on some mountain or into some valley [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Elisha refuses, then gives way when they urge him till he is ashamed. They seek three days and find him not, and he answers, "Did I not say unto you, Go not?" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Only Enoch shares Elijah's end in the Old Testament, taken up without dying [^source-biblicaltraining-elijah].

### Blessing and judgment at the start

Elisha's first two acts as successor are a healing and a curse, and they stand side by side without comment. At Jericho the men of the city tell him the town is well situated but the water is bad and the land unproductive, and he asks for a new bowl with salt in it, throws the salt into the spring, and declares in the Lord's name that the water is healed [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The Hebrew expression suggests the land caused miscarriage or loss, whether of livestock, of crops, or of people [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Salt was associated with the covenant in Israel's worship, but the prophet's words make it clear that the Lord, not the salt, healed the water [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. From Jericho Elisha goes up to Bethel, the town of the golden calf, and as he approaches, a crowd of young people comes out and jeers at him: "Go up, thou bald head" [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. Elisha turns, looks at them, and calls down a curse on them in the name of the Lord, and two bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. This episode has troubled many readers, and its interpretation is discussed elsewhere [^source-biblicaltraining-elisha]. The chapter itself states it without comment, and this account does the same. The new prophet brings both blessing and judgment. Elisha then goes to Mount Carmel, where his master faced the prophets of Baal, and returns to Samaria, the royal capital [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text].

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are about how a prophet's work passes to another man without breaking — and what it costs to receive it. Knowing the world of the handoff keeps the reader from treating Elijah's departure as a disappearance and Elisha's succession as an appointment. The master is taken in a whirlwind, and the disciple is left with a fallen mantle, a parted river, and a question he must answer with his own hands. The double portion is not twice the power but the firstborn's share, claimed by a servant who refused three times to be left behind. The proof of the succession is not a ceremony with oil but the waters parting a second time, and the watchers naming what they see. And the ministry that begins here opens with both hands at once: waters healed at Jericho, and bears at Bethel. The prophet leaves; the word he spoke stays, and the spirit that rested on him rests on another — and the book that follows is the story of what that spirit does next.
