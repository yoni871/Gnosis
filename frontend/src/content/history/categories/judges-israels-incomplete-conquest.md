## Overview

After Joshua's death, Judges opens not with a single national campaign but with tribal initiatives. Judah is told to lead; Judah and Simeon cooperate, and the account moves among engagements involving Bezek, Jerusalem, Hebron, Debir, and the southern settlements. Caleb, Achsah, and Othniel appear in a family episode that also occurs in Joshua 15. The narrative assigns to Judah the capture and burning of Jerusalem (Judges 1:8), but later says the Benjaminites did not dislodge the Jebusites living there (1:21). These statements belong to the book's complex presentation of territorial control; neither should be silently erased to create a uniform conquest story.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

The account also credits the house of Joseph with an operation against Bethel, formerly Luz (1:22–26). It names towns in which Manasseh did not drive out inhabitants, including Beth-shean and Megiddo (1:27–28), and extends its account of incomplete displacement across Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali, and Dan. Some inhabitants are made subject to forced labor rather than expelled. The chapter's cumulative movement is from localized success to the persistence of mixed populations and contested land. That unevenness prepares the reader for the covenant crisis announced in chapter 2.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

Within the chapter the individual episodes also carry their own weight. Judah's opening inquiry is answered with a divine direction that Judah should go up first, and Simeon joins the campaign (1:1–3). At Bezek the coalition defeats the Canaanites and Perizzites and captures Adoni-bezek (1:4–7). Later Judah is unable to drive out the inhabitants of the valley because of their iron chariots (1:19), and Dan is pressed back into the hill country by the Amorites (1:34–35). Joseph's house prevails at Bethel only with the help of a man from the city who shows them the entrance (1:22–26). These details keep the chapter's geography concrete — named towns, valleys, and hill country — even as the larger picture refuses to resolve into a single completed conquest.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

Judges 1 is therefore more than a military ledger. Its repeated statements about those who remained shape the moral and literary transition into the following narratives. The text describes the actions and failures of Israelite groups; it does not give modern archaeology a catalogue of separately confirmed battles. Its local focus also complicates the impression of a fully settled, unified polity at the outset of the book.

## Ancient World

### From Egyptian province to highland settlement

The world behind Judges 1 is best approached through the transition from Late Bronze Age Canaan to the early Iron Age. Late Bronze Canaan included cities, regional rulers, and networks affected by Egyptian imperial power; the subsequent centuries saw changes in settlement and political organization. Archaeological surveys identify significant developments in highland rural settlement, but interpretation remains disputed. Some reconstructions emphasize local social transformation, some population movements, and others multiple interacting processes. Material settlement patterns cannot identify the ethnic identity, motivations, or individual victories of every group named in a biblical narrative.[^source-finkelstein-mazar-quest-for-the-historical-israel-2007]

The Egyptian dimension is concrete. Finkelstein and Mazar describe Canaan of that time as an Egyptian province, administered by Egyptian garrisons, and note that according to the Amarna letters fifty Egyptian soldiers were enough to pacify an area. Canaanite city-state rulers — Labayu of Shechem among them — appear throughout the Amarna correspondence. The collapse of the Late Bronze Canaanite city-state system was not a single event but a long process that took at least several decades. Large-scale surveys conducted in the central hill country in the 1980s indicated that the rise of ancient Israel was one phase in a long-term, repeated, and cyclic process of sedentarization and nomadization of autochthonous groups.[^source-finkelstein-mazar-quest-for-the-historical-israel-2007]

Three models of Israel's origins are commonly set out. One traces semi-nomadic pastoralists arriving from Transjordan; a second sees local pastoralists, including Shasu groups, settling the hill country; a third sees dispossessed Canaanite peasants forming new highland communities. Mazar concludes that a combination of components from all three theories may explain the hill-country settlement wave. The same authors warn against reading ethnicity directly from material culture: it is questionable whether the central hill-country settlements should be attributed to a single ethnic entity, and the identification of the hill-country settlers as Israelites rests mainly on the biblical traditions, so using biblical sources for ethnic identification of Iron I settlers risks circular argument. Nothing in the archaeological record, they add, hints at the existence of Jebusites or Gibeonites as separate ethnic entities.[^source-finkelstein-mazar-quest-for-the-historical-israel-2007]

### Judges and Joshua: parallel episodes, not independent confirmation

Judges 1 and Joshua share several named episodes and sites. Caleb's family narrative in Judges 1:11–15 closely resembles Joshua 15:13–19. The Manassehite list in Judges 1:27–28 corresponds to Joshua 17:11–13. These are genuine literary relationships, but agreement among biblical passages is not equivalent to independent confirmation of the episodes. Likewise, the contrast between Joshua's sweeping conquest language and Judges' detailed non-dispossession raises questions of perspective, genre, and composition rather than permitting a simple verdict that one account is a modern field report and the other an error.[^source-biblical-texts-canonical-cross-references]

The Caleb parallel is particularly close. J. Cornelis de Vos[^source-de-vos-caleb-achsah-episode] observes that Joshua 15:13–19 and its parallel Judges 1:10–15 are remarkable, and that verses 11–15 in Judges 1 are almost identical to Joshua 15:15–19: Caleb's offer of his daughter Achsah to whoever captures Kiriath-sepher, Othniel's success, and Achsah's request for springs of water appear in both. The Manassehite correspondence is equally direct: Joshua 17:11–13 lists Beth-shean, Ibleam, Dor, En-dor, Taanach, and Megiddo, and reports that the sons of Manasseh could not take possession of these cities but put the Canaanites to forced labor without driving them out completely — the same towns and the same outcome as Judges 1:27–28. The two books therefore preserve overlapping traditions about the same places; what they share is a literary relationship between texts, and that relationship must not be mistaken for two independent witnesses to a single event.[^source-biblical-texts-canonical-cross-references]

### Places: anchors and disputed identifications

Place names need different degrees of confidence. Jerusalem and the regions around Beth-shean and Megiddo are substantial geographic anchors, while Debir's exact location remains disputed. The identification of Bethel with a modern site is not the same thing as verification of the narrated ambush. Archaeological destruction at a site, even if datable, may have several causes and cannot automatically be attached to a named Israelite leader. The geography of western Manasseh in Judges 1:27 concerns settlements west of the Jordan, not the later east-bank territory of the same tribe.

Debir illustrates the uncertainty. Its site is debated: Khirbet Rabud, southwest of Hebron, is now the most widely favored identification, ahead of Albright's Tell Beit Mirsim, with Edh-Dhahiriyeh as a third candidate. The *Encyclopaedia Judaica* notes that Albright's Tell Beit Mirsim identification is usually accepted but that some scholars reject it in favor of Khirbat Rūbūʿ, an identification which appears probable; the Biblical Archaeology Society now states that Debir is generally identified with Khirbet Rabud and the identity of Tell Beit Mirsim is unknown. Bethel stands at the other end of the spectrum: the identification with Beitin, based on Albright's 1927 test pit, has been endorsed by the majority of scholars, though a minority has argued for el-Bireh instead, and the archaeology of Bethel, especially for the conquest era, is still debated. A widely accepted site identification still does not verify the ambush narrated at that site, and a disputed one like Debir cannot carry any historical weight at all until the location itself is settled.[^source-debir-bethel]

### One verse, two readings: Judges 1:18

Judges 1:18 deserves special textual caution. The Hebrew Masoretic tradition gives Judah victories at Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ekron, while a Greek textual tradition reads the verse differently, yielding non-capture. Differences among witnesses matter, and the Greek versions must not be treated as if every manuscript says precisely the same thing.[^source-nets-judges-greek-texts] The variation affects how the local conquest list is read but should not be turned into an invented archaeological consensus. The chapter juxtaposes military claims, endurance of local peoples, and later inability to drive them out; each statement must be read in its own textual setting.

The divergence is exact and verifiable. The Westminster Leningrad Codex Hebrew of Judges 1:18 reads *wayyilkōd Yəhūdāh ʾet-ʿAzzāh…* — "Judah took Gaza with its territory, and Ashkelon with its territory, and Ekron with its territory." Both Greek texts in the New English Translation of the Septuagint read the opposite: Judges A, 1:18, "And Ioudas did not inherit Gaza and its territory and Ascalon and its territory and Akkaron and its territory and Azotus and its surrounding lands," and Judges B to the same effect. The Greek therefore adds a fourth city, Azotus (Ashdod), to the Masoretic tradition's three, while reversing the outcome from capture to non-capture. The NETS introduction warns that Rahlfs' A text is now divided into further groups, that neither the A nor the B text consistently represents the Old Greek, and that the forthcoming Göttingen edition will print only one main text, identical to neither Rahlfs' A nor B. The very next verse sharpens the caution: Judges 1:19 limits Judah's success in the valley because of iron chariots, and that limit erases neither reading of verse 18. Readers must hold both witnesses without harmonizing them and without claiming that all Greek manuscripts say precisely the same thing.[^source-nets-judges-greek-texts][^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

### The refrain: "did not drive out"

The phrase 'did not drive out' recurs so frequently that it becomes a narrative refrain. Within the book, this is a covenantal evaluation of incomplete obedience, not an explanation that isolates a single identifiable cause for every settlement pattern. Forced labor also appears in the narrative, showing that social domination and continued co-residence could accompany claims of military superiority. The historical reader should distinguish the text's moral interpretation of Israel's situation from modern reconstructions of the region's demographic history.

The refrain runs through the whole chapter: Benjamin did not drive out the Jebusites (1:21); Manasseh did not drive out the inhabitants of Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam, and Megiddo (1:27); Ephraim did not drive out the Canaanites in Gezer (1:29); Zebulun, Asher, and Naphtali likewise left Canaanites among them (1:30–33). Forced labor accompanies the failure at several points — "when Israel was strong, that they put the Canaanites to tribute, and did not utterly drive them out" (1:28), and the Canaanites in Gezer, Kitron, Nahalol, Beth-shemesh, and Beth-anath "became tributaries" (1:30, 33). The pattern is therefore not only military but social: domination without displacement, co-residence under tribute, and a land shared rather than cleared by force. That is the situation the angel's rebuke in Judges 2:1–3 will name as covenant failure, and it is the situation the rest of the book inhabits.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]

## Why This Context Matters

The chapter helps explain why Judges begins with ambiguity rather than triumph. Israel's tribes have inherited promises and obligations, yet they continue to inhabit a land with other communities, competing religious practices, and unequal local power. The theological question is not simply whether a given city changed hands on a single date, but how Israel responds to the covenant while inhabiting a contested landscape.

Reading the Joshua parallels carefully prevents a false choice between treating every reported conquest as excavated fact and dismissing the narrative's own internal purposes. The differences between Hebrew and Greek readings of Judges 1:18 further remind readers that responsible interpretation starts with the actual wording of the text. Judges 1 offers the setting for the repeated failures and rescues that follow, while leaving many precise historical details open.

For readers today the chapter also models how to hold two things at once: the text's own theological claim that incomplete obedience has consequences, and the limits of what archaeology can confirm about any single episode. The Jebusites remain in Jerusalem (1:21), the Canaanites remain in Gezer (1:29), and the Amorites press Dan into the hills (1:34–35) — a landscape of unfinished business that the book will revisit again and again. Judges 1 does not resolve that tension; it establishes it as the condition under which Israel's story in the book unfolds.[^source-hebrew-bible-judges-masoretic-text]
