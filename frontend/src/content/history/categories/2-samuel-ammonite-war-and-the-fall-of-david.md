## Overview



A condolence visit ends in war, a king stays home while his army fights, and a prophet tells a story that condemns the king who hears it. The three chapters at the centre of 2 Samuel — chapters 10 to 12 — are one war with a sin set inside it. When Nahash, king of Ammon, dies, David sends servants to comfort his son Hanun, "as his father shewed kindness unto me" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. The kindness itself is never explained; the narrative never says what Nahash did for David [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. Hanun's advisers persuade him the servants are spies, and he shaves off half their beards, cuts their garments to the buttocks, and sends them home in disgrace [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. War follows. The Ammonites hire Aramean armies, and Joab fights them on two fronts, dividing his force with his brother Abishai; then David himself crosses the Jordan and defeats the regrouped Arameans at Helam, and the Syrians make peace and help Ammon no more [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. But Rabbah, the Ammonite capital, is never taken — not yet. The next spring, "at the time when kings go out to battle," Joab besieges Rabbah, and David stays in Jerusalem [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. From his roof one evening he sees Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite, and takes her; when she sends word that she is with child, David calls Uriah home from the siege and twice tries to send him to his house, but Uriah will not go while the ark and the army are in the field [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. So David sends him back carrying a letter that orders his death, and Uriah dies before the wall of Rabbah [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then the LORD sends Nathan, who tells the king a story about a rich man who steals a poor man's one ewe lamb. David burns with anger and pronounces the sentence — and Nathan answers, "Thou art the man" [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. The sentence is that the sword shall never depart from David's house; David confesses, and is forgiven, and yet the child born to Bathsheba dies [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Bathsheba bears another son, Solomon, and the LORD loves him [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. And Joab takes the royal city of Rabbah and sends for David, who comes and takes the city and its crown of a talent of gold, and the war is over [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah].

## Ancient World



### Ammon and its capital

The Ammonites were a Semitic people of the Transjordan, the highlands east of the Jordan River, and Genesis traces them to Ben-Ammi, a son of Lot, which made them kin to both Israel and the Moabites [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. Their kingdom centred on the upper Jabbok River, the modern Wadi Zarqa, around their capital Rabbah, the site of modern Amman in Jordan [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. They spoke a language very close to Hebrew and Moabite, so that Israelites and Ammonites could understand one another without difficulty [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. God told Israel not to take Ammonite land on the approach to Canaan, and Israel kept to that command; even so, the two peoples were in conflict for most of their shared history, under Jephthah, Saul, David, and later kings [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. Their national god was Milkom, often identified with Molek [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. Rabbah itself lay about twenty-two miles east of the Jordan, on the edge of the plateau where farmland gives way to desert, and it owed its existence to a strong spring at the head of the Wadi Amman [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. The ancient city had two parts: a lower town beside the stream in the valley, and a high, steep-sided hill to the north, now called Jebel al-Qalʿa, the Citadel, which was easy to defend and held the main public and religious buildings [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. That shape is the key to the siege: when Joab takes the "royal city" and the "city of waters," he has taken the lower town and its water, leaving the citadel cut off, and he sends for David to finish the taking himself [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah].

### An embassy, a beard, and a war

Hanun's father Nahash had begun his reign as an enemy of Israel: he besieged Jabesh-gilead and demanded the right eye of every man, until Saul rallied Israel and routed him [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. Yet David could later say that Nahash had shown him kindness, and the text does not say what that kindness was; interpreters have often suggested it belongs to the years when David was a fugitive from Saul, since both men then had reason to oppose Saul, but that is an inference, not something the narrative states [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. When Nahash died, David sent a delegation to offer condolences, and the Ammonite commanders warned Hanun that the visit was not what it seemed: David had sent these men to look over the city and spy it out so that he could overthrow it [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. Hanun believed them. In the ancient Near East a full beard was a mark of a man's dignity; shaving it was associated with mourning or disgrace, and forced exposure was the treatment given to prisoners of war [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. By shaving half the envoys' beards and cutting their garments away at the buttocks, Hanun shamed David himself and rejected any friendly relationship with Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. David told the men to remain at Jericho until their beards had grown back [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. The Ammonites prepared for war by hiring Aramean troops — Chronicles notes a payment of a thousand talents of silver — from Beth-rehob, Zobah, Maacah, and Tob [^source-biblicaltraining-ammonites]. Joab, caught between the Ammonites at the gate and the Arameans in the field, divided his force with his brother Abishai, each to help the other if pressed, and urged his men to be strong for their people and the cities of their God, leaving the outcome to the LORD [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. The Arameans fled, the Ammonites withdrew into their city, and the war was left unfinished: the Syrians were done with, but Rabbah stood.

### The king who stayed home

The account of David's sin is set in the spring, the season when kings went to war [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. David sent Joab and the army to besiege Rabbah, but he stayed in Jerusalem himself [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. From the palace roof David sees Bathsheba bathing; he asks who she is and is told she is the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah the Hittite [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. He sends messengers, takes her, and she returns home; the narrator adds that she had been purifying herself after her monthly period, a note that explains the bath and also makes clear that the child she conceived could not have been Uriah's [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. Bathsheba's only recorded words in the chapter are her message to David that she was pregnant [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. David then calls Uriah home from the front, but Uriah refuses to go home while the ark and the army are camped in the field, even when David gets him drunk [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. The Hittite soldier holds the line the king has abandoned. His name means "the Lord is my light," which suggests that, despite his foreign ancestry, he worshipped Israel's God, and he was listed among David's elite warriors, the Thirty [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. When the cover fails, David writes a letter to Joab and sends it by Uriah's own hand, ordering him to be put where the fighting is fiercest and then left exposed [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. Joab assigns him where he knows the strongest defenders are, and Uriah falls in a sortie from the city along with other Israelite soldiers [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. Joab scripts the report: if the king is angry at the costly approach to the wall, the messenger is to cite Abimelech, killed by a millstone at Thebez, and then add that Uriah is dead also [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. David answers: the sword devours one as well as another, so make the battle stronger against the city [^source-mechon-mamre-2-samuel-masoretic-text].

### The prophet with a story

Nathan was a prophet at the royal court in Jerusalem, and Scripture shows him at three turning points: he brought God's promise of a lasting dynasty to David, he confronted David over Bathsheba and Uriah, and in David's old age he helped make sure that Solomon, not Adonijah, succeeded to the throne [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. Scripture says nothing of his family, home town, or call; he simply appears at court with ready access to the king [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. When the LORD sends him after David's sin, Nathan does not begin with an accusation. He tells a story. A rich man with large flocks and herds had a guest to feed; rather than take an animal of his own, he seized the one ewe lamb of a poor neighbour, a lamb the poor man's family had raised like a daughter, and served it to his visitor [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. David, taking the story as a real case brought for judgment, burns with anger and declares that the rich man deserves to die and must repay the lamb four times over [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. Then Nathan says, "Thou art the man." [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. The charge follows: the LORD anointed David, rescued him from Saul, and gave him a kingdom, yet David despised the word of the LORD by killing Uriah with the sword of the Ammonites and taking his wife [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. The sentence is twofold: the sword will never depart from David's house, and what David did in secret will be repaid in public — his wives taken before his eyes, in the sight of the sun [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. David confesses, "I have sinned against the Lord," and Nathan replies that the Lord has taken away his sin and he will not die — yet because David showed such contempt for the Lord, the son Bathsheba bore him would die [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. The child falls ill and dies despite David's fasting and prayer [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan]. Then Bathsheba bears another son, Solomon, and the Lord loves the child and sends word through Nathan to name him Jedidiah, "loved by the Lord" [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan].

### Rabbah taken, and the hard verse

At last Joab takes the royal city and the water supply, and he sends word to David to gather the rest of the army and capture the city himself, lest it be named after Joab [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. David comes, takes Rabbah, and carries off a great quantity of plunder, including a gold crown weighing a talent and set with precious stones, which is placed on his own head [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. Whether the crown belonged to the Ammonite king or to an image of the god Milkom is not settled [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. The treatment of the city's people is the hardest verse in the category. Older translations understood the text to mean that David put the people to death with saws and iron tools; most modern versions read it as forced labour, with saws, iron picks, and axes, and in brickmaking [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. The older English and the JPS 1917 agree on the harsher reading, and the verse is genuinely difficult; neither reading is settled [^source-biblicaltraining-rabbah]. Ammon thereby lost its independence and became subject to Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun]. Years later Shobi son of Nahash brought David supplies at Mahanaim; whether David had installed him as ruler at Rabbah is not stated [^source-biblicaltraining-hanun].

## Why This Context Matters



These three chapters are the hinge of David's reign, and of the whole book. The king who was anointed, delivered from Saul, and promised an enduring house is the same king who stays home from the war, takes another man's wife, and has the man killed by letter. Knowing the world of these chapters — a small Transjordan kingdom guarding a trade route, a capital built on a spring, a beard as a man's dignity, a siege that drags on while the king stays home — keeps the story from shrinking into a private moral tale. It is a public fall, in wartime, by a king whose army is in the field. And knowing Nathan's world matters just as much: a prophet could stand before a king who held power of life and death, charge him with murder and adultery in God's name, and be heard. The sentence Nathan speaks does not end with chapter 12. The sword that will never depart from David's house governs everything that follows — the violence among his sons, the revolt of Absalom, the public repayment on the housetop. The forgiveness is real, and so are the consequences; the child dies, Solomon is born, and the war is won. The book will not let the reader have one without the other.
