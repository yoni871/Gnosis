## Overview

David has killed Goliath, and everything changes. Jonathan, the king's son and heir, loves him as his own soul, makes a covenant with him, and gives him his robe, his sword, his bow, and his belt — the heir handing over the marks of his own rank [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. But when the women greet the returning army singing, "Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands," Saul hears a threat to his throne, and from that day he watches David with fear [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The spear follows — twice while David plays the harp — then a marriage trap, with Michal offered as a snare and a bride-price of Philistine foreskins meant to kill the suitor, then an open order to kill [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Jonathan pleads for his friend and wins a short peace, but the spear flies again, and David flees by night through a window, helped by his wife Michal, to Samuel at Ramah, where Saul's own messengers are overcome and prophesy instead of arresting him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. From there the flight never really stops: a secret test with Jonathan at the new-moon feast, the arrows that signal exile, bread and a sword from the priest at Nob while Doeg the Edomite watches, madness feigned before the king of Gath, four hundred outcasts gathering at Adullam, a massacre of eighty-five priests at Nob, a rescue at Keilah, betrayal by the Ziphites in the wilderness, and the Rock of Escape where Saul nearly closes in [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Between the escapes Samuel dies, and Abigail turns David back from destroying Nabal's household on the road to Carmel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. And twice — in a cave at En-gedi and in a sleeping camp at Hachilah — David stands over the king who hunts him, and refuses to lift his hand against the Lord's anointed [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul weeps, confesses, and blesses him both times, and both times the two part with nothing resolved.

## Ancient World

### A covenant between friends

Jonathan was the heir to Saul's throne, a proven commander who had struck the first blow against the Philistines at Geba [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. When he met David he bound himself to him by covenant, giving him his robe, his tunic, his sword, his bow, and his belt [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. The gift was more than affection: the heir handed over the clothing and weapons that marked his own rank. The language of love in these chapters is the language of ancient covenants and political treaties as well as of personal devotion — the same vocabulary of loyalty appears in the treaties of the ancient world, and 1 Samuel also says that all Israel and Judah loved David [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. At the new-moon feast Jonathan asks David to show the Lord's unfailing kindness — *hesed*, loyal covenant love — to him and to his family for ever, even when the Lord has cut off all David's enemies, and he makes a covenant with the house of David [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. In effect he is already treating David as the future king. At their last meeting, in the wood at Horesh, he tells David not to be afraid: David will be king, and Jonathan himself will be second to him — and Saul knows it too [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. Jonathan never breaks with his father's household. He stays at court, serves in Saul's army, and dies at his side, while refusing to share in the pursuit of David — at the cost of his own claim to the throne, which he openly gives up in David's favour [^source-biblicaltraining-jonathan]. The loyalty he swears reaches into the next generation: years later, David cares for Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth because of the oath they swore.

### Bread, sword, and massacre at Nob

Fleeing from Saul, David comes alone to Nob, to the priest Ahimelech, who meets him trembling and asks why he is alone [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. There is no ordinary bread, only the consecrated loaves — the bread of the Presence, twelve loaves kept continually before the Lord on a gold-covered table and renewed every Sabbath, standing for the twelve tribes [^source-biblicaltraining-showbread]. Ahimelech gives them to David once he is assured the men have kept themselves clean, and gives him besides the sword of Goliath, kept at the sanctuary wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod [^source-biblicaltraining-showbread]. Watching is Doeg the Edomite, Saul's chief herdsman, "detained before the LORD" that day. His report brings Saul's vengeance down on the whole priestly town: eighty-five men wearing a linen ephod are slain, and then Nob itself — men, women, children, cattle — is put to the sword [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. One son, Abiathar, escapes to David, bringing the ephod with him. David tells him he knew, the day he saw Doeg at Nob, that Doeg would tell Saul, and that he has brought about the deaths of Abiathar's father's house [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. From then on the priesthood travels with the fugitive: it is through the ephod Abiathar carries that David asks God whether to rescue Keilah, whether Saul will come down, whether the townspeople will hand him over [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The text describes these inquiries and their answers without explaining how the ephod gave them, and this account does the same: the mechanism is not known.

### The wilderness of Judah

David's flight runs through the wilderness of Judah, the dry, rugged country that falls away eastward from the hill country to the Dead Sea [^source-biblicaltraining-wilderness-of-judaea]. The word for wilderness here does not mean a sea of sand. It means open, uncultivated land, too dry for farming but often good enough for grazing flocks after the winter rains [^source-biblicaltraining-wilderness-of-judaea]. The region lies in the rain shadow of the central ridge: Jerusalem receives around twenty inches of rain a year, while the shore of the Dead Sea, less than twenty miles away, receives under four [^source-biblicaltraining-wilderness-of-judaea]. Across only ten to fifteen miles the land drops from around three thousand feet above sea level to more than thirteen hundred feet below it, one of the steepest descents in the land [^source-biblicaltraining-wilderness-of-judaea]. Its smaller districts take their names from the nearest town or spring — the Desert of Ziph, the Desert of Maon, the Desert of En Gedi — and David moves among them as the Ziphites betray him, Saul nearly catches him at Maon, and he withdraws at last to En Gedi [^source-biblicaltraining-wilderness-of-judaea]. En Gedi is a spring-fed oasis halfway down the western shore of the Dead Sea, its name meaning "spring of the young goat," where cliffs climb two thousand feet within a short distance and gorges full of caves have sheltered fugitives throughout history [^source-biblicaltraining-en-gedi]. The springs offered water, the caves offered shelter, and the high ground offered a view of anyone approaching [^source-biblicaltraining-en-gedi]. It was in one of those caves that Saul, chasing David with three thousand chosen men, went in alone — and found David and his men hiding deep inside it.

### The Lord's anointed

Anointing a king in the ancient world marked him out as God's choice, and the spirit of the Lord that came with the oil was the sign of it [^source-milgrom-anointing-encyclopaedia-judaica]. Twice David stands over Saul with his life in his hands, and twice he refuses on the same ground: Saul is the Lord's anointed. At En-gedi he creeps up behind the king in the cave and cuts off only the corner of his robe — and even that troubles his conscience [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At Hachilah he and Abishai slip into the sleeping camp by night, and Abishai offers to pin Saul to the ground with one thrust of his own spear; David takes only the spear and the water jug from beside the king's head [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. In both scenes David's men urge him on, and in both he answers that no one can put forth his hand against the Lord's anointed and be guiltless. He proves his innocence with the piece of the robe and the spear and the jug, calls across the distance to the king, and hears Saul weep and confess — "I have sinned; I have played the fool and erred exceedingly" — and bless him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. And both times the two part with nothing resolved: Saul goes home, and David goes his way. The second sparing changes nothing, which is why the next chapter opens with David deciding he cannot remain in Israel at all.

### Nabal and Abigail

Between the two sparings Samuel dies, and David goes down to the wilderness of Paran, where a very rich man named Nabal is shearing three thousand sheep at Carmel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. David's men have guarded Nabal's shepherds in the wilderness and taken nothing; David asks only for whatever comes to hand on a feast day. Nabal answers with contempt: "Who is David? and who is the son of Jesse?" David sets out to destroy his household, and only Nabal's wife Abigail, riding out with two hundred loaves, wine, sheep, parched corn, raisins, and fig cakes, turns him back [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. She takes the blame on herself, tells him the Lord has kept him from bloodguiltiness, and speaks of the "sure house" the Lord will give him because he fights the Lord's battles [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. David blesses her for keeping him from taking revenge with his own hands. Ten days later Nabal is dead, and David marries Abigail. The episode is the hinge between the two sparings: the same David who will not kill the Lord's anointed is kept, by a woman's words on a road, from a killing that would have stained his own hands.

## Why This Context Matters

The persecution of David only makes sense when its world is in view. A covenant sworn in the Lord's name between an heir and a shepherd, a song that turns a king's court into a conspiracy, consecrated bread eaten by a fugitive, a priestly town destroyed for sheltering him, a wilderness where a man can hide for years in caves and strongholds, and a king who is hunted and yet untouchable because he is anointed — these are the stakes of the story, not its decorations. Knowing that *hesed* is covenant loyalty, not mere kindness, explains why Jonathan gives up a throne for a friend. Knowing that the wilderness of Judah is a rain-shadow desert of cliffs and caves, and that En-gedi is the one green oasis in it, explains how David survives and where the two sparings happen. And knowing what "the Lord's anointed" meant — God's chosen king, marked by oil and spirit — explains the strangest thing in these chapters: a man who will not kill the king who is trying to kill him, twice, and walks away both times with the proof in his hand.
