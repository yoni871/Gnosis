## Overview

Deuteronomy 4–6 is the covenant's frame: Moses' second speech, in which the Horeb theophany is retold, the Decalogue is rehearsed, and the Shema is given as the sum of the covenant.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] It addresses the generation about to enter the land, binding it to the covenant made at Horeb — "not with our fathers, but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive this day."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Deuteronomy 4 opens with an exhortation to hear and do the statutes and judgments, neither adding to the word nor diminishing from it, and warns from Baal-peor: those who followed Baal-peor were destroyed, while those who cleaved to the LORD are alive.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The law is presented as Israel's wisdom before the nations — "what nation is there so great, that hath statutes and judgments so righteous as all this law?" — and the people are charged to teach their sons, and their sons' sons, what their eyes saw at Horeb.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] At Horeb the mountain burned with fire, the voice of the words was heard, and no similitude was seen; the covenant was declared, ten commandments on two tables of stone.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Because no form was seen, no graven image of any figure may be made, and the sun, moon, stars, and host of heaven may not be worshipped. Egypt is "the iron furnace" from which the LORD took Israel to be a people of inheritance, and Moses himself is barred from crossing the Jordan. The chapter looks forward to scattering among the nations to serve gods of wood and stone — yet "if from thence thou shalt seek the LORD thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul." The confession follows: "the LORD he is God; there is none else beside him."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] It closes with the three cities of refuge east of Jordan — Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan — and a geographical introduction to the law, from Aroer by the Arnon to Mount Sion, which is Hermon.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

Deuteronomy 5 rehearses the ten words. The preamble grounds them in the exodus — "I am the LORD thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage" — and the ten words follow.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The substance agrees with Exodus 20; the wording differs, above all in the Sabbath rationale — creation there, deliverance here. The words were spoken to the whole assembly "with a great voice: and he added no more," and written on two tables of stone. The people ask Moses to stand between them and God, and God approves: "they have well said all that they have spoken. O that there were such an heart in them, that they would fear me, and keep all my commandments always."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Deuteronomy 6 gives the Shema: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: and thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The words are to be in the heart, taught diligently to children, bound as a sign upon the hand and as frontlets between the eyes, and written upon the posts of the house and on the gates. Prosperity brings the danger of forgetting the LORD, and the chapter closes with a catechism: when the son asks what the testimonies mean, the answer is the exodus, "We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-clarke-commentary] No absolute year is given; the text's setting is the fortieth year, in the plains of Moab.

## Ancient World

### The Horeb theophany retold

Deuteronomy 4 does not narrate the theophany for the first time; it retells Exodus 19–20 as memory and argument.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] In Exodus the theophany is narrated as event; in Deuteronomy the same scene is recalled to draw a conclusion: because the people heard a voice but saw no similitude, no image may be made.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The mediator is also foregrounded: "I stood between the LORD and you at that time," Moses says, explaining a delivery that Exodus 20 leaves to the people's fear alone, and Deuteronomy 5 expands the people's speech and adds God's explicit approval, which has no counterpart in Exodus 20.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-keil-delitzsch-deuteronomy]

The mountain itself is unidentified. Horeb is the mountain of Deuteronomy 4:10–15 and 5:2; Exodus names the same mountain Sinai, and the two names denote the same theophany mountain in the two books' usage.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] Site identity and event correlation are both Unknown: there is no direct archaeological evidence for the Horeb theophany as narrated, and no event pin is proposed for the mountain.[^source-jfb-commentary] The traditional site is Jebel Musa.

The chapter's close is Transjordanian: the valley over against Beth-peor, the land of Sihon at Heshbon and of Og of Bashan, from Aroer by the Arnon to Mount Sion, which is Hermon, and all the plain eastward even unto the sea of the plain.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] Heshbon is Tell Hesban by tradition, but no clear remains of a Late Bronze Age city — the period usually associated with Moses and the conquest — were found there, and the earliest Iron Age settlement appears to have been modest; Sihon's Bronze Age Heshbon has not been found. Bashan, Gilead, the Arnon, Hermon, and the sea of the plain are identifiable as regions and landmarks; Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan have no established modern sites in the sources read here.

### The two Decalogues

The Decalogue of Deuteronomy 5:6–21 agrees in substance with Exodus 20:1–17, and the wording differences are part of the text.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] "Remember the sabbath day" becomes "Keep the sabbath day to sanctify it, as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee"; the rest list is particularised with ox and ass, adding "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou"; the fifth commandment adds "that it may go well with thee"; the tenth places the wife first, adds "his field," and uses two verbs, "desire" and "covet."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The chief difference is the Sabbath rationale: creation in Exodus 20:11 — "For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth" — against deliverance in Deuteronomy 5:15 — "remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] One commentary holds the added motive implies no contrariety: the Sabbath is of former enactment, and the deliverance motive is peculiarly seasonable.[^source-jfb-commentary]

The Septuagint of Deuteronomy 5 keeps the deliverance rationale and gives the commandments in the order murder, adultery, steal — the same as the Hebrew text, not the adultery, steal, murder order of the Septuagint of Exodus 20.[^source-brenton-septuagint][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy] The Nash Papyrus, a second-century BCE Hebrew manuscript carrying the Ten Commandments and the start of the Shema, blends Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 and gives the commandments in the order adultery, murder, steal; it is the oldest known Hebrew biblical manuscript before the Dead Sea Scrolls.[^source-nash-papyrus] A preamble before the Shema, taken from Deuteronomy 4:45, stands in both the Nash Papyrus and the Septuagint before Deuteronomy 6:4.[^source-nash-papyrus][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy]

### The Shema and its words

"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD." The Hebrew of Deuteronomy 6:4 has four nouns and no copula, and admits several renderings — "YHWH our God, YHWH is one," or "YHWH is our God, YHWH alone" — and no single rendering is imposed by the grammar.[^source-bible-odyssey-shema][^source-clarke-commentary] The verb "hear" itself, *šāmaʿ*, means hear with attention, understand, give heed, obey — so the Shema's "Hear" is obedient hearing, not bare audition; and *ʾeḥād*, "one," is the numeral properly meaning united.[^source-bdb-lexicon] "And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might" — *mĕʾōd* as a substantive meaning might, force, abundance.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

One scholarly reading, presented in a Bible Odyssey article read in full, holds that in its biblical context the Shema is a call to covenant loyalty to YHWH alone — a monolatrous reading, with a monotheistic rereading in the Second Temple period, evidenced by the Septuagint's translation "YHWH is one" and by the Nash Papyrus. That reading is Debated: it is one scholarly reading, and this page does not present it as the text's plain meaning. The counter-reading is the text's own confession, recorded as Established: "the LORD he is God; there is none else" (Deut 4:35, 39).[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-bible-odyssey-shema] Whether the monolatrous reading appears on the published page, and how prominently, is an editorial decision reserved to Yonatan.

The New Testament takes up the Shema in three forms. Mark 12:29–30 quotes Deuteronomy 6:4 verbatim and adapts 6:5, adding "mind" and keeping "strength." Matthew 22:37 adapts rather than quotes: no 6:4, and 6:5 reduced to heart, soul, mind. Luke 10:27 combines all four terms and joins Leviticus 19:18; the words are spoken by the lawyer and affirmed by Jesus.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv] The "mind" addition is explained by the Septuagint, whose Deuteronomy 6:5 renders "with the whole of your mind" — a scholarly inference, assessed Probable.[^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy] The New Testament Decalogue lists confirm the two orders: Romans 13:9 and James 2:11 follow the Septuagint order, adultery first, while Matthew 19:18 follows the Hebrew order, murder first.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### Bind, write, remember

"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children... And thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes. And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The verb for "teach them diligently," *šinnantām*, carries the sense of repeat, iterate, whet, sharpen.[^source-clarke-commentary]

The historic Jewish reading of Deuteronomy 6:8–9 is literal — tefillin, four passages in a case worn on forehead and arm, and the mezuzah on the doorposts — attested from antiquity to the present day.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-bible-odyssey-shema] Josephus, in his retelling of Moses' farewell, attests the practice: the blessings inscribed on the doors and borne on forehead and arm. One commentary suggests Moses used the language merely figuratively, "to denote the unremitting attention and the intense love" with which the words should be treasured, while noting the Israelites read the words literally. Whether Moses intended the language figuratively is Debated; the practice is Traditional. Whether the figurative reading is named on the published page is an editorial decision reserved to Yonatan.

The same binding language reaches back to Exodus 13: "a sign upon thine hand, and for a memorial between thine eyes."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] And the Shema's later use was apotropaic as well as liturgical: silver scrolls of the seventh century BCE bearing the Priestly Benediction, and a sixth- or seventh-century CE amulet carrying the Shema, attest its protective use in antiquity.[^source-bible-odyssey-shema]

### Covenant, treaty form, and the conditional shape

The covenant here is the Horeb covenant — the ten commandments as its terms, written on two tables of stone.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-bible-odyssey-covenant] The Hebrew Bible holds together two perspectives: the unconditional, eternal covenant — Abraham, David, the Sabbath — and the conditional covenant, broken if the people fail and restored on repentance. Deuteronomy 4:25–31 holds both: exile is predicted as the consequence of breach, restoration promised on seeking — "the LORD thy God is a merciful God."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-bible-odyssey-covenant]

The covenant's language is based on ancient Near Eastern treaties, and Deuteronomy's structure — historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, witnesses (heaven and earth, Deut 4:26) — follows that form. That comparison rests on the Bible Odyssey covenant article and the text's own structure, and is assessed Probable; the treaty-form study itself was not read.[^source-bible-odyssey-covenant]

One textual variant touches this category. Deuteronomy 6:13 reads, "Thou shalt fear the LORD thy God, and serve him, and shalt swear by his name." A commentary note records that the form "him only shalt thou serve" — *lebaddo* — was anciently in the Hebrew text, retained in the Septuagint, the Coptic, the Vulgate, and the Anglo-Saxon, but absent from the printed Hebrew text. It is the form Jesus quotes (Matt 4:10; Luke 4:8).[^source-clarke-commentary][^source-bible-gateway-kjv] The variant is Probable; no emendation is proposed. Whether the published page notes the variant is an editorial decision reserved to Yonatan.

Prosperity — cities, houses, wells, vineyards not built by Israel — brings the danger of forgetting the LORD: "when thou shalt have eaten and be full; then beware lest thou forget the LORD."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-clarke-commentary] "Ye shall not tempt the LORD your God, as ye tempted him in Massah," naming the testing of Exodus 17, and the son's question is answered with the exodus: "We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-clarke-commentary] The Septuagint renders the closing line "there will also be mercy for us" against the King James Version's "it shall be our righteousness" — a genuine variant in the transmission.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy]

## Why This Context Matters

These three chapters are the hinge of Deuteronomy: the covenant given at Horeb is rehearsed for a generation that did not stand there, and its sum is given in two sentences — one LORD, and love with all the heart, soul, and might.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The passage makes the covenant a matter of hearing and doing, of teaching children and talking of the words at home and on the way, and of remembering the exodus when prosperity tempts the people to forget.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-clarke-commentary]

The differences between the two Decalogues show that the law was not copied but re-spoken: the same substance, with the Sabbath grounded now in deliverance rather than creation, and the promise "that it may go well with thee" added — the very form Paul quotes in Ephesians 6.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-bible-gateway-kjv] The Shema's afterlife confirms its centrality: quoted verbatim by Mark, adapted by Matthew and Luke, carried on a second-century BCE papyrus and on doorposts and foreheads to the present day, and rendered by the Septuagint with the "mind" the Gospels preserve.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nash-papyrus][^source-nets-septuagint-deuteronomy]

What the passage leaves unresolved is left unresolved here too: the mountain's place, the speeches' year, and the scholarly debates over the Shema's sense and the binding words' intent are recorded as they stand, not settled. The reader meets the text as it stands — a covenant offered to the living generation, a commandment to love, and a catechism that answers the child's question with the exodus.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]
