## Overview



Manasseh is twelve years old when he begins to reign, and he reigns fifty and five years in Jerusalem — the longest reign in the book [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He is the son of Hezekiah, the king who removed the high places and broke the brasen serpent, and his reign undoes his father's work in a single chapter. He builds up again the high places Hezekiah destroyed, rears altars for Baal, makes a grove "as did Ahab king of Israel," and worships all the host of heaven [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. He builds altars in the house of the LORD itself, and in the two courts of the house, and sets a graven image of the grove in the house — the house of which the LORD said, "In Jerusalem will I put my name" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. He makes his son pass through the fire, deals with familiar spirits and wizards, and sheds innocent blood "till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the LORD speaks by his servants the prophets, and the sentence falls. "Because Manasseh king of Judah hath done these abominations... I will stretch over Jerusalem the line of Samaria, and the plummet of the house of Ahab: and I will wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish, wiping it, and turning it upside down. And I will forsake the remnant of mine inheritance" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The sentence is measured by the north: Samaria's line, Ahab's plummet. Manasseh dies and is buried in the garden of his own house, in the garden of Uzza, not in the city of David with his fathers [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. His son Amon reigns two years, walks in all his father's way, and is slain by his own servants in his own house. The people of the land slay the conspirators and make Josiah his son king in his stead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The chapter ends with a boy on the throne and a sentence hanging over the city — a sentence Josiah's great reform will not turn away.

## Ancient World



### The longest reign, told as a list

Fifty and five years is the longest reign in the book, and the chapter spends almost none of it on events. There is no war, no embassy, no building project, no illness, no sign. There is a list. High places rebuilt, altars reared for Baal, a grove made, the host of heaven worshipped, altars built in the house and in its two courts, a son passed through the fire, times observed, enchantments used, familiar spirits and wizards dealt with [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Each item is added to the last until the narrator closes the list with his own summary: Manasseh seduced the people "to do more evil than did the nations whom the LORD destroyed before the children of Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The length of the reign is the point. A short reign could be a lapse; a fifty-five-year reign is a settled order. The chapter gives the reign no story because the reign is the story: half a century of the worship Hezekiah tore down, built up again and left standing. The Jerusalem article reads the reign the same way, naming its acts together — the high places rebuilt, the altars to the starry hosts in the temple courts, the sorcery and child sacrifice, the innocent blood — and calling it the point that made the city's destruction certain [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The list is the evidence for the sentence, and the sentence answers it point for point.

### The house where the Name was put

Jerusalem is the city David captured and made the capital, where Solomon built the temple, and from then on it was the center of Israel's worship, the seat of David's dynasty, and the focus of the prophets' warnings and hopes [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. The temple was the permanent sanctuary of the LORD, the place where God chose to put his Name, replacing the portable tabernacle and housing the ark of the covenant [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. Its plan was a porch, a main hall, and a Most Holy Place, set within courts that held the bronze altar and the great bronze Sea [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. That is the building Manasseh fills. The altars for the host of heaven stand in the two courts, and the graven image stands in the house itself, where the LORD had said to David and to Solomon, "In this house, and in Jerusalem, which I have chosen out of all tribes of Israel, will I put my name for ever" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Solomon's Temple article records the act in one sentence: "Manasseh built altars to the starry hosts in both temple courts and set up a carved Asherah pole in the temple itself" [^source-biblicaltraining-solomons-temple]. The First Temple article counts it among the changes to the sanctuary portrayed as violations of its sanctity [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. No trace of the temple has been unearthed, because excavations are not allowed on the Temple Mount, so what the building looked like is known from the Bible's own description and from neighbouring Iron Age temples of the same plan [^source-bible-odyssey-first-temple]. The chapter does not need the stones to make its point. It quotes the promise in the same breath as the pollution, and lets the two stand together: the house of the Name, holding the image of the grove.

### Graded by the north

Every standard applied to Judah in this chapter comes from somewhere else. The worship is graded by Ahab: "as did Ahab king of Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The wickedness is graded by the Amorites, the peoples before Israel in the land: Manasseh "hath done wickedly above all that the Amorites did, which were before him" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The sentence is measured by Samaria and by Ahab's house: "the line of Samaria, and the plummet of the house of Ahab" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Ahab is the north's worst king, Samaria is the north's fallen capital, and the Amorites are the peoples whose abominations cost them the land. Judah is judged in units that are not its own. The line and the plummet are a builder's tools — a line stretched to mark a wall, a plumb-weight dropped to test whether it stands true — turned here into instruments of measuring for destruction. What was measured against Samaria, and against Ahab's house, is now measured against Jerusalem. Then the image changes, and the chapter reaches for the household instead of the building site: "I will wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish, wiping it, and turning it upside down" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. A dish is wiped clean and turned over, emptied of everything it held. The sentence is total, and it is written in the north's handwriting.

### Blood beside sin

After the sentence has already been spoken, the chapter adds a second charge. "Moreover Manasseh shed innocent blood very much, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another; beside his sin wherewith he made Judah to sin" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The blood is not part of the sentence's stated reason. The sentence names the abominations, the idols, and the evil done since Egypt. The blood comes after, as a further count, and the chapter does not say whose blood it was. No victim is named, no number is given, no single killing is narrated. The blood simply fills the city, from one end to another, and waits. It waits two reigns and a reform. When the fall comes, the book attributes it to the blood by name: "for the sins of Manasseh, according to all that he did; and also for the innocent blood that he shed: for he filled Jerusalem with innocent blood; which the LORD would not pardon" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The distance between the shedding and the sentence is the chapter's quietest fact. Manasseh dies in his bed and is buried. The blood is not answered in his lifetime, and the pardon is refused for it a generation later. The chapter leaves the reader holding both counts at once: the sin that made Judah sin, and the blood beside it.

### The garden, and the people

Manasseh "slept with his fathers, and was buried in the garden of his own house, in the garden of Uzza: and Amon his son reigned in his stead" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. David was buried in the city of David, and the kings after him with their fathers there. Manasseh is not, and neither is his son: Amon "was buried in his sepulchre in the garden of Uzza" [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The Jerusalem article records the break plainly: "He and his son Amon were buried in the garden of Uzza rather than in the old royal tombs" [^source-biblicaltraining-jerusalem]. Where the garden lay, and why these two kings were buried there, the text does not say, and this account does not guess. What it shows is the pattern broken at exactly the reign the sentence falls on, and broken for father and son together. Amon's two years repeat his father's way in miniature — the same evil, the same idols, the same forsaking of the LORD God of his fathers — and end with his servants conspiring against him and slaying him in his own house [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. Then the people of the land act. They slay all the conspirators and make Josiah, Amon's eight-year-old son, king in his stead [^source-mechon-mamre-2-kings-masoretic-text]. The throne does not pass by the servants who slew the king. It passes by the people of the land, who will act as kingmakers again when Josiah dies at Megiddo. The chapter ends with a boy on the throne, a garden holding two kings, and a sentence waiting.

## Why This Context Matters



This chapter is about how a kingdom talks itself into destruction — not in a single battle, but in fifty-five years of rebuilding what a reforming father tore down. Knowing the world of the chapter keeps the reader from treating Manasseh as a footnote between two good kings. The longest reign in the book is told as a list of abominations, and the list is the evidence for the sentence that follows it. The sentence is measured by the north — Samaria's line, Ahab's plummet — and spoken in the house where the LORD put his Name, beside the image set up in that house. The blood beside the sin waits a generation to be answered, and the reform that follows, the greatest in Judah's history, does not turn the wrath away. That is the chapter's warning, and the reason it stands alone: the sentence has to be heard on its own before the reform can be understood, because the reform's failure to turn it is the book's central tension.
