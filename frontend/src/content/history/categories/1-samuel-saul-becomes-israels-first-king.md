## Overview

It begins with lost donkeys. Kish's asses stray, and his son Saul searches the hill country with a servant until they reach the land of Zuph, where the servant remembers the man of God in the city [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel is waiting for him: the Lord has told him a Benjamite will come, and he is to anoint him prince over Israel to save the people from the Philistines [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At the feast Samuel seats Saul at the head of the guests; at dawn he sends the servant on and says, "Stand thou still a while, that I may cause thee to hear the word of God" — and pours oil on his head [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then come the three signs: two men by Rachel's tomb with news that the asses are found, three men at the oak of Tabor who give him two loaves, and the company of prophets at Gibeah, where the Spirit comes upon Saul and he prophesies among them, and the people say, "Is Saul also among the prophets?" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At Mizpah the tribes present themselves by lot, and the lot falls on Saul, found hiding among the baggage, standing taller than all the people [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel writes the manner of the kingdom in a book and lays it up before the Lord [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. But the king is not yet king. It takes a war to make him one: Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh-gilead and demands every man's right eye, and Saul cuts a yoke of oxen in pieces, summons all Israel, and scatters the Ammonites in the morning watch [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At Gilgal the kingdom is renewed, Saul is made king before the Lord, and the people rejoice greatly [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Samuel speaks his farewell: he has taken nothing from any man's hand, the Lord has delivered them again and again, and now, if they fear the Lord, it shall be well with them and with their king [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Thunder and rain fall in the days of wheat harvest, and the people are afraid [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The king is made — and the condition of his standing is stated before he has done a single deed as king.

## Ancient World

### The seer and the feast

Kish's asses strayed, and Saul searched with a servant through the hill country of Ephraim, Shalishah, Shaalim, and Benjamin, until he reached the land of Zuph and was ready to turn home [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The servant remembered the man of God in the city: a seer whose word came true, and he offered the quarter-shekel of silver in his hand [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The narrative pauses to explain the word itself: "Beforetime in Israel, when a man went to inquire of God, thus he said: Come, and let us go to the Seer; for he that is now called a Prophet was beforetime called a Seer" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The seer was a figure who spoke for God in a community where prophetic speech was rare [^source-biblicaltraining-samuel]. At the ascent, maidens told them the seer was at the high place for a sacrifice, and the people would not eat until he blessed it [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel seated Saul at the head of the thirty invited guests and served him the reserved portion — the honour kept for the guest of honour [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The feast is the first public honour Saul receives, before any anointing: the private honour precedes the public one.

### Anointing: the central act

At dawn Samuel sent the servant on and told Saul to stand still to hear the word of God, and then he took the vial of oil, poured it on Saul's head, kissed him, and said the Lord had anointed him prince over His inheritance [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Anointing was the central act in making a king in Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. To anoint is to smear or pour oil on a person's head; the Hebrew verb is *māšaḥ*, from which the word *māšîaḥ*, "Messiah," comes [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The sacred anointing oil for the tabernacle was compounded from myrrh, cinnamon, calamus, cassia, and olive oil, in proportions given in Exodus 30, a formula whose imitation was forbidden on pain of being cut off [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The act carried two meanings at once: the person was set apart for God's service, and the Spirit came upon him for the task [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. "The Lord's anointed" became a title of sacred standing — even a bad king was not to be struck down because he was the Lord's anointed [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. Anointing was known across the ancient Near East: an Amarna letter from the fourteenth century BC reports a vassal king anointed at the Pharaoh's command, and other texts describe the anointing of kings, priests, and even cultic objects [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. What is distinctive in Israel is the combination: the anointing is the Lord's choice, conveyed by His prophet, and it is accompanied by the Spirit. Samuel anointed Saul privately with a flask of oil; David would later be anointed at Bethlehem [^source-biblicaltraining-anointing].

### The three signs

Samuel gave Saul three signs, and all three came to pass the same day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Two men by Rachel's sepulchre in Zelzah told him the asses were found; three men at the oak of Tabor, carrying three kids, three loaves, and a bottle of wine, greeted him and gave him two loaves; then a company of prophets came down from the high place at Gibeah with psaltery, timbrel, pipe, and harp, prophesying before him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Spirit came mightily upon Saul and he prophesied among them, and the people said, "Is Saul also among the prophets?" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The company of prophets was a band associated with a high place, marked by music and by ecstatic speech; Samuel's circle at Naioth was the same kind of company [^source-biblicaltraining-samuel]. The signs serve a purpose in the story: they confirm the secret anointing in public experience, and they carry Saul from the private word at the dawn parting to the public lot at Mizpah. The route of the signs is a geography in miniature: from the tomb of Rachel, to the oak of Tabor, to the high place at Gibeah — from the ancestor's grave, through the oak of the covenant, to Saul's own town.

### The lot at Mizpah

Samuel called all Israel to Mizpah, reminded them that they had rejected God as their king, and had the tribes present themselves by lot: tribe, clan, family, and man — Benjamin was taken, the Matrites, and Saul son of Kish [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Casting lots was a recognised means of discerning the Lord's choice in the Old Testament, used for the scapegoat, for the division of the land, and for the choice of a king [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul was not found — he had hidden himself among the baggage — and they ran and fetched him [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He stood head and shoulders above the people, and Samuel presented him: "See ye him whom the LORD hath chosen, that there is none like him among all the people?" The people shouted, "Long live the king" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Samuel told the people the manner of the kingdom and wrote it in a book and laid it up before the Lord [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The written manner of the kingdom is the only written king's law in the Bible, and it echoes the law of Deuteronomy, where the king is to write himself a copy of the law and read it all the days of his life, so that his heart is not lifted above his brethren [^source-biblicaltraining-mizpah]. The book was laid up before the Lord — a charter, not a chronicle — and it is never quoted again in the story.

### Jabesh and the renewal

The king was not yet king. Nahash the Ammonite encamped against Jabesh-gilead and offered terms of unusual cruelty: every man's right eye thrust out, as a reproach upon all Israel [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The elders asked seven days' respite to send messengers through Israel; a longer form of the story, preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls and in Josephus, says Nahash had already gouged out the right eyes of the Gadites and Reubenites across the Jordan, so that the seven days were a last hope [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead]. The messengers came to Gibeah and the people wept; the Spirit of God came mightily upon Saul, and he cut a yoke of oxen in pieces and sent them through Israel, summoning every man to follow Saul and Samuel on pain of the same treatment [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The summons recalls the Levite's dismembered concubine in Judges 19, sent through Israel to summon the tribes [^source-biblicaltraining-saul]. At Bezek they numbered three hundred thousand men of Israel and thirty thousand men of Judah — the first time the story separates the two counts [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Saul divided the army into three companies, attacked in the morning watch, and scattered the Ammonites so that no two of them were left together [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. When the people demanded the death of the men who had despised Saul, he refused: "there shall not a man be put to death this day; for to-day the LORD hath wrought deliverance in Israel" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then Samuel led them to Gilgal to renew the kingdom, and Saul was made king before the Lord, and they sacrificed peace-offerings and rejoiced greatly [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Jabesh-gilead itself is a place whose exact site is debated: the name survives in Wadi Yabis, and Tell Abu Kharaz is the site most widely favoured, but no inscription confirms it [^source-biblicaltraining-jabesh-gilead].

### The farewell at Gilgal

At Gilgal, the same assembly that made Saul king heard Samuel's farewell [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel first asked the people to witness against him: he had walked before them from his childhood to this day, and they answered that he had taken nothing from any man's hand [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then he rehearsed their history — Jacob and the Exodus, the forgetting and the selling into enemies' hands, the crying out and the deliverances by Jerubbaal, Bedan, Jephthah, and Samuel — and named the cause of the demand: "when ye saw that Nahash the king of the children of Ammon came against you, ye said unto me: Nay, but a king shall reign over us; when the LORD your God was your king" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The farewell therefore ties the demand to the Ammonite threat, a military cause the elders' own account in chapter 8 did not name. Then the condition: if they fear the Lord and serve Him, it shall be well with them and with their king; if not, His hand will be against them and their king [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. As a sign, Samuel called upon the Lord and thunder and rain came in the days of wheat harvest — a sign because wheat harvest is the dry season [^source-biblicaltraining-samuel]. The people were afraid and asked Samuel to pray for them, lest they die; he promised to pray and to teach them the good and right way [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The king is made, and the condition of his standing is stated before he has done a single deed as king.

## Why This Context Matters

Three different moments make Saul king, and the story refuses to choose between them: the oil poured in secret at dawn, the lot cast in public at Mizpah, and the victory won in the morning watch at Jabesh. That is the point. In Israel a king was not made by birth or by conquest alone, but by the Lord's choice shown three ways — by the prophet's word, by the lot, and by the Spirit that turned a farmer searching for donkeys into a commander who scattered an army. The context matters because it keeps the reader from reducing the appointment to any one of those moments: the private anointing without the lot would be a secret, the lot without the victory would be a name drawn from a bag, and the victory without the farewell would be a triumph without a condition. Samuel's farewell is the seal on the whole arc: the king stands, and the Lord's condition stands over him. Knowing how the pieces fit — the seer, the signs, the book laid up before the Lord, the oxen cut in pieces, the thunder at wheat harvest — is what lets the reader see the making of a king as the story tells it: not a coronation, but a choice confirmed three times and a charge laid down before a single deed is done.
