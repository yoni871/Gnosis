## Overview



David is old, and he cannot get warm. His servants bring Abishag, a young woman from Shunem, to care for him — and the old king's eldest surviving son decides the throne is his for the taking [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Adonijah, son of Haggith, gathers chariots, horsemen, and fifty men to run before him, wins over Joab the commander and Abiathar the priest, and holds a great sacrificial feast by the stone of Zoheleth, at the spring of En-rogel [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. He invites all the king's sons and the men of Judah — everyone except Nathan the prophet, Zadok the priest, Benaiah, and Solomon [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Nathan moves first. He warns Bathsheba, Solomon's mother, that her life and her son's are in danger, and tells her to remind David of his oath that Solomon would reign [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan][^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. She goes in to the dying king, Nathan follows to confirm her words, and David swears that Solomon shall sit on his throne that very day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. At David's order, Zadok and Nathan take Solomon down to the spring of Gihon on the king's own mule, anoint him with oil from the tabernacle, blow the trumpet, and cry, "God save king Solomon" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The shouting reaches En-rogel, and Adonijah's feast breaks up in fear. Adonijah flees to the horns of the altar, and Solomon spares him — on condition that he prove a worthy man [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Then David dies, after forty years on the throne, and Solomon sits in his place [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The new king finishes what his father left undone. Adonijah asks Bathsheba to request Abishag for his wife, and Solomon reads the request as a claim on the kingdom itself; Adonijah is executed the same day [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. Abiathar is sent to his fields at Anathoth, thrust out of the priesthood, fulfilling the word spoken long ago against the house of Eli at Shiloh [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Joab flees to the altar and refuses to come out, and is slain there for the innocent blood of Abner and Amasa [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. Shimei is confined to Jerusalem, crosses the Kidron after three years to fetch runaway servants, and is executed on his return [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. And the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon.

## Ancient World



### Two feasts, two anointings

The first chapter is a contest between two ceremonies, and the whole succession turns on which one counts. Adonijah stages a sacrifice. He slaughters sheep, oxen, and fat cattle by the stone of Zoheleth, beside the spring of En-rogel, and invites his brothers, the captains of the army, and the men of Judah [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. It is a royal feast in everything but the name: the food, the guests, and the place all say that a king is being made, and the men he leaves out — Nathan, Zadok, Benaiah, the mighty men, Solomon — say who is being passed over [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Solomon's making is different in every detail. It is ordered by the king himself, not staged by the claimant; it happens at Gihon, a spring outside the city, rather than at the feast; and it is performed by the priest and the prophet together, Zadok and Nathan, with the commander who stayed loyal and the royal guard in attendance [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The oil comes out of the tabernacle, the king's own mule carries the new king down to the water, the trumpet sounds, and the people shout until the earth seems to break with the noise [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The two gatherings hear each other. The rejoicing at Gihon reaches En-rogel while Adonijah's guests are still eating, and the feast dissolves in a single verse: the guests rise and scatter, each man going his own way [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The chapter does not argue about who should be king. It shows two public acts and lets the second one drown out the first.

### The first hereditary succession

Israel had never passed a throne from father to son before. Kingship in the ancient Near East was normally dynastic and hereditary in principle, though not necessarily through simple primogeniture — passing from the king to a son, preferably the first-born, or another male relative [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. Israel began differently: Saul was anointed privately by Samuel, chosen by lots in a public gathering, and confirmed by his victory over the Ammonites — three varied accounts of one appointment [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. David, the youngest son of Jesse, was anointed by Samuel and later by the people as king of Judah [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. Hereditary kingship began only after David's death, when Solomon, though not his first-born son, succeeded him [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. That is what makes Adonijah's claim so strong and so dangerous. He is the elder surviving son, and he can say, truly, that all Israel set their faces on him to reign [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The first-born held legal privileges, including a double share of the property, but primogeniture was often not enforced, as the narrative itself attests [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession]. Solomon's claim rests on something else entirely: the dying king's choice, carried by the prophet who delivered the promise of a lasting dynasty and by the priest who anoints with oil from the tabernacle [^source-biblicaltraining-nathan][^source-biblicaltraining-anointing]. The chapter is the first time in Israel's story that birth order and the king's choice collide — and the choice wins. From Solomon on, the house of David rules Judah father to son, while in the north dynastic succession is never fully established, and Israel is ruled by an alternating series of ruling families and independent kings [^source-bible-odyssey-royal-succession].

### The king's mother and the king's women

Bathsheba is the quiet centre of the first chapter and the hinge of the second. She was the wife of Uriah the Hittite, then a wife of David, and the mother of Solomon; near the end of David's reign she and Nathan secured the throne for Solomon against Adonijah's claim [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. The plan is Nathan's, but the voice is hers: she goes in to the king, reminds him of his oath, reports the feast, and warns that she and Solomon will be counted offenders when David dies [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. The oath she recalls is not recorded anywhere else in Samuel or Kings, though Chronicles reports that God had revealed to David that a son named Solomon would reign in peace and build the temple [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. After David's death she is seated at the new king's right hand. Solomon rises to meet her, bows to her, and has a throne brought for the king's mother [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. It is the first picture in Israel of the queen mother, an office of real standing in Judah, later called *gĕbîrâ*, "great lady" [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. But the honour carries a risk, and the second chapter shows it. Adonijah asks her to request Abishag, the young woman who had cared for David, as his wife [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. She carries the request, and Solomon hears in it a claim on the throne itself: taking a former king's woman could signal a claim to his authority, as Absalom's public act with David's concubines had shown [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. Whether Bathsheba passed the request on innocently or foresaw how her son would respond, the text does not say [^source-biblicaltraining-bathsheba]. What it shows is the cost of her position: the mother who secured the throne is the channel through which the rival makes his last attempt on it, and the son who honoured her with a throne answers the request with an oath and an execution.

### The altar and the blood

Two men flee to the same altar, and the altar saves one and not the other. Adonijah catches hold of its horns, and Solomon spares him on condition that he prove a worthy man [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. Joab catches hold of its horns, refuses to come out, and is slain where he stands [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. The difference is the law. The law of Moses denied the protection of the altar to anyone who killed another person deliberately, and Joab had killed Abner and Amasa in peacetime, shedding their blood in peacetime as if in battle [^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. Solomon's order states the reason: the execution would remove from the king and his father's house the guilt of the innocent blood Joab had shed [^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. The verdict draws on the principle that shed blood defiles the land until it is answered by the blood of the killer [^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. The tent Joab fled to was the one David had pitched in Jerusalem for the ark; the older tabernacle stood at Gibeon at the time [^source-biblicaltraining-joab]. The altar in these chapters is therefore the place where the ark's tent stood — and the protection it offered depended on the hands that caught hold of it. Adonijah's hands were empty of blood; Joab's were not. The chapter's other blood-debt is settled the same way. Shimei, who had cursed David on his flight to Mahanaim, is confined to Jerusalem and told that crossing the Kidron means death [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. He keeps the bargain for three years, then breaks it for two runaway servants, and Solomon holds him to the oath he swore [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The throne is secured not by the anointing alone but by the settling of these debts, one by one, in the open.

### The priesthood changes hands

The last of the four judgements reaches further back than the others. Abiathar had carried the ark before David in his fugitive years and shared in all his affliction, and Solomon spares his life for that service [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. But he thrusts him out of the priesthood all the same, and the text says why: "that he might fulfil the word of the LORD, which he spake concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh" [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text][^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Shiloh was the town in the hill country of Ephraim where Israel set up the tabernacle after the conquest, and for the whole period of the judges it was the nation's main sanctuary [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. There Eli's sons treated the offerings with contempt, and an unnamed man of God announced that Eli's family would lose its priestly standing [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. When Solomon removed Abiathar, the writer of Kings saw this as the fulfilment of that word [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. The ark never returned to Shiloh; it was kept at Kiriath-jearim for many years until David brought it to Jerusalem [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Abiathar is the last priest of Eli's line to serve at the centre of Israel's worship, and his exile is the last act of the transfer the fall of Shiloh began: the sanctuary moves from Ephraim to Jerusalem, and the priesthood passes from Eli's house to Zadok's [^source-biblicaltraining-shiloh]. Zadok takes Abiathar's room on the same day Benaiah takes Joab's [^source-mechon-mamre-1-kings-masoretic-text]. The old order — the priest who backed the wrong son, the commander who backed the wrong son — is replaced in a single verse, and the chapter can close with its verdict: the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon.

## Why This Context Matters



These two chapters are about how a throne changes hands without tearing the kingdom apart — and how close it comes to doing so. Knowing the world of the succession keeps the reader from treating Solomon's rise as a smooth inheritance. The elder son had the feast, the army's commander, and the priest; the younger son had a dying king's oath, a prophet's plan, and an anointing at a spring. The throne was won by a ceremony that drowned out a rival feast, and then secured by four judgements, each with its own stated reason: a request read as a claim, a priesthood ended to fulfil an old word, innocent blood answered at the altar, and an oath broken at the Kidron. The chapter also sets the terms for everything that follows in the book. The first words of the new reign are a condition — if the king's children take heed, the throne will stand — and the promise behind the throne had no condition at all. That tension, stated here and never resolved, is the question the rest of Solomon's story will answer.
