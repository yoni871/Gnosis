## Overview

Genesis 12–25 is the hinge of Genesis. The primeval history ends with the nations scattered at Babel and the line of Shem narrowing, generation by generation, to Terah's family; the patriarchal history opens with a single man's call. "Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee" (Gen 12:1). From that call to Abraham's death and burial in the cave of Machpelah (Gen 25:7–11), the category follows one household through wandering, covenant, judgment, testing, and leave-taking. Everything turns on three interlocking promises — land, descendants, and blessing to the nations — and on the covenant that formalises them in two distinct ceremonies, the covenant of the pieces (Gen 15) and the covenant of circumcision (Gen 17). The representative passage is the call itself, Gen 12:1–9.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis]

The story moves in four movements. First, call and wandering (Gen 12–14): Abram leaves Haran at seventy-five, sojourns in Egypt in famine, separates peacefully from Lot, and rescues him in the war of the kings. Second, covenant (Gen 15–17): God promises a son and enacts the covenant of the pieces; between the two covenant scenes, Sarai's gift of Hagar produces Ishmael; then God renames Abram and Sarai and institutes circumcision. Third, judgment and the promised son (Gen 18–21): three visitors at Mamre announce Isaac, Abraham intercedes for Sodom, the cities are destroyed, the wife-sister episode recurs at Gerar, Isaac is born, and Hagar and Ishmael are sent away. Fourth, testing and legacy (Gen 22–25): the binding of Isaac, Sarah's death and the purchase of Machpelah, a wife for Isaac, and Abraham's death at a hundred and seventy-five, buried by Isaac and Ishmael together.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-skinner-genesis]

The category carries no independently established date. Its chronology is narrative only: the text gives internal ages — seventy-five at the departure, eighty-six at Ishmael's birth, a hundred at Isaac's, a hundred and twenty-seven for Sarah at her death, a hundred and seventy-five for Abraham at his — but no external attestation exists for Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, or any event in Genesis 12–25, and no absolute date is established. The text also gives no reason for God's choice of Abram: the call arrives without preamble, and the narrative records the fact without explaining it.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-skinner-genesis]

Two features set the category apart. Its women act decisively — Sarah initiates and demands, Hagar names God and receives a promise in her own right, Rebekah consents in her own voice — and its silences are part of the story: Sarah's absence from the binding, Isaac's single question on the way to Moriah, the unlocated mountain. The prose below records those silences as textual facts and the readings of them as readings, each labelled with its source.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-skinner-genesis]

## Ancient World

### The Call and the Promise (Gen 12–13)

The call is a fourfold promise — great nation, blessing, great name, being a blessing: "in thee shall all the families of the earth be blessed" (Gen 12:3). The formula uses the niphal *nibrekhu*, "be blessed" (Gen 12:3), against the hithpael *hitbarekhu*, "bless themselves" (Gen 22:18); Keil and Delitzsch read passive against reflexive, a debated distinction, and the Septuagint renders it passively in both places.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis]

Abram departs from Haran at seventy-five; Terah dies at two hundred and five there (Gen 11:32) — a tension Augustine resolves by recapitulation, the call preceding Haran (Acts 7:2–3); recorded, not resolved. "Ur of the Chaldees" is no settled identification — southern Tell el-Muqayyar or a northern Ur — and the Chaldeans are unattested before the 800s BCE, so the name is anachronistic either way.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-skinner-genesis]

Altars stand at Shechem and Bethel/Ai (Gen 12:6–8); "Bethel" is proleptic, still Luz (Gen 28:19, K&D's cross-reference). Famine drives Abram to Egypt, where he presents Sarai as his sister; Pharaoh takes her, and Abram is expelled with wealth. Lot chooses the Jordan plain; Abram initiates a peaceful separation, and the land promise is renewed (Gen 13:8–9, 14–17).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis]

### The War of the Kings and Melchizedek (Gen 14)

Genesis 14 names four eastern kings — Amraphel of Shinar, Arioch of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer of Elam, Tidal of Goiim — against five kings of the plain, campaigning through the Transjordan and the Negeb to Hobah, north of Damascus. Abram pursues and recovers Lot. The Septuagint's "the emigrant" (Gen 14:13) renders the Hebrew *hāʿibrî*, "the Hebrew". Skinner judges the royal names historical and "some such expedition" possibly historical, but the details unattested: "a Bedouin razzia magnified into a systematic campaign".[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis]

Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of El Elyon, brings bread and wine, blesses Abram, and receives a tithe (Gen 14:18–20). *El Elyon*, "God Most High", first occurs in Genesis here (Gen 14:18–22). Josephus calls him the "righteous King", Salem is Jerusalem.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-josephus-antiquities]

### The Covenant of the Pieces (Gen 15)

Genesis 15 divides into the promise of a son (vv. 1–6) and of the land (vv. 7–21); the covenant strictly embraces the land (Skinner). "He believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness" (Gen 15:6). The ritual: a heifer, a she-goat, and a ram divided, a smoking furnace and a burning torch passing between the pieces (Gen 15:17), and the land grant to the Euphrates (Gen 15:18).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis]

The cutting follows a known custom: Keil and Delitzsch cite Jer 34:18, their parallel, not read in full, and note God followed "the custom of the Chaldeans". Jubilees 14, read in full, retells it as a dream vision dated to the fifteenth of Sivan. Josephus reads the "four hundred years" (Gen 15:13) as four hundred years in Egypt; its exact referent is disputed.[^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### Hagar and Ishmael (Gen 16; 21:8–21)

In Gen 16, Sarai gives Hagar to Abram; Hagar conceives, despises her mistress, is afflicted, and flees. The Angel of the LORD meets her at the spring on the way to Shur, sends her back with a promise, and names the child Ishmael, "God hears" (Gen 16:11). Hagar is the first person in Genesis to name God — El-roi, "Thou God seest me" (Gen 16:13) — and the first woman to receive a theophany and a promise in her own right.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis]

In Gen 21, Sarah sees Ishmael "mocking" and demands his expulsion; God confirms it, promises Ishmael a great nation, and opens Hagar's eyes to a well; Ishmael grows up in Paran. Yet Ishmael was born when Abram was eighty-six and Isaac when Abraham was a hundred, while Gen 21:14–16 stages Ishmael as a small child — a textual tension, unresolved, which Skinner attributes to J and E variants. Against it stands the text's own counterweight: the theophanies, El-roi, and the promise.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis]

### The Covenant of Circumcision (Gen 17)

God appears as El Shadday and institutes circumcision as the covenant's sign: on the eighth day, for every male of the household including slaves, "cut off" as the penalty (Gen 17:9–14, 23–27). *El Shadday* is of dubious etymology: *shadad*, "to be strong" (Keil and Delitzsch), or *she* + *day*, "self-sufficient", or a conjectured *sheday* (BDB). Abraham's laughter (Gen 17:17) is joyous amazement, not unbelief (Keil and Delitzsch).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-bdb-lexicon]

One covenant or two scenes is a disagreement, not settled here. Skinner: two distinct scenes — Gen 15's covenant strictly embraces the land; Gen 17 is P's covenant-sign ordinance. Keil and Delitzsch: one covenant in two moments — Gen 15 establishes it by faith, Gen 17 maintains it by obedience (Gen 17:1); *karat berit*, "cut", against *natan berit*, "give" into operation. The covenant is established with Isaac alone (Gen 17:19, 21); Ishmael is separately blessed, twelve princes (Gen 17:20). On *zeraʿ*, "seed", BDB records the collective and the singular senses without deciding; Paul reads the singular as Christ (Gal 3:16).[^source-skinner-genesis][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

### Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 18–19)

Three visitors come to Mamre; the promise of a son is repeated, and Sarah laughs. Abraham intercedes from fifty righteous down to ten (Gen 18:23–32).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis]

Two readings stand side by side, neither privileged: hospitality (Bible Odyssey) — welcome against demand — and justice (Keil and Delitzsch, Augustine) — the intercession as theodicy. What the text states is this and nothing more: the cry is great and the sin very grievous (Gen 18:20), and the men demand the visitors (Gen 19:4–5).[^source-bible-odyssey-sodom-gomorrah][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-bible-gateway-kjv]

Fire and brimstone destroy the cities; Lot's wife becomes a pillar of salt (Gen 19:1–29), which Josephus reports still visible. Lot's daughters bear Moab and Ben-ammi, believing mankind destroyed; the text gives no moral judgment on their act (Gen 19:30–38). Skinner reads it as an etiology of contempt for Moab and Ammon; Gunkel, reported via Skinner and second-hand, theorised a Moabite legend — neither the text's own judgment.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-skinner-genesis]

### Gerar, Isaac's Birth, and Beersheba (Gen 20–21)

At Gerar, Abimelech takes Sarah; warned in a dream, he protests his innocence, and Abraham calls her his sister (Gen 20:12). Abraham is called a prophet who will pray for Abimelech (Gen 20:7), the first use of "prophet" for Abraham, and Abimelech restores Sarah with gifts. Skinner reads Gen 20 as a variant of the chapter 12 tradition from a more advanced ethical standpoint (E). The Bible Odyssey article argues the sister was perceived as sexually available — no reason to kill the brother — and the patriarch is rebuked in all three episodes, endangering household and community. Isaac is then born (Gen 21:1–7); Abraham covenants with Abimelech at Beersheba, calling on "the everlasting God", El Olam (Gen 21:33).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-bible-odyssey-sarah-abraham-pharaoh]

### The Binding of Isaac (Gen 22)

God "did tempt Abraham" — tested him: "take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest" (Gen 22:1–2). The Septuagint calls the destination "the high land", with no "Moriah" as a proper name; Isaac carries the wood and hears, "God will provide himself a lamb" (Gen 22:8). Abraham binds his son — *ʿāqad*, occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible, hence "the Akedah" — the angel intervenes, a ram is substituted, and the promise is confirmed by oath (Gen 22:15–18).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-bdb-lexicon][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis]

The text gives no age for Isaac (Josephus: twenty-five; later tradition: thirty-seven). Sarah is absent; nothing says she knew. Abraham does not tell Isaac beforehand; his single question (Gen 22:7) is the only dialogue on the way. No reason is given for the test. The mountain cannot be located: Skinner judges all attempts futile.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis]

The readings stand side by side, none the text's meaning. Augustine: probation — "Now I know" as "Now I have made to be known" — wood and horns prefiguring cross and thorns. Keil and Delitzsch: purifying love. Philo: "the most important action of all", defended — others sacrifice from custom, fear, or glory; Abraham acted voluntarily, from love of God alone. Chrysostom (second-hand): the father's agony. Gunkel (via Skinner, second-hand): a cult legend. Jubilees (second-hand): Mastema proposes the test.[^source-augustine-city-of-god][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis][^source-philo-de-abrahamo][^source-chrysostom-homilies-genesis][^source-skinner-genesis][^source-charles-jubilees][^source-fitzmyer-sacrifice-isaac-qumran]

### Sarah's Death and Abraham's Legacy (Gen 23–25)

Sarah dies at a hundred and twenty-seven in Kirjath-arba, Hebron; Abraham buys the cave of Machpelah from Ephron the Hittite for four hundred shekels of silver — the first landed possession in Canaan (Gen 23). In Gen 24, Abraham's servant seeks a wife for Isaac: thigh oath, sign at the well, Rebekah's consent, "I will go" (Gen 24:58). Abraham then takes Keturah, whose six sons he sends eastward with gifts, giving all he has to Isaac (Gen 25:1–6).[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis]

Abraham dies at a hundred and seventy-five, and Isaac and Ishmael bury him in Machpelah with Sarah (Gen 25:7–11). Ishmael's twelve sons are named, and his settlements stretch "from Havilah unto Shur" (Gen 25:12–18). The Shem-to-Terah genealogy (Gen 11:10–26) is the entry bridge; the birth of Esau and Jacob (Gen 25:19–26) is the handover to the next category.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-nets-septuagint-genesis][^source-josephus-antiquities][^source-augustine-city-of-god]

## Why This Context Matters

The promise made to one man was universal from its first verse: "in thee shall all nations be blessed" (Gen 12:3), and Gal 3:8 quotes the same words. Babel's builders tried to make a name; God gives Abram a great name instead, and the blessing meant for one family is offered to all. The covenant's two moments hold together the shape of that faith: Gen 15 establishes it, when Abraham believed and it was counted to him for righteousness (Gen 15:6; Rom 4:3), and Gen 17 maintains it, when the sign is cut into the flesh and Abraham is told to walk before God and be perfect.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis]

The oath after the binding is the anchor of that hope: because God could swear by no greater, he swore by himself (Gen 22:16–18; Heb 6:13–18). The New Testament remembers Abraham as the father of faith — going out not knowing where he went, and accounting that God was able to raise Isaac up even from the dead (Heb 11:8–19) — and as the man justified by works when he offered Isaac on the altar (Jas 2:21–23). That reception is the later tradition's reading, not the Genesis text's own claim about itself. To read Genesis 12–25 is to see how one household's story became the pattern by which three faiths measure trust: a promise believed before it is seen, a covenant kept in the flesh, a blessing meant for all the families of the earth.[^source-bible-gateway-kjv][^source-keil-delitzsch-genesis]
