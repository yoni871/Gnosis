## Overview

Deuteronomy 1–3 is Moses' first speech, a historical review of the journey from Horeb to the plains of Moab, spoken to the generation about to enter the land.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The speech is set precisely in the text: "in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month," on this side Jordan, in the land of Moab, after Sihon king of Heshbon and Og king of Bashan had been slain.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] It is a retrospective, not a first telling — the events of Exodus 18 and Numbers 13–14, 20–21, and 32 rehearsed as the prologue to the law that follows in Deuteronomy 4–6.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

Deuteronomy 1 recalls the command at Horeb to leave the mountain and take the land from the Amorite hill country to Lebanon and the Euphrates, the appointment of judges over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens — charged to judge righteously, "for the judgment is God's" — and then the failure at Kadesh-barnea.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The spies went up to the valley of Eshcol and brought back fruit and a good report, but the people would not go up: they murmured in their tents, fearing a people "greater and taller than we," cities "great and walled up to heaven," and "the sons of the Anakims."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The LORD swore that none of that evil generation would see the good land, save Caleb son of Jephunneh; Joshua would enter it, and the little ones would possess it. The people's presumptuous attack after repenting was chased by the Amorites "as bees do" unto Hormah, and they wept before the LORD, who would not hearken. "So ye abode in Kadesh many days."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Deuteronomy 2 covers the long wandering — thirty-eight years from Kadesh-barnea to the brook Zered, "until all the generation of the men of war were wasted out" — and the three peoples spared: Edom, Moab, and Ammon, whose lands had already been given to Esau and to Lot's children.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] Antiquarian notices interrupt the itinerary: the Emim in Moab, the Horim in Seir, the Zamzummim in Ammon, the Avim displaced by the Caphtorim — earlier, taller peoples displaced in turn. Then the command to cross the Arnon against Sihon: the peace embassy from Kedemoth, Sihon's refusal and hardening, the battle at Jahaz, and the utter destruction of every city.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

Deuteronomy 3 turns to Bashan: Og came out at Edrei, and Israel took all his cities — "threescore cities, all the region of Argob," fenced with high walls, gates, and bars — and utterly destroyed them, as with Sihon.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The land from the Arnon to Mount Hermon was allotted to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh, on condition that their fighting men cross armed before their brethren. Moses charged Joshua from what his eyes had seen of the two kings, prayed to cross over and see the good land, was refused — "Let it suffice thee" — and was sent to the top of Pisgah to see with his eyes what he would not enter. It ends: "So we abode in the valley over against Bethpeor."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

No absolute year is given for the speeches; the text's setting is the fortieth year, and the category's date is unknown. The absolute date is disputed: the traditional Mosaic dating is c. 1406 BCE, while most modern scholars date Deuteronomy's composition to the 7th–5th centuries BCE. ## Ancient World

### The speech and its frame

The book presents itself as Moses' farewell discourses in a third-person frame: "These be the words which Moses spake unto all Israel" opens it, and "began Moses to declare this law" follows.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The first-person voice dominates — "I spake unto you," "we went," "I sent messengers" — but retrospective notices look back after settlement: "unto this day" of Jair's naming of Bashan-havoth-jair, and "as Israel did unto the land of his possession," a comparison presupposing Israel's possession as an accomplished fact.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] One commentary attributes such a remark to "Ezra, or some of the pious men who arranged and collected the books of Moses."[^source-jfb-commentary] A Bible Odyssey dictionary entry, read in full, describes the book as "a series of three discourses by Moses… with a concluding addendum (Deut 31–34)."[^source-bible-odyssey-deuteronomy]

The parenthetical second verse stands against the forty years of the third: "There are eleven days' journey from Horeb by the way of mount Seir unto Kadeshbarnea" — the journey that should have taken eleven days took a generation, because, one commentary reads, the delay was "banishment for their apostasy and frequent rebellions," not the length of the way.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

### The failure at Kadesh

The Kadesh narrative differs from Numbers, and the differences are recorded rather than harmonised away. In Numbers 13:1–2 the LORD commands Moses to send the spies; in Deuteronomy 1:22 the people propose it — "We will send men before us" — and Moses assents.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] One commentary judges the proposal "emanated from the people through unbelief," Moses assenting because he believed them sincere.[^source-jfb-commentary] The spies' report was good, but the people refused, and the oath followed: Caleb shall see the land, Joshua shall enter it, and the little ones "which ye said should be a prey" shall possess it — the very children whose fate the people feared become the inheritors.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

Moses' own exclusion is attributed here to the people's sake — "the LORD was angry with me for your sakes" — while Numbers 20:12 attributes it to Meribah. The two accounts stand in tension. One commentary reads Deuteronomy 1:37 as a parenthesis referring to the later event at the waters of strife; that resolution is recorded, not adopted as settled.[^source-jfb-commentary] The judges passage likewise omits Jethro, whose advice is the occasion in Exodus 18.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

### The peoples spared: Edom, Moab, and Ammon

Deuteronomy 2 is structured as a series of divine prohibitions, each grounded in a prior divine gift to others. Edom is "your brethren the children of Esau," given Mount Seir; Moab and Ammon are "the children of Lot," given Ar and their land. Israel may not take land God has already assigned to others, and must buy food and water from Edom for money.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The text frames the forty years both as punishment — "the hand of the LORD was against them" — and as provision — "thou hast lacked nothing."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The same forty years are recalled in Nehemiah 9:21 and Acts 13:18.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The sparings also frame the conquests that follow: Sihon and Og are Amorites, "one of the nations doomed to destruction," while Edom, Moab, and Ammon are not.[^source-jfb-commentary] Sihon had himself taken his land from Moab. The antiquarian parentheses encourage by precedent: as God displaced the giants before Moab, Edom, and Ammon, so he will displace the Canaanites before Israel.[^source-jfb-commentary]

### Sihon and Og: the Transjordan conquests

The two conquest narratives mirror each other. Against Sihon, messengers go from Kedemoth with words of peace, and Sihon refuses, his spirit hardened, and comes out to fight at Jahaz.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The embassy agrees with Numbers 21:21–22 and with Jephthah's rehearsal in Judges 11:19–22; Deuteronomy adds that Edom and Moab had sold Israel food and water, and the gloss of the hardening.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] From Aroer to Gilead, "there was not one city too strong for us."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

Against Og the pattern repeats at Edrei: "Fear him not," total defeat, the sixty walled cities of Argob, the same utter destruction.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The land ran from the Arnon to Hermon — Sirion to the Sidonians, Shenir to the Amorites.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The land was allotted to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh; Jair took Argob, calling it Bashan-havoth-jair "unto this day," and Machir received Gilead.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary] The eastern tribes were bound to cross armed before their brethren until the LORD gave them rest.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

The herem language of both episodes is stated without softening: "we took all his cities at that time, and utterly destroyed the men, and the women, and the little ones, of every city, we left none to remain: Only the cattle we took for a prey."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The destructions have no independent confirmation located in this research — no inscription, destruction layer, or battlefield evidence tied to Jahaz or Edrei. Rhetoric, historicity, and ethics are separate analyses, not decided here.

### Giants, cities, and Og's bedstead

Deuteronomy 2–3 preserves a memory of earlier, taller peoples — the Emim, "accounted giants, as the Anakims"; the Zamzummim; the Rephaim, of whom Og is the last remnant — the Anakim the spies reported.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] Whether the Rephaim are the *rpum* of Ugarit is debated: a Ugaritic text describes a divine figure dwelling in Ashtaroth and ruling in Edrei — the same pair of cities linked with Og — and scholars debate what the parallel means.[^source-biblicaltraining-og] The reading as naming "Og, king of Edrei" was not verified: mainstream translations read Rapiu, patron of the Rephaim, with no "Og" in the tablet as translated.[^source-biblicaltraining-og]

Ashtaroth and Edrei are usually located at Tell Ashtara and modern Der'a (Daraa) in southern Syria.[^source-biblicaltraining-og] The claim of Egyptian attestation for Ashtaroth — in Thutmose III's lists and an Amarna letter — was removed: the source read in full does not state it, and the specific letter still needs a direct read. No extrabiblical inscription naming Sihon was located.

Og's bedstead is the chapter's most discussed verse: an iron bedstead, nine cubits long and four broad, in Rabbath of the Ammonites.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] One commentary notes that at half a yard to the cubit the bedstead measures thirteen and a half feet, putting Og's stature "at about eleven or twelve feet" — or the bed may have been made oversized, as Alexander the Great did for his foot soldiers, to impress.[^source-jfb-commentary] How it came to be in Rabbath is unresolved, and some critics read "bedstead" as "coffin": Og, wounded, fled to Rabbath, died there, and was buried in it. Bed, oversized display bed, or coffin is not settled.[^source-jfb-commentary]

### Kadesh and Heshbon: what the ground says

Kadesh-barnea is proposed as Tell el-Qudeirat, the leading candidate, accepted by most scholars for the spring, the route position, and the Iron Age remains; Ein Qadis is an alternative, and no inscription names Kadesh at the site.[^source-bible-archaeology-tell-el-qudeirat] The excavated remains are Iron Age fortresses of the 10th–6th centuries BCE, with Hebrew ostraca attesting Judahite administration — and no Late Bronze Age remains have been found there, so no excavated layer is tied to the narrated encampment.[^source-bible-archaeology-tell-el-qudeirat] The absence "raises questions about the historical background of the wilderness traditions"; what follows from it is not established, and the finding is stated as a finding, not a verdict on the narrative.

Heshbon is Tell Hesban by tradition: the name survives at the ruin, Eusebius placed it twenty Roman miles from the Jordan, and it stands on the plateau between the Arnon and the Jabbok.[^source-biblicaltraining-heshbon] The later town is the secure part: a long sequence from the early Iron Age through Mamluk times.[^source-biblicaltraining-heshbon] But Sihon's Bronze Age Heshbon has not been found there: "No clear remains of a Late Bronze Age city… were found at Tell Hesban, and the earliest Iron Age settlement appears to have been modest."[^source-biblicaltraining-heshbon] The question remains open — Tell Jalul is proposed as an alternative — and archaeology alone cannot settle it. Map pins say traditional or proposed, never verified event location.

The wider regional geography — Arabah, Seir, Zered, Arnon, Jabbok, Gilead, Bashan and Argob, Hermon, Salt Sea, Chinnereth — is identifiable as the text describes it, except the Jabbok as Wadi Zarqa, not verified by a directly-read source.[^source-jfb-commentary][^source-bible-archaeology-tell-el-qudeirat][^source-biblicaltraining-heshbon] The route of the thirty-eight-year wandering cannot be reconstructed from Deuteronomy 1–3: the text gives no itinerary, only "we compassed mount Seir many days," and the reconstructed route south through the Arabah and back north is a reading, not an established fact.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

### The date of the speeches

The traditional position holds Moses as speaker and substantially author, the speeches delivered in the fortieth year; on that chronology the exodus falls c. 1446 BCE and the speeches c. 1406 BCE (Matthew Henry's titles give 1451 BC).[^source-matthew-henry-deuteronomy] The modern critical position dates the book's composition to the 7th–5th centuries BCE: the core associated with Josiah's reform of 622 BCE, the final form completed in the Persian period. ## Why This Context Matters

Deuteronomy 1–3 is the historical prologue to the whole book: before the law is given, Moses rehearses what the LORD has done — the failure at Kadesh, the long wandering, the sparing of kindred peoples, and the two Transjordan victories — so that the generation about to enter the land knows why it stands where it stands.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The review is the argument: the land is gift, not conquest by Israel's own strength, and the generation that refused it died in the wilderness while the little ones they feared for became its inheritors.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-jfb-commentary]

The passage also sets the terms for reading everything that follows. The eleven days against the forty years make the wilderness a judgment, not a journey; the sparing of Edom, Moab, and Ammon sets a limit on conquest before any conquest is narrated; and the allotment to the eastern tribes, with its armed-vanguard condition, binds the tribes that already hold land to fight for the rest.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub] The later books keep returning to these chapters: Nehemiah's confession summarises the victories over Sihon and Og, the Psalms turn them into praise, and Hebrews draws the lesson of the wilderness generation — "they could not enter in because of unbelief."[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]

What the passage leaves unresolved is left unresolved here too: the speeches' absolute year, the route of the wandering, the Bronze Age cities of Sihon and Og, and the meaning of the Ugaritic parallel are recorded as they stand, not settled.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub][^source-biblicaltraining-og] The reader meets the text as it stands — a farewell review, a charge to Joshua, and a view from Pisgah of a land Moses will see with his eyes and never enter.[^source-deuteronomy-kjv-biblehub]
