## Overview

Saul is still king, but the Lord has already chosen his successor. Samuel, told to stop mourning the rejected king, is sent to Bethlehem with a horn of oil and a heifer for sacrifice, to anoint one of Jesse's sons [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He fears Saul will kill him if the errand is known, and the elders of Bethlehem meet him trembling; he sanctifies Jesse and his sons for the sacrifice [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Seven sons pass before him and are refused — Eliab first, tall and kingly to look at, refused because the Lord looks on the heart — and the youngest, David, is out keeping the sheep and has to be fetched before the sacrifice can proceed [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Samuel anoints him in the midst of his brothers, and from that day the spirit of the Lord comes mightily upon David — and departs from Saul [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. At court, Saul is tormented by an evil spirit, and David is brought in to play the harp; Saul loves him, makes him his armour-bearer, and finds relief whenever David plays [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Then the Philistines gather in the Valley of Elah, and their champion Goliath of Gath challenges Israel to settle the war by single combat [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. For forty days, morning and evening, no one answers. David arrives with food for his brothers, hears the taunt against the armies of the living God, and volunteers, though his eldest brother rebukes him and Saul objects that he is only a youth [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He refuses Saul's armour, takes his staff, his sling, and five smooth stones from the brook, and answers the champion in the name of the Lord of hosts: the battle is the Lord's [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. One stone fells Goliath; David finishes him with his own sword, and the Philistines flee [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The victory makes David known to all Israel — and brings him into Saul's household for good, where Jonathan will make a covenant with him and Saul will begin to watch him with fear.

## Ancient World

### Anointing a king

Pouring oil on a person's head was an old and widespread act in the ancient Near East, used for grooming, for healing, and — in its solemn form — to mark a change of standing [^source-milgrom-anointing-encyclopaedia-judaica]. A slave woman freed, property transferred, a bride betrothed, a vassal deputed: each elevation was sealed with oil. Israel kept the Syrian and Anatolian practice of anointing the king, and in Israel the rite did something the other anointings did not. It conferred the spirit of the Lord — his support, his strength, his wisdom — on the anointed king [^source-milgrom-anointing-encyclopaedia-judaica]. That is why the story pairs the two events so tightly: Samuel pours the oil on David, and the spirit comes upon him from that day forward; the next verse says the spirit had departed from Saul [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The Hebrew word for the anointed one, *mashiach*, would in time become the title "Messiah," but in this story it is first of all a royal designation: the king is the Lord's anointed, and to lift a hand against him is to lift a hand against the Lord's own choice [^source-milgrom-anointing-encyclopaedia-judaica]. David's anointing is private — before his brothers only, at a sacrifice in Bethlehem — and it gives him no throne. Years of service, danger, and exile come first, and he never tries to take the kingdom from Saul by force [^source-biblicaltraining-david].

### The youngest son

Samuel's first instinct is to choose by sight. Eliab is tall and looks the part, and Samuel assumes he is the Lord's anointed — until the Lord refuses him: man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. Seven sons pass and are refused; the eighth, the youngest, is out with the sheep and is not even called until Samuel asks whether any son remains [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The count matters because the family record in Chronicles names seven sons and lists David seventh, while Samuel counts eight with David youngest. The difference is often explained by supposing one brother died young or without children, so the later genealogy omits him [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. Either way, the story's point is plain: the chosen king is the son nobody thought to summon, fetched from the sheepfold while the sacrifice waits.

### Goliath and the champion's challenge

Goliath of Gath stood about nine feet nine inches tall on the Hebrew text's measure — six cubits and a span — though the ancient Greek translation, a Hebrew manuscript from Qumran, and the historian Josephus all read four cubits and a span, about six feet nine inches [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. Scholars are divided over which figure is original, but either would have made him a towering man in an age when most men were much shorter. His equipment is described in unusual detail: a bronze helmet, a coat of scale armour weighing about a hundred and twenty-five pounds, bronze greaves, a javelin, and a spear with a shaft like a weaver's beam and an iron point weighing about fifteen pounds, with a shield-bearer walking before him [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. The mixture of bronze armour and an iron spearhead fits the early Iron Age, when iron was coming into wider use, and the whole picture is of a professional warrior equipped in the manner of the Aegean and Philistine elite [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. His challenge was not mere boasting. Deciding a conflict by a contest between champions is attested across the ancient Mediterranean world — in the Egyptian Story of Sinuhe, in Homer's Iliad, where Paris and Menelaus and Hector and Ajax fight to settle matters between armies — and the speeches of the two fighters before the blows follow that pattern [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. Goliath stakes servitude on the outcome: if his man wins, Israel serves the Philistines; if the Israelite wins, the Philistines serve Israel. For forty days, morning and evening, no Israelite answers.

### The shepherd's weapons

David's answer to Goliath is a shepherd's answer. He has killed a lion and a bear that attacked his father's flock, and he tells Saul the Lord who rescued him from them will rescue him from the Philistine [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He tries Saul's armour and sword, finds he cannot walk in them — he has not tested them — and puts them off [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. He goes out with his staff, his sling, and five smooth stones chosen from the brook [^source-mechon-mamre-1-samuel-masoretic-text]. The sling was no toy. Skilled slingers formed a recognized part of ancient armies, and the book of Judges describes seven hundred left-handed Benjamites who could sling a stone at a hair and not miss [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. A stone thrown by an expert could strike with great force from a distance, before an opponent armed for close combat could reach him. David's instrument at court was likewise a shepherd's tool turned to royal use: the *kinnor*, a hand-held lyre, whose playing brought Saul relief whenever the evil spirit came upon him [^source-biblicaltraining-david]. The story sets the two kinds of power against each other — the champion's bronze and iron against the shepherd's sling — and lets David state the difference himself: "Thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a javelin; but I come to thee in the name of the LORD of hosts."

### The valley and its cities

The armies face each other across the Valley of Elah, in the Shephelah, the low hills between the coastal plain and the hill country of Judah, southwest of Bethlehem [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. It was one of the natural routes from Philistine territory up toward the heart of Judah, and control of it mattered to both peoples. Gath, Goliath's city, is identified with Tell es-Safi on the edge of the Shephelah, where excavations have uncovered a large Philistine city of the Iron Age [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. In 2005 excavators there found a potsherd from about the tenth or ninth century BC inscribed with two names that resemble Goliath in form. The inscription does not name the biblical Goliath, but it shows that names of his non-Semitic type were in use at Gath in the general period of the account [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. Overlooking the valley stands Khirbet Qeiyafa, a fortified town of the early tenth century BC, proposed as Shaaraim, named in the Philistine retreat — though the identification remains under discussion [^source-biblicaltraining-goliath]. The valley was a contested border zone, and the story's geography is the geography of a frontier war: the Philistines pushing up from the coast, Israel holding the hills, and a single champion stepping into the space between the armies.

## Why This Context Matters

These two chapters turn on a contrast the ancient world makes visible. Anointing was how the ancient Near East marked a king, and the spirit that came with it was the sign that God, not the army, had chosen him — which is why the spirit's departure from Saul in the very next verse matters as much as its coming on David. And single combat was how ancient armies settled what whole battles could not, so Goliath's challenge is not a sideshow but the war itself in miniature, fought by champions while two armies watch. Knowing that the sling was a real weapon of war, that Goliath's bronze and iron marked him as a professional of the Philistine elite, and that the Valley of Elah was a contested frontier helps the reader see what the story is doing: the smallest figure on the field, a shepherd fetched from the sheep, answers the largest with nothing but a sling and the name of the Lord — and the battle, as David says, belongs to the Lord.
