## Quotation

> Now the Lord is that Spirit; and then, lest any one should think the Son to be signified, and to be called the Spirit on account of His incorporeal substance, has gone on to say, And where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty; and no one ever doubted the Spirit of the Lord to be the Holy Spirit.

- Edition: Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series I, Vol. 3, ed. Philip Schaff (1887)
- Translation: Arthur West Haddan
- Location: Book II, chapter 10, section 19
- Reproducibility: public-domain

## Explanation

Augustine is arguing that the name 'Lord' is not appropriated to the Son alone. He cites 2 Corinthians 3:17, 'Now the Lord is that Spirit', and notes that Paul immediately adds 'where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty', so the Spirit meant cannot be the Son but is the Holy Spirit, who is therefore also called Lord.

## Relationship to the doctrine

(C) Augustine reads 2 Corinthians 3:17 as showing that the Holy Spirit is called Lord. (A) The verse states that the Lord is the Spirit and that where the Spirit of the Lord is there is liberty; it does not name the Trinity or state the doctrine. (B) Identifying 'the Lord' here with the Holy Spirit rather than the Son is Augustine's reading, argued from the following clause. (D) The formulated doctrine is later and is not read back into the verse.

## Historical context

De Trinitate was written 399-422 (range; see works.json). Books I-IV defend the unity and equality of the Trinity from Scripture against those who argued, from texts about the sending and subordination of the Son and the Spirit, that the Son is less than the Father. This passage comes from Book II, chapter 10, where Augustine asks whether the Father, the Son, the Holy Spirit, or the Trinity itself appeared to the fathers.
