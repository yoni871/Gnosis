## Quotation

> But since three men appeared, and no one of them is said to be greater than the rest either in form, or age, or power, why should we not here understand, as visibly intimated by the visible creature, the equality of the Trinity, and one and the same substance in three persons?

- Edition: Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series I, Vol. 3, ed. Philip Schaff (1887)
- Translation: Arthur West Haddan
- Location: Book II, chapter 11, section 20
- Reproducibility: public-domain

## Explanation

Augustine is asking whether God the Trinity, or one person of the Trinity, appeared to the fathers. Of the Mamre visitation he notes that Scripture first says 'The Lord appeared to him' and then tells of three men whom Abraham addresses in the singular as one Lord; since no one of the three is said to be greater, he reads the scene as a visible intimation of the Trinity's equality and one substance.

## Relationship to the doctrine

(C) Augustine reads the three visitors at Mamre as visibly intimating the equality of the Trinity and one substance in three persons. (A) The Genesis text calls the visitors men and the LORD, names no persons of the Trinity, and does not state the doctrine. (B) The step from 'three men, none greater' to the Trinity is Augustine's inference. (D) The formulated doctrine is later and is not projected back onto the narrative.

## Historical context

De Trinitate was written 399-422 (range; see works.json). Books I-IV defend the unity and equality of the Trinity from Scripture against those who argued, from texts about the sending and subordination of the Son and the Spirit, that the Son is less than the Father. This passage comes from Book II, chapters 10-11, where Augustine examines the theophanies to the fathers to show that the Trinity works indivisibly in each appearance and that being sent or appearing does not imply inferiority.

## Notes

Augustine himself notes the uncertainty: in II.10.19 he says it 'does not appear plainly whether it was any person of the Trinity that appeared to Abraham, or God Himself the Trinity.' His conclusion in II.11.20 is framed as a question ('why should we not here understand...'), not as a certainty.
