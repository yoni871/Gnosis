## Quotation

> Who, then, can deny that the supreme Wisdom, when it conceives of itself by expressing itself, begets a likeness of itself consubstantial with it, namely, its Word?

- **Location:** Monologion, ch. 33
- **Language:** English
- **Translation:** Sidney Norton Deane
- **Edition:** English translation by Sidney Norton Deane (Chicago: Open Court, 1903), Logos Virtual Library
- **Reproducibility:** public-domain

## Explanation

In Monologion ch. 33 Anselm asks how the supreme Wisdom expresses itself. He argues by analogy with the human mind, which in conceiving itself has with itself its image, its word, born of itself: so the supreme Wisdom, conceiving of itself by expressing itself, begets a likeness of itself consubstantial with it, namely, its Word. The Word is therefore the self-expression of the supreme Wisdom, one in substance with it, and the same Word by which the Creator expresses all He creates. The 'Word' here is the Johannine Word of John 1:1-3, through whom all things were made (developed in chs. 29-31); Anselm does not cite John 1:1 by chapter and verse in ch. 33, and the passage link is recorded as partially verified for that reason.

## Relationship to doctrine

(C) Anselm reads the Word — the Word of John 1:1, through whom all things were made — as the supreme Wisdom's self-expression, begotten as a likeness consubstantial with the Wisdom itself. (A) John 1:1 states that in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; it does not describe the Word as the Wisdom's self-conception or use the term 'consubstantial'. (B) The analogy from the human mind conceiving itself to the supreme Wisdom begetting its Word is Anselm's reasoning, offered explicitly as rational argument rather than as scriptural authority. (D) 'Consubstantial' as applied to the Word is Nicene vocabulary (homoousios), later than John's Gospel, and must not be presented as John's wording.

## Historical context

Written at the abbey of Bec, Normandy, 1075-1076 (certainty: range; completed 1076). The Monologion is Anselm's first major work, an attempt to show by reason alone, without appealing to scriptural authority, what God is like; its account of the Word as the self-expression of the supreme Wisdom became the starting point for later scholastic Trinitarian thought, including Aquinas's treatment of the Word in Summa Theologiae I, q. 34.
