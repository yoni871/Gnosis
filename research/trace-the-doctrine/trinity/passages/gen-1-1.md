# Genesis 1:1-3 — Research Note

- **Reference:** Genesis 1:1-3 (canonical position 1, Protestant canon)
- **Doctrine:** trinity
- **Classification:** indirect
- **Justification:** The text states that the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters at creation. It does not name the Spirit as a distinct divine person, does not name Father or Son, and does not state that God is three persons in one essence, so it cannot be direct. It bears on the doctrine by implication: the Spirit's presence and activity at creation is consistent with, and points toward, the later claim that the Holy Spirit is divine and personally active.
- **Status:** verification-pending

## Relationship to the doctrine (A–D)

- **(A) What the text states:** In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth; the earth was without form and void; the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters; then God spoke light into being.
- **(B) Inference (labelled as inference):** Because the Spirit is presented as God's own Spirit acting in creation, a reader may infer that the passage is consistent with — and points toward — the later doctrine that the Holy Spirit is divine and personally active. That step is an inference, not a statement of the text.
- **(C) Historical reading:** Any trinitarian reading of the Spirit here belongs to later reception, to be supplied with attribution in Phase 3. No specific writer or quotation is recorded here.
- **(D) Later formulation:** The formulated doctrine of the Trinity, including the personhood and deity of the Spirit, was articulated in later centuries and must not be projected back onto this creation account.

## Interpretive questions and alternatives

- Does "the Spirit of God" here denote a divine person, God's power in action, or the wind/breath of God — and what in the wording decides between these?
- What does "hovering" convey about the Spirit's activity, and does the text connect that activity to creation or to something else?
- Alternative: the phrase may be rendered as a wind from God rather than the Spirit; how should that alternative be weighed?
- What would have to be true of the text for it to count as direct evidence for the Spirit's deity, and does it meet that bar?
