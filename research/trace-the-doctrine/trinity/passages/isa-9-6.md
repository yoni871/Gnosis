## Classification and justification

Classified `historically-interpreted`. The text does not state the doctrinal content; only later writers read it in a trinitarian light. This classification records reception history, not a fact about what the text says (RESEARCH-STANDARDS §1.3).

## Relationship to the doctrine (A–D)

(A) What the text states: a child is born and a son is given, the government rests on his shoulder, and his name shall be called 'Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.' (B) Inference, labelled as inference: the title 'Mighty God' is consistent with the deity of the promised child and supports identifying him with the Son, but the verse never names the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and never states that the child is a distinct divine person within one God. (C) Later readers identified the child with Jesus the Son; that messianic identification is reception, not the oracle's own statement, and Phase 3 will attribute specific readings. (D) The later trinitarian formulation must not be read back into a royal birth oracle. The same verse complicates any direct reading, since it also calls the child 'Everlasting Father,' a title that resists mapping the child onto the Son alone. The passage is therefore historically-interpreted.

No historical writer is named and no quotation is given here: attributed readings are Phase 3 work. The later trinitarian formulation (D) is dated generally as later and is never projected back onto the text.

## Interpretive questions and alternatives

- Does the title 'Mighty God' state the child's deity, or is it a throne name in a royal birth oracle that could describe a Davidic king in his own time?
- How is the title 'Everlasting Father' to be understood if the child is identified with the Son — does it resist, or merely complicate, a trinitarian reading?
- Alternative: the oracle may refer to a contemporary royal birth (for example a Hezekiah reading) rather than to a future messianic figure — what evidence decides between these readings?
- What exactly did later readers claim about this verse, and where — to be established with attributed quotations in Phase 3?

## Cross-references

- `ps-2-7` — Psalm 2:7 also speaks of a royal 'son,' another Old Testament sonship text later read messianically.
- `mic-5-2` — Micah 5:2 likewise promises a ruler whose origins are ancient; both oracles are later read of the same promised figure.
- `john-1-1` — John 1:1 states the Word's deity explicitly; contrast with Isaiah 9:6, which gives the child a divine title without stating the doctrine.

## Textual-variant and uncertainty flags

Uncertainty: 'Everlasting Father' in the same verse resists mapping the child onto the Son alone; this is a genuine complication for a direct trinitarian reading, not a detail to smooth over. Alternative reading: the titles describe a Davidic king in his own time. Phase 3 to attribute specific later readings; none are asserted here.
